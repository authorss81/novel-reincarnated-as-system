# The Boards She Waited On

She said his name once into the shed on the seventh and nothing came back, and she waited anyway.

That was the whole of what happened at the end of the month before this one, and she has not written it anywhere, and there is nowhere on nine days of road she would put it if there were.

On the eighth she was up before the light with the bar against the wall where it goes and the boards cold under her and the roof cord holding the way it has held every night for two years. The wind came in under the door at a foot a second. She put a new lump of clay in the ring at the low end of the boards and worked it down with the heel of her hand until the ring would sit flat, and then she sat on the boards with her back against the wall and her hands on her knees and waited for something.

## Nine Minutes

Nine minutes is a length she knows the way she knows the length of her own reach, because she has spent seven years finding out what happens in the room behind the one she is in.

He is in there. She has never once doubted that and she has never once been able to say how she knows it. There is a presence in there the way there is a room behind a room, and for seven years something in it moved when she spoke and put things into the ground under her feet and came back with a number attached.

For eighty-five days now — she worked that out at the sixth hour yesterday by taking day one thousand two hundred and fifty-six, when he put a thing down and did not pick it up again, away from day one thousand three hundred and forty — nothing in it has moved.

Not slowly. Not a little. Nothing.

She laid down on the boards at the seventh and counted nine minutes on the slow count she has used since she was fourteen and then she lay there and counted nine more, and the wind came in under the door and the light went across the boards and off them, and both counts came out where a count comes out.

Eighty-five days is two thousand and forty hours, and she has that figure because she did it twice, once on the seventh and once yesterday.

## What Is Left To Count

She has counted things since the eighth of a month seven years back and it is the only instrument she has ever owned, and she has never once found that it was a good one, and she has never once found that there was another.

Three coppers in the fold in the inside of a coat, stitched shut. Bread for a day in a piece of paper. Forty-one lines on a board outside a lifting floor, measured by the weight of what comes out of the ground or by the length of it, and she can take none of them and never could. Four fingers and a thumb on the right hand. The right arm open half the time and open more than that today. Eight of eleven holds, and she went through them this morning in order, on purpose, standing up, the way she does four times a month.

Breath at four counts. Four counts standing still, four counts walking, four counts with a bar against her shoulder, and four counts inside a loaded reach, which is the one that costs her.

A small open place low behind her fourth rib that came open on the eighteenth of a month two months back and has not closed since, and there is nothing above her to close it with.

And a piece of paper against her ribs, folded in four, softened at the folds, that says Foundation the first stage on the front of it in a hand that is not hers, that she put there herself with her own hands four years ago, and that she has never once opened.

That is the whole of what she has. She said it out loud on the boards at the sixth hour, in the ordinary voice, because she says everything out loud exactly once and this was the first time she had put the whole of it in one sentence.

It came to nine seconds.

## The Ring With Clay In It

Then she got up and did the day, because the day was there and there was nothing else to do with it, which has been the reason for most of what she has done for two years and is going to be the reason for most of what she does next.

The ring is clay in a low dish at the end of the boards and it is the only thing in the world that has ever asked anything of her. It asks for water and pressure and a day, and it gives back what she puts into it and not one thing more, and she has not once been disappointed by it, which is not a thing she can say about any other arrangement she has had.

She worked the lump flat and centred and she set the dish where the last one stood and she put four drops of water out of her own skin into it, and she pressed the pad of her thumb around the inside wall the way she has done four thousand times, and the clay took the water and went soft and took the shape.

It has not gone anywhere since she came to this ground. It came up a finger’s width four months back and stopped, and she has known for two months exactly why, and the reason has not changed and will not change while there is nobody standing in this room with a second pair of hands in it.

She does not light it. The furnace will not light itself. She has said that sentence to herself in this shed eleven times and she is not going to say it again today.

## Saying It To The Boards

What changed on the eighth was not in her body and nothing came up out of the ground.

She ran out of waiting at the second hour and she got up and did the day, and then at the fourth hour she sat down again to wait, and she noticed that she was doing it out of habit and not because there was anything on the other end of it.

Eighty-five days is a long time to sit and listen to a room. She had got to the place where the listening had started costing her something, and what it was costing her was the shape of the waiting — the way her attention went out into the space in front of her face and came back empty and went out again, over and over, for hours, like a hand going to a door that has not opened in a long time.

She said his name at the fourth hour, once, into a shed with boards on the floor, and nothing came back, and she waited nine minutes.

And then she said something else out loud, and this is the part she has not decided is right.

She said: “I know you are in there.”

And then she waited another nine minutes for an answer to that, and got nothing, and she told herself what there was to be told, which is that a man who has had nothing to spend for eighty-five days is not choosing not to answer. There is nothing to answer with. He is in there the way a tool is in a box when the work is done and the box has been put down.

She lay on the boards with her hands behind her head and worked out that she could do one of two things with the rest of her life and that she had been doing the first one without noticing.

The first one was waiting for a voice to come back and get up when it did.

The second one was being in the room with the voice in it, on her own, out loud, for as long as it took.

She got up and made the second one into a rule in four seconds, standing in the cold, and the rule was one sentence long, and she did not write it on the board because the board is for arithmetic.

## One Loaf Is Two Coppers

On the ninth she went down the lane and out on to the made road and did nothing at all until she was two miles past the mill, and then she turned round and came back.

On the tenth she counted what a day cost her, which is a thing she has done twice a month for two years, and this time she did it out loud on the boards because there was nobody within nine hundred yards of her to hear it.

Bread is two coppers at the counter five miles west of the mill, and it has been two coppers every time she has gone there, and she has not once tried to argue it and she is not going to start. Water is free because it comes out of her own skin, and if there is a trough it is eleven miles from where she sleeps and she has not been to it in eleven days. There is no wage. There has not been a wage in eight months.

Three coppers.

One loaf is two.

That leaves one, and the one does not carry a day, because a day is a loaf and a half if she is walking and a loaf if she is not, and she has been walking.

She wrote it on the board at the eleventh hour on the tenth, where nobody checks it, in her own hand, in three figures and a line under them, and then she looked at it for a while.

Three. Two. One.

And under that, because she is a person who writes the arithmetic down and not the feeling, she wrote one more line, and it was this: “one and a half a day, at two a loaf, is three, and I have three.”

Which is the same three, and it is the last of them, and tomorrow she has to spend one of them or walk a mile further than she meant to.

She put the point of the bar down and squared the blade against the stone and put it back against the wall.

There was a piece of paper against her ribs that was the only door in the world and she had three coppers and bread for a day and she was not going to open it that day because opening it was a thing to do and she had not worked out yet what it was for.

She lay down on the boards with her boots on, the way she had lain down eleven nights out of the last thirteen, and she let the door stand where it stands.
