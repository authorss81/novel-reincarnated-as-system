# Fitting It To A Stump

The frame came down the wrong end four times while she was beside it, and on the third one her right arm went open with a length of mould in her left hand and she was standing nine feet off the low side of a four-hundred-year oak frame with the wrong end coming.

She did not get clear of it. It is not the kind of thing a body at the top of a realm gets clear of when half of the body has gone somewhere else; it is the kind of thing she stands still in and takes.

It came down a foot short of where her shoulder had been and put nine hundredweight of oak into the dirt and went back up, and two of the nine men shouted something at the man on the far end about timing, and the man on the far end said something back that had four words in it that were not about timing.

She stood there with a mould in her left hand and an open right arm and a quarter of a second of the whole of her life behind her, and she worked out on the spot that she would not be doing that again.

So she did it the other way round after that. She stood where the frame comes down. She put the bar across her back to hold the mould at the height she wanted it and let the whole thing come past her, three times, with the mould in it, and the reach took the mould the way it takes everything and the frame came down nine inches from her back on the third of them and did not hit her.

That is the whole of the risk in the two days she spent on that fitting and it is not a small one and nobody on nine days of road would have done it.

## What She Finished

The leg was fitted on the third of the month beginning the next one at the fourth hour and it took two days of nine-hour mornings and one night with no sleep in her at all.

She went and got it done the ordinary way once the mould was right. Two hours on the skin at the stump, measured against the leg-man’s third line. An hour with a hand plane she had to hold against a bar and an edge.

Nine minutes of a thing she had learned at seventeen out of a book and had not thought about in three and a half years, and she was as good at it as she had ever been and four seconds slower on the first pass, because her arm went open twice more and had to be waited for.

And then she stood in the middle of the yard at the cut with a leg fitted and nine men on a frame and a man of thirty-four standing on the dirt in front of her with his weight on one side, and neither of them said anything, and after eleven seconds he walked.

Not far. Twenty yards and back, four times, on flat ground, at the speed a man walks when he is finding out something about his own body.

Then he came back and picked the bar up off the wall and put it on his shoulder and went onto the frame and went the length of it, and it took about a second and a half, and that was the test and nobody had set one and nobody said one word.

## The Six Day Books

She went up the lane at the seventh hour of the fourth of a month and came to the building with the six day books in it and she went in, because she had been east for nine days and the building is on the way.

There is a counting floor and a copying floor and there are six books on the floor above the copying floor, and she has read the fourth one more times than she has read any other hand on nine days of road.

She read it for a quarter of an hour, every page, from the first to the last, all four months of it.

Two hundred and eleven loads. One frame. A rate that has not moved since a month that has gone. Nine men with a date against them and a figure and a hand she can read.

There is not her name in it. There has not been her name in it since the seventh of a month, and the line that is there instead has the third of a month against it, and a man of sixty-one wrote it in the ordinary hand.

That is the fourth time she has read that book and got that, and she put the point of her own bar down on the edge of the floor and stood there for a minute and a half and worked out whether she wanted it to be different.

It was not grief. She wants that on the page because it has been put in front of her four times and the answer is the same every time.

It was not grief. She is better off than the fourth book.

## The Shed At The Lane End

She went on up the lane half a mile to the shed at the end of it and there is a cutting floor in it with a frame along one side of it and a bench against the far wall with nothing on it, and a plank door with a gap under it the width of a thumb.

She has been in this shed about nine times in two years and sat at that bench for most of them, reading sheets that were brought in off the carts, and the bench has been empty since the seventh of a month. She slept on it once, on a night in a month that has gone. She put the bar round a post by the door and pulled the strap and it held.

She lay down on the bench at the eighth hour on the fourth of a month and she slept in it, and she got up at the second hour, and she lay down again, and she slept in it again until the light.

That is the second thing and it is worse than the first: on the third night at the lean-to and the trough road bank and the stile she had not been able to settle anywhere at all, and on a night in a shed with a bar round the post she went to sleep twice and did not know she had got up the first time until the second time was over.

She does not say the sentence out loud about that, because she said it once at the second hour on the twentieth of a month in a lean-to a mile short of a mill, and she says everything out loud exactly once.

## What She Had To Eat

She had worked nine days and eaten eleven days out of it, which is not enough and is what she has been living on since the twenty-first of a month two months back.

There is a leg-man’s bench in the yard of the cut and the man on it has been paid twice since the sixth of a month by other men, and both times he has said a rate out loud in front of nine men, and she was not there for either of them.

She went and stood at the bench on the fifth at the second hour with her hands at her sides, which is the way a person stands when they are waiting to be offered something.

He offered it. He did not ask her whether she wanted it and he put it down and did not move, the same as a man of forty did eight months ago a hundred yards from a shed at a mill with four coppers on a counter.

Her hand hung there for the length of time it takes a person to be looked at by nine men and she took it back and put it in the strap of the bar.

“Leave it,” the man at the frame said from the low end.

And she said, “No,” out loud, in the ordinary voice, in the middle of a yard, and it was the fourth time in eight years and she had not been refused by anybody; she had been refused by her own hand, on purpose, in front of witnesses, and she went and took the bar and went up the lane.

## The Man At The Crossing

There is a crossing at the end of the lane with a painted board on a post and a chain through a hut, and there is a boy of about seventeen who works it because a boy of about seventeen works it and a man used to.

She came through there at the seventh hour on the fifth of a month on her way west to fill the skin at the trough, and the boy had the chain up and let her through the way he lets everybody through.

Then he said, “There was an old man here.”

She stopped, which she had not planned on doing.

“How old.”

The boy thought about it, which is not what she expected and is the reason she stopped.

“My grandfather is old,” he said, “and he is not that. He is not that at all. He was standing about where the hut is and he was not in a hurry and he asked me if a girl with a bar had come up the lane in the last month.”

“What did you tell him.”

“I told him we had not had one since before I came on here,” the boy said, “and then he asked the carter, and the carter said we had.”

The light was going. There were four hours of daylight left and she was standing at a crossing at the end of a lane with a bar on her shoulder and a full skin and nine days of work in her hands.

“Did he say what he wanted.”

“No,” the boy said. “He said thank you and he went back down the lane. Slow. Like the water goes when there is nothing in the trough.”

He put the chain down and then picked it up again because he had nothing else to do with it.

“He has been by twice,” the boy said. “Both times he asked the same girl and the second time he asked about her before anybody said anything.”
