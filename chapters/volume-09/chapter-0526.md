# A Table And A Page

She took the blanket off the shelf at the fourth hour of the twenty-fifth of a month and it came apart at one fold, because the thread in that fold had gone, and she did not put it back on the shelf in the state it came off in.

## Not The Book

Here is why, and it is forty seconds’ worth and she said it out loud once at the ninth hour of the twenty-fourth while she was looking at it.

A hand that cannot write a false line cannot be trusted with a leaf somebody else cut, and a hand that will not take a gift cannot be trusted with a book. Both of those are the same hand. It has been the same hand since a room with the shutters up in the second year of the realm and it has never once been two different hands.

The book on the table has sixteen clean leaves in it and no writing anywhere on any of them and it would have done the whole of what she needs.

She is not going to write one line on it.

## What There Was To Make It Out Of

The blanket was the only soft thing in the room. It was folded in a manner four hundred years old and it was wool, and it was the size of a folded coat, and it came apart in her hands over the whole of a morning.

The water was at the trough four hundred yards off the flat and she filled the jug twice a day and carried it in through the opening, and it costs her nothing to go through the opening, which is the only thing on this ground that is free.

The frame was four splints out of a bed frame with nothing on the frame. She got them out with the edge of the dish and a stone and they were the two hardest hours of the three days and there are four small holes in the bed frame now with the grain standing up in them and she cannot put them back and she did not expect to.

The mesh was two handfuls of the wool taken out of the middle and spun by hand against her thigh, because a mould has to let the water out of it and there is nothing in a nine-foot room with a mesh in it.

The weight was the flat end of a bar nine feet nine.

## The Making

Three days, and it is worth putting down what she actually did, because it took her nine years of other people’s work to find out that it is not difficult.

Tear it. Thumb-sized, with four fingers and a thumb and her teeth, which is an arrangement and not a style.

Wet it for two days. Wool that has not been wetted will not part, whatever anybody in any county says.

Beat it. Out on the flat, against a stone, with the flat end of the bar, two hours at the fourth hour and two hours at the ninth. It goes to a pulp that is grey and cold and slips sideways under the end of the bar.

Lift it out of the water and press it with the bar and then with her own weight on the table, twice, and the water comes out of it and what is left is a wet cake the size of two hands.

Press the frame on it, mesh down, lift it, and shake it once — hard — and put it down wet on the table under a board.

Dry it slow. On the sill, on the shelf, on the boards of the floor, and one hour outside in the sun at the fourth hour, because a sheet that is dried fast has a grain in it that a hand can find with a fingertip.

## One Simple Sentence

Nobody in this room is going to read any of it.

She worked that out on the first afternoon, standing at the table with grey water to the second knuckle, and it is the first thing she has built in four months that is not a thing somebody else made first, and a person who has not had that in four months discovers it in a minute and then has to stand still for a while.

## What It Came Out Like

It came out the shape of a bag.

That is what a sheet of wet wool is and she did not find that out until the second afternoon and she was angry for an hour and a half, and then it stopped being a thing she was angry at and became a fact of wool.

Three days. One hundred and four sheets on the first, ninety-seven on the second, a hundred on the third, and she counted them herself at the fourth hour on the third morning, in nine passes across the sill, one at a time with her left hand, because that is the method.

Three hundred and one. Of those three hundred and one, three hundred are no good for anything at all.

Some came out the size of a coin. Some came out the size of two hands. A few came out with a corner missing where she had taken the frame off too early.

## The Ones That Came Out Square

She set the standard on the second day and she did not move it afterward.

Square means all four corners within a finger’s width of a right angle, and no edge shorter than the width of her left hand, and no grain a fingertip can find.

One in a hundred.

On the third evening, at the ninth hour, she found three. Two of them were the size of a hand and one of them was bigger than that and square enough that she could lay the bar’s flat end on it and it sat flat.

She put the three on the shelf with their edges squared to each other, and she looked at them for a quarter of an hour, and she said nothing out loud to anybody, and there was a man nine feet off on the other side of a wall with a light on in his window who had no idea any of it had happened.

## Four Hours A Day And What It Cost

It took four hours a day and it is not work. She wants that said plainly.

Her right arm was open from the sixth hour on the first day and stayed open for the whole three, which is a rate and not an event, and she has four fingers and a thumb and she was beating wool with the flat end of a bar nine feet nine and swinging it rather than carrying it.

A swing is a loaded reach. Her fourth count went somewhere around the fortieth stroke on the first afternoon and did not come back for nine seconds, and it came back in four on flat ground that evening, and she measured both of those herself, at the ninth hour, sitting on the boards, and she wrote the two figures on the edge of a sheet that came out the size of a coin and then could not read it again.

She stopped swinging at a hundred and forty strokes each time after that and beat the rest out under the weight of the bar, standing on the end of it, which is slower and which she is going to be doing for another eight days.

## What One In A Hundred Comes To

She worked it out at the ninth hour of the third day with three square sheets on a shelf.

Three hundred and one in three days. One square in a hundred.

The blanket is two thirds gone. She has weighed what is left of it against the jug twice and she would put it at six more days of it at the rate she is going, and that is nine hundred and some sheets in all, and about nine of those nine hundred will come out square.

Nine leaves.

That is what she is going to have to leave somewhere, and it took her a while to see it that way, because she had been thinking of a page and what she has actually got is a number, and the number is nine, and nine is what the whole of the rest of it is going to be decided on.

## One Is Not Enough

And then she worked out the last of it on the twenty-seventh at the second hour, lying on the boards, and it took her eleven minutes and it is the whole of the twenty-seventh.

One square sheet, made on a table in nine feet by eleven, out of a blanket somebody else folded in a manner four hundred years old.

She has no way of knowing how long it lasts. She made it three days ago. She is nineteen years old and she has never in her life had to know how long anything lasts and she is not going to bet the reason for a wall on it.

She has spent four months being told what a piece of paper is. There is one against her ribs that a man with a brush read aloud to a room with the shutters up, and another that a man of nine hundred years put on a bench at a crossing in a hand nine hundred years old, and a third one in his own book with her name and the date on it that she gave away.

She is not going to leave any of it on something she made herself out of a blanket.

So there has to be a second square sheet, and a third, and enough of them to make a thing out of with more than one leaf in it, and there is one square sheet in the world and she made it and it is on the shelf in a nine-foot room against the west face of a wall.

## Nine Days Of Road

There is a post on a strip road nine miles west of the turn where the lane meets the store road, and it has a board a foot square on it that gets filled off a cart every nine or ten days, and a man stands at the rail with a paste pot and a rag.

She has been to it twice in two years.

That is the whole of what she has got, and it is nine days of road off, and on the twenty-eighth she is going to walk out of that room and down eleven miles of made road to the gate and west from there, on four fingers and a thumb, at nine days without bread, with a bar and a skin, to ask a man she has spoken to twice in her life for a sheet of paper.

Nobody on nine days of road is going to carry anything for her. She has known that for four months and it has not stopped her walking anywhere.

That is not the difficulty. The difficulty is that there is not one arrangement in the world in which a person gives her paper, and she is going to go anyway, because she has worked out that the thing she wants is to be asked for something once, and she has run out of everything else.
