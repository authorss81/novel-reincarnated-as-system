# The Stone Under The Ladder

She asked a man with a paste pot for a sheet of paper at the fifth hour of the first of a month on a strip road nine miles west of a turn, and he said no, and she left having got nothing.

## The Post In The Morning

There is a post with a board a foot square on it, a ladder that goes up to the board and not past it, a rail, and a trough at the foot with a dipper on a chain.

The keeper was fifty-five. He had a rag over his shoulder and a paste pot on the board and eleven lines of writing on it, which means it is between days.

There was a cart at the trough with its shafts up and a horse standing with its head down, and a man of thirty-five under the board with his back to them.

That is all of what is at a post nine miles west and it is twenty years in the spring coming and there is nothing else coming to it.

## What She Asked For

She said it in the plainest possible version and gave no reason for it, in the ordinary voice, standing at the rail, to the man and not to the man of thirty-five, who was getting nothing off the rail.

She asked.

That is the whole of the question and it is one word and it took her nine years to get to the point of being able to say one word to a man about something she wanted.

She did not say why. She did not say what it was for. She did not say how many and she did not say what she had of her own to give for it, because there is nothing of her own to give for it and because she had decided all four of those things in a cart on the second day and had not gone over them since.

## What He Said

He did not stop what he was doing for it.

“No,” he said.

That is the first one and it took under a second.

He went on down the board with the rag and then he stopped, and looked at her, and gave her the second one four seconds late and in the ordinary voice.

“I have not got one to sell and I have not got one to give,” he said. “Everything that comes on this post goes back down the road on the next cart and it goes with somebody who has paid for it.”

“All right,” Ning Yan said.

He looked at the rail for a while.

“You have been off a cart and you have been walking,” he said, “and you have not eaten since before you got on it.”

“Yes,” Ning Yan said.

“I am not asking you about that and I am not putting anything on the stone,” he said, and he turned back to the board.

That is all he said to her on the first of a month: nine sentences and not one of them a reason.

## The Shelf On The Wall

There is a shelf inside a little roof on the back of the post, and on the shelf, at the fifth hour, standing up where anybody on a cart could see it from forty feet, there was a bundle of forty sheets of paper with a board on top of it to keep the dust off.

She saw it when she came in and she had seen it on both of her other visits and she had not looked at it on either of them.

He does not sell paper. He does not give paper. He has forty sheets on a shelf within twenty feet of a girl with a bag who is going to stand in front of a table in a nine-foot room and put down nothing but what she made herself.

She looked at the shelf for nine seconds and she went on standing at the rail.

## The Eleven Lines

She read the board twice, standing up, the way she reads everything, and it took her a minute and a half both times.

Eleven lines on a foot square of board at a post nine miles west of a turn, and every one of them a thing going down a road, and there is no rate on it and no body on it and nothing on it about a person at all.

She took none of it. She stood there and looked at it for nine minutes afterwards.

That is everything a board has ever done for her on nine days of road: forty-one lines outside a room with six day books, and thirty-one lines here two years ago, and eleven now, and not one line in four years has had her name on it.

The man of thirty-five under the post asked her whether she was going the long way or the short way to the crossing, and told her the short way floods after a rain, and she said she did not know which of the two that was, and he said the flooded one, and that is the entire conversation the two of them had.

## The Three Days

She was at that post for three days and there is nothing to be done at a post.

That is the honest reason the twenty-two miles cost her what they cost her. She did not come for the paper in the end. She came because there was a bar on two posts and a wall with an opening in it and a nine-foot room in which nothing whatsoever needed doing, and there is not one other place on nine days of road where that is true.

She filled the skin at the trough twice a day. She read the board. She sat on the rail for two hours of the afternoon of the first of a month and she was not hungry, and that is finished, and she is thinner than she was on the eighth of one that has gone.

On the second she swept the ground around the post with the side of her boot for a quarter of an hour because there was nothing else, and the man with the rag watched her do it and did not stop her and did not thank her.

On the third, at the fourth hour, she got up off the rail and picked up the bar.

## The Three Names

Here is the part she worked out afterwards, and it is the reason for everything else here, and she got it on the second of a month at the seventh hour, sitting on the rail with a full skin.

There is a flat stone under the bottom of that ladder with a book on it, and there have been three names in that book in nineteen years and every one of them he put there himself.

She has stood at that ladder twice in two years and both times she had it in her hand to ask him for the stone. She did not ask. He told her on the first of her visits that no office has ever sent him a thing to do with it and no man has ever come up that road and asked him for it, and that whoever has this post next will not know to look.

And on the first of a month she asked him for a sheet of paper.

There is no version of that which is asking him for something else. There is no version of that which is a trade. A man who keeps three names under a ladder for nineteen years and has never been asked for one of them cannot be handed a request by anybody who has been to that ladder twice.

## What She Gave Him

Nothing.

She gave him her name and the direction and the fact that she had been there twice, and she took all of that away with her.

She did not give him the reason for the sheets. She did not give him the three hundred and one sheets and the nine that came out square. She did not give him a reason to put a fourth name on a flat stone, and she would have given him one if she could have found one that was true and small, and she stood at a rail for a quarter of an hour looking for one and did not find it.

A thing that is kept is not a thing that is owed.

That is everything she learned at that post and it is the reason she went back twenty-two miles having nothing in her hands, and she is going to keep the reason and the thing she asked for a long time, because the first is correct and the second is not.

## The Second Shame

She had a small one on the second and it is the least useful thing in it and the only thing in it she is sorry for.

She went twenty-two miles to ask a man she had spoken to twice in her life for a sheet of paper, and she made nine of them herself in three days with her own two hands, and the three days were better than the twenty-two miles.

That is not a fact of the world and it is not a fact of her either. It is the second time in five days she has found herself measuring something against how big it is instead of what it is for, and the first was counting the nights in a room she was going to be put out of, and she is not going to make a third.

## Leaving

She filled the skin at the trough at the seventh hour on the second of a month and she did not say thank you, because she does not say thank you to a man she cannot give anything to and does not intend to, and a woman with a paste pot is not owed one.

She put the bag on her back and the bar on her shoulder and went east at the fourth hour of the third.

Nine miles of strip road, two miles of lane, and eleven miles of made road to a gate, which is the same twenty-two the other way and it took her two days because there is nothing on it that will carry a thing for her.

## What She Is Going Back To

She said it out loud once, standing in the middle of the strip road at the fifth hour of the third, to nine miles of nothing in both directions, in the ordinary voice.

A book I made out of a blanket, a table I am being let have, and a man with a book of his own on the wrong side of a wall.

Then she walked the four hundred yards to the trough and drank again because her fourth count had gone at the ninth hour and she was not going to spend it twice.
