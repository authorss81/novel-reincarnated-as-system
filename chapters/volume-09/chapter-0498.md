# The Door Waits For The Latch

She had the bar under the door because she has put it there since the twenty-second of a month two months back, and it is not a repair and it takes nine seconds, and on the seventeenth of a month at the fourth hour she was sitting on the bench with her back against the wall four feet from the door doing the ring in a dish.

She heard him at two hundred yards.

That is not a thing she can do on a lane with wind on it. What she heard was not a sound, it was that the sound of the lane changed, in the way it had changed at the seventh mile kerb when a cart came up behind her two years ago, and she put the bar’s point down on the bench and did not move for four minutes.

Then there was a footstep on flat ground at eleven paces and it stopped, and there was nothing at all for the length of time it takes a person to decide something.

He did not knock.

What he did was shut the door.

She was four feet from it and she heard a plank door close against a frame from the outside, and then there was a pause, and then a small hard sound the height of a man’s thumb, and she knew what it was before it finished happening because it is the sound a latch makes when a man has closed a door and is waiting for the catch to go home before he does anything else at all.

He waited for the latch. He stood on the outside of a shut door with his hand still on it until the latch had gone all the way in.

## Four Inches Of Oak

She did not get up for nine seconds.

Then she got up and she did not take the bar out from under the door, and she did not open it, and she stood in the middle of the shed four feet off the door with her right arm open at the side and her breath at four counts and the ring in a dish at her feet.

A voice came through the wood, and it was not loud, and it was an old man’s voice and it was in the ordinary voice.

“Shuyin.”

That is the whole of it. One word, said once, at the outside of a shut door, to a person he had not proved was in there.

He did not say it again. He did not say good evening and he did not say one word of the lane and he did not ask whether anybody was in the shed, and she stood there two minutes waiting for the second thing, and it did not come.

Then she said, to the door, out loud, in the ordinary voice, once:

“There is an old man on this lane.”

“Yes,” he said.

“You asked a boy at the crossing about a girl with a bar.”

“I did.”

“And you have been up this lane twice in nine days.”

“Three times,” he said. “The first one was before the boy had been on the crossing.”

## What He Did For Two Hours

He did not come in and he did not go away and he did not try the door again.

She listened to him for two hours and she wrote down what she could hear, in her own head, in order, and it is nine things and it took until the light went.

He sat down. She heard the weight of a man going onto flat ground and then nothing for a while.

He stood up once, at the fortieth minute, and she heard him go four paces east and four paces back, which is what a man does who has been sitting on the ground for a while and whose weight is on one leg.

He did not speak again for an hour. She is certain of that and she is certain of it because she said his name out loud twice in that hour in the shed to see whether anything came back, once at the fifth hour and once at the sixth, and neither of them went anywhere, and he did not answer either of them, because he had not been asked anything.

And at the seventh hour, with the light going off the flat ground, he said, without raising his voice and without moving:

“Your bar is under the door.”

“Yes.”

“It is the light end that is under it.”

“Yes.”

“That is a woman who thought about it,” he said.

That is the whole of what he said in two hours, and it is the first thing anybody has said to her in two years that had a person in it, and she stood in the middle of a shed with a bar under the door and did not have anywhere to put it.

## The Door Came Open

She took the bar out at the seventh hour because there was nothing else to be done, and she had it in her left hand and her right arm open, and she opened the plank door herself with the flat of her hand because the hand does not have to close for that.

He was sitting on the ground against the outside of the shed with his back on the plank wall and his weight on the wrong leg for standing up, and he was not a young man and he was not an old man in the way that a person of ninety is old.

That is the thing she could not put anywhere afterwards. He was nine hundred years old or he was not, and there is no way to look at a face and know it, and what she could see was a man who had got up off the ground in one movement without a hand on it and had not made any noise doing it.

He looked at the bar in her hand and he did not look at her face.

“You did not come out,” he said.

“No.”

“You have been in there since the fourth hour.”

“Yes.”

“Why.”

That is the first question he asked her and it is the only one he asked in nine days of walking and two hours of standing on a lane.

She thought for long enough that he came to the end of what he had come for, which turned out to be nine seconds.

“Because you shut the door and waited for the latch,” Ning Yan said, “and I wanted to know what you did after that.”

He looked up at her then, for a second and a half, and it was the first time in two hours he had looked at her face.

“That is the correct reason,” he said.

## The Wrong Shed

She asked him for it at the eighth hour with the door open and the light going, sitting on the bench with her boots in the dirt and the bar across her knees, and he answered it in five sentences and did not make it longer.

“A boy at a crossing told me a girl with a bar had slept in this shed for nine days. I came up the lane because he told me.”

“You are at the top of a realm,” Ning Yan said.

“I know that.”

“Then a boy at a crossing is a strange place to start.”

“Yes,” he said. “It is a strange place to start, and it is the only place anybody on this road will say anything to a stranger about, and I have been on this road nine days and I have asked about four people and three of them were the wrong four people.”

He put his hand flat on the ground and got himself up with it and stood with his weight on one leg, which is how he stands.

“I came up this lane for a shed,” he said, “because a shed is the only thing anybody can tell me of, and I have got the wrong shed and I have been sitting against it for two hours, and the reason I have got the wrong shed is that a person at the top of a realm does not sleep in a shed at the end of a lane and I did not know that four days ago and I know it now.”

He looked at the door, and at the bench, and at the ring in its dish on the floor where she had left it.

“You sleep in it,” he said.

“No,” Ning Yan said. “I do not. I have stopped.”

## What He Wanted

He came to it then, and he came to it in one sentence and he did not build up to it and he did not put anything in front of it.

“I keep a book,” he said. “And a name goes in it when a person gets to the top of a realm with no document to take them past it, and you are at the top of a realm, and I have been on this road nine days for that.”

The light had gone most of the way off the lane.

“There is no document,” Ning Yan said.

“There is no document that anybody has read to you,” he said. “That is the same thing and it is not the same thing, and I am not going to spend tonight on which one it is, because you have had two hours to open a door and you have not, and that is your business and not mine.”

She sat on the bench in the dark with a bar across her knees and looked at an old man on flat ground four miles east of a mill with his weight on one leg.

She had got two things out of those two hours that she did not have at the seventh hour. The first was that he had shut a door on a shed he had no claim to and waited for the latch before he did anything else at all. The second was that he had not once asked her a question about herself.

He said the other thing after a while, and he said it the same way he says everything.

“You will want to know what I do with the book,” he said. “I write the name down, once, and I write it either kept or lost, and I will take either, and I have never once preferred one, and I have been doing that a long time.”

“And if I say no.”

“Then you say no,” he said, “and I go back down the lane, and I will be back, because I have never in my life not come back, and I have got nowhere else.”

He shut the door behind him and he waited for the latch, and then he went down the lane at the speed of a man with his weight on one leg, and he did not look back, and that is how she knew it was the whole of it.
