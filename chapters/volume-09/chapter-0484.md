# The Woman Who Never Asked

She went back to the store on the second of the month, on purpose, with nothing to buy, and the woman said good and went behind the shelves and left her standing there with a full skin and an empty basket and ninety seconds before she had to say something or look like a person who had come in on an errand.

She said her errand.

“What happens to a person who stops coming.”

The woman came back out with a sack on her shoulder and stopped.

“That is a bigger one than bread,” she said.

“Yes.”

“All right,” she said, and she put the sack down, and she went to the middle of the floor and picked up a weight that was the wrong shape for the thing she was doing and moved it two inches.

## Between The Weighings

She did not answer straight away. She did four or five other things first, in order, at the ordinary speed, and she answered the whole of it in the gaps.

There is one scale in that store and it is a two-pan balance on a post of oak, and she had gone to it about the time she had picked up the sack, and she put a weight in the far pan and a loaf in the near one and read the beam without coming back to it, which is a thing you can only do in a shop you have worked in for a long time.

The lamp in the daytime was still lit. It has been lit in the daytime for two years, every hour the shop is open, and Ning Yan asked about it once at the beginning and the woman said it was for the look of it, and had not been asked since.

“Everybody stops,” she said. “That is not a thing that happens to you particularly.”

“That is not what I asked.”

“I know what you asked,” the woman said. “I am telling you what it is like from here so that you know what you are asking.”

She moved the weight again.

“There was a man came in here every day for nine years,” she said. “He bought about what you buy and he stood about where you are standing and he said good to me the same way you say good to me.”

“What happened to him.”

“He stopped,” she said. “And I did not ask, because you do not ask.”

The woman lifted one end of the sack and put it down again without having changed anything about it.

“He stopped on an afternoon. There is nothing about an afternoon. He came in the morning and he came in the afternoon for nine years and then one afternoon he did not, and I have not seen him since, and that was five months back.”

## The Thing She Did Not Say

She put her hands flat on the counter and looked at the floor between them for a moment, and when she spoke it was in a different voice, or the same voice at a different weight, and Ning Yan had never heard the second one.

“I did not go and find out,” the woman said.

“Why not.”

“Because he never asked me not to,” she said. “Because in nine years he never told me one single thing about himself, and if I had gone up the road to his door and asked, he would have had to answer me, and I would have been the person who made him answer.”

She straightened up.

“That is the whole of what is in this for me. Nine years and one afternoon and I have not got one single thing about him and I did not go and get one.”

She went back behind the counter.

“He would have said something. They always say something.”

## What She Does With It

Ning Yan stood at the counter with her hands on the wood and did not say anything for a while, and the woman went on working, and there was no hurry in the room at all.

That was different. Two years in that store and this was the first time Ning Yan had ever stood in it while a person did two full minutes of work.

“He is not written down anywhere,” Ning Yan said.

“No.”

“Nothing at all.”

“There is a heap of bread in that back room that I will not sell and cannot give away,” the woman said, “because he bought the last of it on the last afternoon and did not pay for it and I did not ask him to and he has not been back to settle it.”

She stopped.

“There is no book for it,” she said. “Not a bad one. Not a rule that says you must. There is simply no book for a person who stops coming, and I have looked.”

## What She Wants

“So you cannot ask him either.”

“I cannot ask him because he has gone,” she said. “That is what people mean when they say a person has gone. It is not that they are somewhere else. It is that there is no door.”

She put a hand on the sack.

“There was a woman before him, and she stopped too. And one before that, and that one was four years ago and she moved east. I know she moved east because the carter brings her letters back when he cannot leave them.”

She looked up.

“A person can stop needing a shop and still need bread. That is the whole of what I know about it. About nine of them in two years, and every single one of them was still hungry, and not one of them came back.”

She put the beam right.

“I have thought about going up the road,” she said. “I did it four times in two years and I got as far as the gate on the first one. And then there is the thing that if I go up there then I have made him a thing that happened, and he has not got a thing that happened. He has got one afternoon.”

She wiped her hands on her apron, which she had not needed to wipe.

“That is the whole of it. There is a bread thing on a shelf with no name on it and there is one afternoon, and after that there is nothing, and I have been the only person in the world who knew either of them.”

That is what she had to say, and she said it in nine short sentences over four minutes with a sack on her shoulder, and she did not stop to be impressive at any point and she did not look at Ning Yan once while she was saying any of it.

Then she went behind the shelves.

## Not On The Board

Ning Yan walked out with a full skin and an empty basket at the ninth hour and she did not write any of it on the board.

She wanted to. That is the thing she had to hold on to while she walked the five miles back, and it came up on about the second mile and did not go away.

She could write a true line. Her hand cannot write a false one and that has been settled for a long time, but a true line is a true line, and there are nine people on nine days of road who have said a true thing to her in the last three months and she has got the only instrument in the world for it.

She did not write it.

Because a column is a request, and a request is a thing that goes up a road, and she has had four of them go up a road in eight years and every one of them cost her something that was not the writing.

So nine sentences about a counter and a sack and one afternoon went into the ground on flat ground four miles east of a mill and were not written down, and she has them, and there is nobody on earth she could give them to.

She worked out on the walk back why that was the right thing to do, and it was not humility and it was not the cold.

It was that she has four things of her own written down in the world right now and three of them were put there by other hands and one of them was put there because she asked for it in a room, and if she put a fifth true thing down on a board in a shed it would go up a road like everything else has ever gone up a road, and a fifth one about a shopkeeper who has been good to her for two years is a fifth thing that can be read by somebody she will never meet.

She has given enough things to roads. She is not giving that one away.

She also worked out, two miles further on, that there is no version of this in which she says any of it to him, and she put that away where she puts the other things about him.

## The People Who Have Not Left

And that was the night she worked out the other half of it, and it is the half that hurt.

There is a woman of fifty who ran a floor east of the turn and asked Ning Yan about carts every day for two months and said that there was nothing wrong with it. There is a boy on a stair who could read a third of a board. There is a man with a brush who has asked to see a slip three times. There is a man behind a counter who asks her about a rate the way he asks anybody.

Not one of them has left.

That was the grief without anything to put it on. A man who stops coming is a thing that happens and you can say the afternoon of it. These four are all still there, and all four of them have gone quiet towards her, and not one of them is anywhere else, and there is no afternoon at all.

She sat on the boards in the dark on the second of the month with the wind coming in under the door and put her hand flat on the piece of paper against her ribs and worked out that she could lose four more people without losing anybody, and that she would go on seeing them, and that they would go on not asking.

And she thought about a bench outside a store on a made road nine days of road east, and a woman who asked her about carts every day for two months and was gone inside a month of it, and about not being able to say that name out loud to a shed.

She said it out loud to the shed.

Then she lay down and did not sleep for a while, and at some point in the night she decided that on the seventh of the month she would go down to the rail and stand at it for eleven minutes and see whether anything at all comes down that lane in the whole of eleven minutes.

She knew the answer before she got there. She had known it on the seventeenth of a month at a rail with a full board and four people on it.

She went anyway.
