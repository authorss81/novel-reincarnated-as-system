# Kept Or Lost

He came back up the lane on the eighteenth at the seventh hour in the morning and she was four hundred yards west of it at the trough road with a skin on her shoulder, and she saw him come past at about nine hundred yards and he did not stop and she did not wave, and they met at the crossing at the eighth hour because that is where the ground is flat and you can see a long way.

She did not invite him into the shed and he did not ask. There is a hut at a crossing with a bench in it and they did not go in it either.

“You said you would be back,” she said.

“I said that yesterday and it is still true today,” he said.

## The Rule

She got it out of him in eleven minutes and she is not proud of the eleven minutes because she did it by not asking a single question, which is a thing she has never managed in eight years.

He had a book and a rule. That was all he had, and he said so in about four hundred words on flat ground at the eighth hour of the eighteenth of a month with a boy of about seventeen putting a chain up and down at the end of the lane.

A name goes in the book when a person gets to the top of a realm and there is no document to take them past it. That is the whole of the rule.

The rule is older than she is by a very long way. He did not say how much older and she did not ask, and the reason she did not ask is that she had worked out on the fourth of a month at the cut that a man who says older than you are has said the useful half and is going to spend the rest of the day on the half that is his own.

There are two places a name can go. Kept or lost.

Kept means the person is written down and is on the inside of the thing that is being kept, and the bound does not take a person who is on a list, because a list is the one thing in the world the bound goes around rather than through.

Lost means the bound took them and somebody wrote down that it did. That is all lost is. It is not a sentence about where they went.

“You write a name and the thing stops being able to take you,” Ning Yan said.

“No,” he said.

That was the first time he had said a flat no to her and he said it the same way he says everything else.

“It does not stop anything,” he said. “It is written down. I write it and the bound takes them anyway and the page says they were taken, and that is the entire effect of it.”

## What He Said Once

And then he said the thing that he had said to two people in nine hundred years or to none, and he said it once, and she has it exactly.

“Somebody has to write down the ones the bound takes,” he said, “or there is no record that the bound takes any. I write them. I have written them for nine hundred years. The writing does not save one of them and I have never once said it did.”

The boy at the crossing put the chain up and took it down again.

He did not put anything after it. He did not ask her whether she agreed and he did not wait for her to be impressed, and that was the whole of his argument and it is not wrong, and it took eleven seconds to say and about nine hundred years to arrive at.

## The Question She Asked

She had one question and she had had it ready since the seventh hour of the seventeenth and she had been carrying it down nine miles of lane, and she asked it in the ordinary voice.

“Why does a bounded world need readers.”

He did not answer.

That is the whole of what happened. There was no pause and there was no negotiation about it and he did not say that he was not going to answer; he said the thing she had said back to her in the ordinary voice and then he shut up.

“Fine,” she said.

“It is not that I cannot tell you,” he said. “I have a version and the version is nine hundred years old and it belongs to a person who is not standing here. When I say it, it will be a thing I have said to one person in a field, and it will be worth exactly that to whoever is standing in the field after me.”

She looked at him for a while.

“That is the most careful thing anybody has said to me in two years,” she said, “and you have said it while refusing to answer me.”

“Yes,” he said.

## What She Said Back

And then she said the thing that was actually in her, and she had it out before she had decided to, which is the only way she has ever said a thing that turned out badly.

“Nobody asked them.”

“Absolutely not,” he said.

“Not one of them. Not in nine hundred years. Not one person at the top of a realm has ever been asked what they wanted to be called.”

“No,” he said. “And I am not going to pretend that is a fault in the rule. It is a fact about the rule and it has been a fact about the rule for longer than you have been alive, and if I started arguing with it on a lane I would be doing it to a person who is nineteen and hungry, and I have been doing that for nine hundred years and I do not do it.”

“I am eighteen,” she said.

“That is worse,” he said, “and I am sorry.”

That is the whole of it and it took him nine days of walking to say and he said it in nine seconds and she has not been able to get it out from under anything else since.

## The Part She Did Not Say

There is a thing she worked out at the ninth hour standing on flat ground at the end of the lane with a boy at a crossing, and she did not say it out loud, and it is the whole of what she did over the next two days.

The book is a book. She has been inside eleven rooms with a book in them and a lamp on every table and a person qualified standing in front of her reading a page out loud in daylight, and every one of those was a room and a man and a line read back before it went in.

That is not what this is.

This is a book in a bag on a lane, and a man with his weight on one leg, and a rule that goes on whether anybody agrees with it or not. And nobody is going to ask her. And nobody is going to read it to her. And there is no office nine days of road that can be made to do it either, because it is not an office, and nobody in this world can be compelled to, and there is nobody to refuse.

He writes the name or he does not. That is all of it. That is the only hand in the whole of it and it is nine hundred years old and correct.

She stood on flat ground at the ninth hour and worked out that for eight years every single thing that has ever come for her came out of a document she could refuse to open, refuse to answer, or refuse to be read by, and that she was extremely good at all three, and that not one of them was going to be any use to her here.

Then she worked out the other thing, which took forty seconds and which she had not been able to see from the inside of it until that moment.

There are four pages in the world with her name on them.

A finding at seventeen. A second entry carried up out of its own office in a hand she has never seen. A reading in a room with a lamp in it, on the ninth of a month. And a refusal, in the best book on nine days of road, entered at the third hour of the third of a month by a man behind a counter in a room with the shutters up, and read back to her before it went in.

Three of those were done to her. One of them she went at herself.

And he has one book, and he has nothing about her in it, and he has been on a lane for nine days because he has nothing about her in it.

She did not say any of that out loud on flat ground at the end of a lane. She said, “I will think about it,” which is a thing a person says, and he said, “Take as long as you want, and I am not going to be able to be anywhere else,” and then he shut the door of the hut behind him and waited for the latch before he went down the lane.

And that is how she came to be carrying a thing home up thirteen miles of made road that she had not been carrying the day before, which is a page she chose, in a book she cannot reach, that nobody has ever read to her and that she cannot prove is hers to anybody except herself.

## The Rest Of The Day

She went back up the lane at the tenth hour and she had four hours of daylight left and she spent all of them on purpose, which is a thing she has not done since she was fourteen.

She worked the ring in a dish and it came up a finger’s width and stopped. She sat on the bench with her back against the wall. She walked down the lane to the crossing at the eleventh hour and stood at the rail and watched a boy put a chain up and down and did not ask him a question.

And at the second hour she worked out that she had spent four hours not doing one single thing on the ground, and that four hours earlier she had been walking down a made road with a full skin and no food in her, going to tell a shopkeeper that she could not take a loaf of bread, and that the four hours had been the best four hours she has had since the second month of this.

She had not said anything out loud in any of them.

She said it at the second hour, once, standing on flat ground, in the ordinary voice.

That is what she bought. Not the silence. The four hours in which a thing is going to come for her and she is not doing one thing about it and she is not telling one living soul, and it is four hours of not counting.

Then she picked the bar up off the ground and went east along the lane, and she has not had four hours like that since, and she has wanted them about four times since.
