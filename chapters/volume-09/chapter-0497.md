# The Man Who Carries Boards

She did not wait for him, and she wants that said first because the whole of what happened over the next nine days hangs off it.

She has waited for a voice for a hundred and twenty-nine days and she worked out on the sixth of a month what that cost and she is not doing it again. Waiting is a thing you do toward something and there was nothing at the end of it.

What she did instead was go and be thirteen miles east of a mill on the seventh of a month with a bar on her shoulder, and if an old man came up the lane he would find her doing what she does.

## Nine Coppers A Head

She got as far as the long road on the seventh and got two hundred yards down it and her right arm went open in the wind a mile and a half from the mill and stayed open for the length of forty yards, and she put her hand on a kerb and waited for it and did not like waiting for it any more than she liked waiting for anything else.

She walked four miles like that and then Wei Ran’s team came out of the west going east with an empty cart and eleven boards on it, and the cart stopped, because it always stops.

Wei Ran was on the front bench with her hand on the rail and she looked at the bar and then at the arm and then at the road ahead.

“Where,” she said.

That is how Wei Ran asks. It is one word and it means the whole of a negotiation and she has never in eight years asked Ning Yan a second question at the start of one.

“West to the store and back,” Ning Yan said.

“Nine coppers a head,” Wei Ran said. “And the bar goes in the cart and does not go in the rate, and if it goes in the cart it goes in the cart by itself and I am not responsible for it.”

“You have never put a bar in a cart.”

“No,” Wei Ran said. “I have carried a bar once and it went through the bottom and I got the blame for it in two places. Nine coppers a head.”

That was the rate and she said it out loud, and she said it out loud again a quarter of an hour later when she had the cart moving and Ning Yan was standing at the kerb watching a girl of twenty-one with a team and a trade, and the second time she said it she said it slower and she said it looking at her.

Nine coppers a head. Two hundred yards east of the mill.

And then she said the other thing, which she always says, and she said it the same way she has said it for eight months.

“You do not have nine coppers.”

“No.”

“No,” Wei Ran said. “You had three on the fifteenth and one on the twenty-third and I know that because the storekeeper tells me what you buy, and he does not do it to be unkind, he does it because a woman who buys one loaf every five days is a thing he thinks about.”

She put the cart in motion.

“It is on the book. Nine coppers. I have said it out loud twice and that is the whole of what I do about it, and I am not going to chase it, and you are not going to pay it.”

There are eight coppers on Wei Ran’s book from eight months back that she has never chased either, and Ning Yan’s hand will not take them, and it has never once been a comfort to her that a woman with a team has declined to chase, and she did not say so.

She got onto the cart with a bar and four fingers and a thumb, and she was carried four miles west at the speed of an empty cart, and she said nothing for the whole of it.

## Four Coppers Of Work

She got off at the store and filled the skin at the trough road and went in.

“Good,” the woman said, and she had a loaf on the counter before the sentence was finished, and Ning Yan put nothing on the wood.

“I have no money,” she said.

The woman put the loaf back on the shelf behind her and did not say anything about that, and that was the whole of it.

“I have had no money since the twenty-third of a month,” Ning Yan said, “and I have done four days of fitting since then, and the man whose leg it was cannot pay me and I cannot take it if he could.”

The woman came round with the sack.

“There is the thing at the back,” she said. “The bread that man did not pay for. It is four months old and it is not bread and I cannot give it away and I cannot sell it.”

“No,” Ning Yan said.

“You are not going to eat it.”

“I am not going to eat it,” Ning Yan said, “and you are not going to throw it at me, and both of those are correct, and I am sorry.”

She went out into the road with nothing and stood in the sun for the length of time it took her to work out what she had come five miles for, and she had come five miles for a sentence she had said out loud to a shopkeeper who has been good to her for two years.

That was all it was. She had come to say it to somebody who had not asked her a question, because there is no one else within eleven miles.

## The Carrier

She was on her way back east at the second hour of the eighth of a month with a full skin and no food and eleven hours of daylight, and a man came up the long road with a hand bar and four boards on it.

He was about fifty. He has been carrying boards across that lane and up to the end of the long road for nine years, and there are four on his bar and he carries four, and he carries them every week whether or not there is anything to carry.

She had been up the lane eleven days and had not seen him, which she worked out later was because he comes on the seventh and the seventeenth and she had been on the seventh.

He put the bar down in the road to shift it, and she was nine feet off and he looked at her.

“You are the one with the bar,” he said.

“No,” Ning Yan said. “I am the one leaning on it.”

He laughed at that, which she had not expected, and then he picked the bar up and went forty yards and set it down again and looked at her properly.

## The Five Sentences

He said five things, and she counted them at the fourth hour and has them exact, and he did not make a speech and did not stop to be impressive and did not look at her while he was saying any of them.

“I have been written down twice.”

He set the bar down.

“Both times at the end of the long road. A man with a book takes a name and a date and a place and that is the whole of the time it takes.”

He picked the bar up and shouldered it.

“Neither time did anybody read it to me. I did not know for about a month, and then a woman at the end of the road said it out loud in a doorway, because I had asked her what it was for.”

He went ten yards and stopped.

“That is what happens, on that road, to a person. It is not a thing anybody thinks is strange.”

He looked at her.

“It is only strange to the person it happens to.”

That is the whole of it and it took him about a minute and a half and he carried four boards the whole time and did not put them down again.

## What She Asked

She asked him one question and she got nine words out of it before she threw the rest away.

“Two,” she said. “How long between them.”

“About eleven years,” he said.

“Do you know what was written?”

“No,” he said. “And I have never wanted to know, because wanting to know is a thing you do at the end of the long road and not at this end of it, and I have never been that far with a question.”

She stood in the road and worked out that a man of fifty who has been written down twice does not ask what is written, and that he is not being careful, and that he has never once had to be careful about it, because a man who carries boards is not a thing anybody has a use for.

Then she said the thing she had come thirteen miles east to be in the same lane as.

“An old man has been asking at the crossing about a girl with a bar.”

The carrier did not laugh this time.

“Slow,” he said. “With his weight on one leg. He came past me on the seventh and he asked me straight out, and he said he was not in a hurry.”

“Why would a man say he is not in a hurry.”

He shifted the bar on his shoulder.

“Because he has been up this lane before,” he said, “and he has found it a road that goes on, and he is telling you that he knows so.”

He went on east with the four boards.

And Ning Yan stood in a road with a full skin and no food and worked out, in nine seconds, that a man of nine hundred years does not come up a lane twice in the same week unless the first time told him something, and that the only thing on that lane worth coming back for was a girl who had been sleeping in the shed at the end of it for nine days and was not in it on either day he walked the length of it.

## What She Decided

She went on east at the third hour and she got to the shed at the end of the lane at the eighth hour and she put the bar round the post and she did not lie down, which is the second night out of three that she has not lain down inside two hours of arriving somewhere.

She sat on the boards with her back against the wall and worked out what she was going to do about an old man who is not in a hurry, and it took eleven minutes and it came out in four lines on the wall with the point of the bar.

He will come up the lane. He will come to this shed because it is the only shed anybody on that lane can point at, and because she was in it for nine days and the ring and the boards and the trodden dirt of it are the whole of what anybody would say about her.

She is going to be in it.

She wrote that down and then she looked at it for a while, because she had written a line about where she was going to be on the wall of a shed she has slept in twice in two years, and she had done it on purpose, and there was not one other line on that wall that she had put there on purpose.

Then she wrote a fifth line underneath it.

He shuts doors and waits for the latch.

She did not know where that had come from. She had not worked it out. She said it out loud on the boards at the ninth hour, once, in the ordinary voice, standing up, and then she lay down on them with her clothes on and her bar across her knees, and she did not sleep for two hours, and she did not lie there waiting for anything after that.
