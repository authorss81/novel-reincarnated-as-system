# Eleven Minutes At The Rail

She got down to the lane at the fifth hour on the seventh of the month and stood at the rail, and she had decided on the second of the month, lying on the boards, that she would give it eleven minutes and then go.

She did not decide it as a vigil. She decided it the way she decides everything now, which is as an instrument: eleven minutes is a length long enough that if anything is going to come down that lane it will come down that lane, and short enough that a woman with no wages and no bread does not waste a morning.

The bar was against her leg. Her right arm was open at the side and she put it in the strap of the bar so it would not swing and hit her, which is a thing she does on a good day and cannot do on a bad one.

The sun came up off flat ground the way it does.

## What Came Down The Lane

A carter came through at about the second minute with a hand bar and two things on it and went on up the made road without looking at anything.

The boy from the stair came down, went to the water, and went back up.

A man with a brush came out of the office, stood at the board for a while, and went back in.

The man behind the counter came out to the end of his own floor and said a question into the middle of the air, about the chain, and the boy on the stair answered it from the top of the stair without coming down.

That is all of it. That is the entire contents of eleven minutes on that floor on the seventh of a month.

Not one of those four men came to the rail. Not one of them put a question to her. One of them answered a question that was asked into a room and one of them was asked nothing at all and the other two were on their way somewhere else.

She read all forty-one lines down once, on purpose, at about the fourth minute, and got the same forty-one she has got for forty days, and took nothing, because there is nothing on that board that she could take if she asked for it and she was not going to ask.

## Eleven Counted

She counted it.

That is the part that is different from the last time and she wants it written down, because she has claimed twice in two years to have stood at this rail for eleven minutes and has never once counted to prove it, and the number has been an impression both times.

She counted it on the fingers of her left hand, which is a hand that works, one touch at a time, from the flat of the rail, and when the eleventh touch came she took her hand off the wood.

Seven minutes of something arriving and four minutes of nothing at all.

Eleven minutes, counted, on the seventh of a month, one month after the last time she stood here and did not know how long she had stood.

The difference between the two is small and it is the whole of what she had left to measure.

## What She Did Not Say

She started to say a thing out loud at about the seventh minute.

It was the sentence she said at this rail a month ago, on the seventh of the month before, into the wind, which took about half of it. She bought her own anonymity with her own usefulness. That is the sentence. It is true, it is accurate, and she has said it once, and she says everything out loud exactly once.

She got as far as the first word and she stopped, on purpose, with the word half out of her mouth, because the sentence is finished and there is nothing after it that belongs to it.

What belongs on this rail this morning is the other half, and she had never said it out loud to anybody including herself.

## The Best Thing In Two Years

She said it once, into the wind, in the ordinary voice, on the seventh of a month at the sixth hour with four men on a floor and none of them listening.

“Nobody wants anything from me and it is the best thing that has ever happened to me.”

The wind took about half of it, the same as last time.

She stood there and let that stand, because she says everything out loud exactly once and that one is now said and she is not going to be able to say it again as long as she lives.

Then she put it away.

That is what she is good at and nobody has ever given her credit for it. She has been putting things away since she was fourteen. She put away a name, and a hand, and a bench, and a rate, and a year, and eight months of a floor that asked her about carts every day. She put away a refusal that is standing in the best book on nine days of road with her name in it, and she has not put that away, because she asked for it.

She put this one away too, inside four seconds, and then she stood at a rail on flat ground and watched a man with a brush come out of an office for the second time.

## Before A Twelfth

She did not leave at the twelfth minute.

She wants that said, because it is the thing that would have made the rest of it a lie. She stayed past eleven, on purpose, for four minutes, with her hand off the rail, waiting for something to come down the lane that she had not counted.

Nothing came down the lane.

And she worked out on the fourth of those minutes that she had stayed to find out whether the count was wrong, and that is a different thing from waiting, and she had been doing it since the seventh of a month a month ago and she had got good at it.

So she left.

That is the whole of the finding on the seventh of a month at the seventh hour: she stood at a rail for eleven minutes, counted them, stayed four minutes past the eleventh on her own, and walked away, and there was one thing in the whole of it and it was the four extra minutes.

## The Sum That Does Not Come

She walked back up the lane and did the sum on the way, which is the last useful thing she has got.

It went like this.

She has been counting days since a morning in a shed at a mill when a man put something down and did not pick it up again. She counted it on the eighth of a month as eighty-five, and she has been writing it forward every morning since, which is a number moving, and a number moving is a thing she can do.

And on the seventh of this month she made herself do the real one, which is to take the day it happened away from the day it is now, and see what comes out.

It came out at a hundred and fourteen days.

A hundred and fourteen, by subtraction, at the seventh hour of the seventh of a month, on a lane, in the open. She had been carrying eighty-five for a month, and eighty-five had been true on the eighth of a month, and it had gone on being true for four days after that, and she had gone on writing it forward every morning like a woman marking a wall.

And here is the thing she stopped walking to look at.

It did not point at anything.

It had never pointed at anything. Eighty-five did not point at anything and a hundred and fourteen did not point at anything, and the longer she carried either of them the further away from the one thing she wanted it would take her.

She got to the shed and got the point of the bar out and wrote the true one on the board, and it took four seconds, and it was the first time in thirty days that she had written down a number she had measured instead of a number she had carried.

Then she stood looking at the other lines on that board, the ones underneath the days, and for the first time in four months she understood that she had been writing all of them forward.

## What The Board Is

Three coppers a day, over the last twenty-eight days. One and a half a loaf a day, thirty-six days. Eleven miles to the trough, which is wrong and is six and is now a morning’s walk and a full skin. A door that is four inches of job. A ring that comes up a finger’s width and stops. Forty-one lines read down twice, taken, none.

Eleven lines of arithmetic on a board in a shed, and every single one of them had been carried forward instead of measured, and she had been putting a new day on the top of them for four months and had not once gone down and asked whether the line underneath it was still true.

She got the bar and squared the blade and then she did a thing with it that she had not planned on.

She scraped off the days.

Not all of it. Just the line that had been carrying itself forward since the eighth of a month, forty-one marks deep, one a day, four inches apart, with the true one written over the top of the last.

That took a while. She got the dust up with the side of her boot and the wood went back to the colour of the rest of it, and the board underneath was a board with four months of other arithmetic on it and not one day of the thing she had been counting.

And she sat on the boards in the middle of the afternoon on the seventh of a month with no days written on them anywhere and found that she was not sorry about it, and that she could not work out for a minute why.

Then she worked it out.

Because a board with days on it is a thing you go back to. And she had been going back to it every morning for four months, and every morning the number was a little further forward, and a number that is further forward is a reason to look at it again tomorrow.

It is the same instrument as the waiting. It is the same shape as standing past the eleventh minute at a rail.

And it has been keeping her in a shed four miles east of a mill since the day somebody put a hand down and did not pick it up, and she has had it in her hands every morning for a hundred and fourteen days, and it has not once cost her a thing.

The other eight lines were still on the board, and she went and looked at them, one at a time, for the first time since she had put them there.
