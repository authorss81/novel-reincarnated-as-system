# The Paper Opened Alone

She shut the door on the twenty-second of the month at the fourth hour, on purpose, and it is the first time in two years she has done it.

The gap at the bottom is a finger thick on the hinge side and nothing at all on the other. She got the bar — the light end, which is the end she can hold — and put it flat on the boards and set the bottom of the door down on the end of it and pushed, once, with her hip.

The wind came up against it and stopped.

That is the whole of it. The door shut with a piece of bar under it. The bar was in her way and the bar was the reason it shut, and it took her nine seconds and it is not a repair.

She put the strap round the door ring and pulled it, and then she stood in the middle of the boards with her heart going and her breath at four counts and eleven hours of dark left in the night.

## Four Years Of Not Doing It

She did not do it for a quarter of an hour.

That is the part nobody is going to believe and it is true. She had planned it for four days and she had got up on the twenty-second and put a lump of clay in the ring and eaten nothing and put the bar under the door, and then she stood in the middle of a shed with her hands open at her sides for a quarter of an hour and did not open it.

Four years is a long time to carry a thing.

She was sixteen when she put it there. She had a furnace behind her and a hand that could not write a false line and a paper she could not read and nowhere to go, and she put it inside her own coat against the fourth rib with her own two hands and stitched the lining over it, and it has been there ever since.

She has taken it out eleven times. She has held it and looked at the front of it and put it back eleven times over four years, and eleven is a good number and it is not the number that matters.

What stopped her on the twenty-second, at the fourth hour, with the bar under the door, was not fear and she has got a fairly clear head on that and is not going to pretend otherwise in her own hearing.

It was that there was nobody to do it to.

## Nobody To Do It To

There was a room two months back with the shutters up and forty people in it and a lamp on every table, and a man of forty at the end of the counter reading a column out loud in the middle of his own sentence with a line in it that was about her, and her name in the best book on nine days of road the next day because she had asked for it.

She has been in eleven rooms like that since she was fourteen. She has been handed eleven things in eleven rooms and read eleven things out in eleven rooms, and every single one of them was read to her by somebody qualified, out loud, in daylight, while she stood there.

And she is in a shed with boards on the floor at the fourth hour of the twenty-second of a month with a bar under the door and four fingers and a thumb and nobody within eleven miles.

She put her hand inside her coat.

## The Front Of It

It came out the way it has come out eleven times, folded in four, and the folds are soft as cloth after four years and the outside of it is soft too, and she has carried it in the rain and in a cart and under a coat in a winter.

The front of it says Foundation the first stage.

It has said that since the day she was sixteen and it has never faded and it is not her hand and it is not the hand at the yard and it is not a printed thing. It is written, in a careful upright hand, about half an inch tall, the way a person writes when they are being looked at.

She has read the front of it four hundred times.

She turned it over.

## Seven Lines

There is one sheet inside and it is not folded, because the whole of the thing is folded around it.

There are seven lines on it. They are numbered, one to seven, in the same careful upright hand, and the numbering is done by whoever did the writing and not by a clerk, because the two and the four are the same size as the one.

None of the seven says what to do.

That is the first thing she got out of it and it took her four seconds and then she had to sit down on the boards.

Each one names a place and a thing that happened at it. She read the first: a place she knows, and a thing that happened there, and she knows what happened there because she was there and it took four years.

She read the second. Same shape. She knows it.

The third she knows. The fourth she knows.

The fifth she cannot place. The sixth she cannot place. The seventh she cannot place.

Four out of seven and three she has never heard of, and there is nothing in the writing that says they are hers, and there is nothing in the writing that says they are not.

Under four of the seven lines there is a mark. It is the same mark each time. It is about the size of the head of a nail and it is pressed into the paper rather than written on it, and she has spent eight years reading a hand that four people cannot read and she cannot read this mark, and she looked at all four of them for a minute and a half and then she stopped.

## The Claim At The Foot

At the bottom of the sheet, under the seventh line and above nothing, there is a claim. It is four sentences long. It is in the same hand and it has no name on it and it does not say who has to do anything.

She read it twice.

The paper is a door. It opens for the person it is about and it does not open for anybody else. It opens once. It does not shut again.

That is the whole of it. Four sentences, forty words, in a careful upright hand about half an inch tall.

There is nothing in it about her.

That is what she sat on the boards with for four minutes in the dark in a shed four miles east of a mill. There is nothing in that paper about whether she has done enough, and nothing about whether she may, and nothing about her name, and no office and no date and no rate and nobody in it at all.

She has spent four years being afraid of what was on the other side of the folds.

It was a door. It is a door and it is not a judgement.

## She Opened It

She opened it at the fifth hour of the twenty-second of a month with a bar under a door and her hands shaking from the cold in a way she could not stop.

There is no ceremony to it and there is no moment in it. She unfolded it and there was a sheet inside it and she read the sheet, and that is the whole of the act, and she did it with four fingers and a thumb, alone, in the dark, with a bar under the door and a strap that she had bound out of her own coat lining holding the door ring.

Her hands shook for the whole of it and it took her nine minutes to get through forty words and seven lines and four marks and she had to put it down twice.

That is what it is. That is the whole of it and it took nine minutes and there was nobody qualified in the room and nobody in the room.

And it cannot be un-opened.

She found that out by trying, at the sixth hour, and she wants it written down in the ordinary way: she tried to fold it back the way it had been folded for four years and the crease at the fourth fold is a crease the wrong way now and the paper will not lie flat again.

It is four years of cloth-soft folds and a crease the wrong way. That is all that has changed in the world.

## Terror And Relief

She was afraid and relieved at the same time and both of them came at once, which is not how it is supposed to happen.

The relief was that there is no person in it. Four sentences about a door and a woman in a shed and nobody else’s name anywhere on the sheet, and nothing in it that can be read out loud to her in a room by anybody.

The fear was three lines on a sheet that she cannot place, and she could not have told anybody on nine days of road what the fear was, and she has not tried.

And then, at the seventh hour of the twenty-second, sitting on the boards with her back against the wall and the paper in her lap and the sky going grey at the gap under the door:

She was still herself.

That is the plain part and she said it out loud once, on the boards, because she says everything out loud exactly once.

She was eighteen years old. Four fingers and a thumb and an arm that was open at her side and a small open place behind her fourth rib and eight of eleven holds and one day of nothing to eat and three coppers gone and a shed with boards on the floor four miles east of a mill whose wheel has not turned since before she came.

Nothing on nine days of road wanted anything from her. A woman at a store had said “you are back” to her once and a woman at a store had told her there was no book for a person who stops coming.

He had been silent for one hundred and twenty-nine days and had nothing to spend and had said nothing.

All of that was still true at the seventh hour of the twenty-second of a month, and she was the same person who had lain on the boards the night before with a plan, and a door does not change anybody.

It changes what they can do.

She got the point of the bar out and did not write anything on the board.

She was not going to put it on a road. She was not going to put it on a board. She was going to keep forty words and seven lines and three lines she cannot place against her own ribs, and it is the first thing she has ever owned that nobody can take by reading it.

And the door under her back was shut, with a bar under it, on purpose, and she had opened something that could not be un-opened, and she had done it alone.
