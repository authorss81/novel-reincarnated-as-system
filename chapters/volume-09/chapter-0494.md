# Cut Timber And A Stile

She got as far as the lean-to of cut timber at the side of the made road a mile short of the mill before the light went, and she lay down on the boards under it at the ninth hour with the bar across her knees, and she was up again before the fourth hour.

That is the whole of the first night and it took about a minute to have it.

She had done it three times before she worked out what she had done, and the third time was the one she counted. She lay down. She put her left hand flat on the boards. She waited for the ground to come up under her hand the way it came up under the dish on the nineteenth of the month, and nothing came up, and after forty seconds she took her hand off.

She got up, and she walked nine feet out into the road, and she stood there in the dark and found that she was awake and upright and had no idea at what point she had decided.

Then she went and lay down again on purpose and it happened again inside nine minutes.

So it was not the cold and it was not the wind and it was not the boards. She went back to the lean-to and lay down on the boards with the bar under her head and worked it out properly, and it took her the better part of an hour and it came down to one sentence.

She had got up because she was somewhere she had chosen for the night and she was not going to be there in the morning.

That is what she said out loud on the boards of a lean-to at the second hour, once, because she says everything out loud exactly once.

## What She Took

She did not go back for the ring.

She wants that on the page because it is the whole of what the three nights are. She did not go back for the ring and the dish and the board, and it was not pride and it was not a decision she made standing up. She was four miles east of the shed at the seventh hour of the twentieth with a bar on her shoulder and she did not turn round, and by the time she thought about the clay ring it was dark and she did not turn round then either.

It is the first time in eight years she has left a thing of hers behind on purpose, and the reason is the sentence she said at the second hour, which is that a thing you go back for is a thing that has a place, and she had worked that out on a floor in a book about carts.

She has four fingers and a thumb and an arm that is open at the side and she was carrying a bar she cannot hold at the heavy end.

She had the coat with the lining four inches narrower than it was. She had a piece of paper against her ribs that is open and has never been given a trade term. She had a coat lining and a bar and a piece of paper and that is the whole of what she took off a floor she had slept on for two years.

## The Second Night

The second night she put her back against the bank of the trough road, a mile and a half short of the stone, where the ground goes down to the goats.

She got water at the fourth hour and filled the skin and drank for a quarter of an hour sitting on the bank, and there were forty goats on that road and not one of them looked at her, and the whole of her life up to two years ago had been rooms with people in them.

Then she lay down on the bank in the middle of the night with the bar across her and the wind coming off the low ground, and she got the fourth count forty times before the light, because the place behind her fourth rib takes the fourth count and she has to go and get it.

She got up twice. Both times she stood in the road.

The third time was the one she is going to remember, and what she remembered afterwards was not that she got up. It was that on the way back to the bank she stopped and put her hand on the ground and asked it a question, in the ordinary voice, about the fourth count.

It came. It came in nine seconds, which it has never once done in the open anywhere.

She got her breath, and she went and lay down, and she lay there and worked out what she had just done, and what she had done was ask a piece of flat ground to give her a count of breath at the fourth hour of the morning, which is not a thing anybody asks for, and which it did.

## The Third Night

The third night she went as far as a stile on the made road with a hedge on the far side of it, four miles west of the shed, because a stile is high and dry and out of the wind and it is the best place on that ground to sleep if you have nothing.

She lay down with her back against the upright post of it at the ninth hour and she was up at the second, and this time she did not go and stand in the road, because by the second night she had got used to it and that was worse.

So she sat on the ground at the foot of the stile with her back against the post and did the arithmetic, which is what she does now instead of the other thing.

Bread: none. She had had the last of a loaf on the twenty-first of a month two months back and she had had nothing since, and the four months of not wanting food finished somewhere around the sixth of a month and she has been hungry since and has been living at one day out of seven out of the trough road.

Water: the trough road, six miles from the shed, and a skin that holds one day.

Money: none. Three coppers on the fifteenth of a month, one on the twenty-third, and an arithmetic board with four lines on it that said so.

Wages: none, and no season, and no board on nine days of road with a line on it for a person, and she is not going to be on one.

That is six lines and it took her eleven minutes and not one of them pointed at a person.

Then she wrote the seventh line on the ground with the point of the bar, in the dirt, and it went like this: the shed at the mill has a ring in it that has come up a finger’s width a month for two years.

And then she looked at the seven lines and did the thing she has done four times in five weeks, which is to ask what they add up to.

They added up to thirteen miles.

## The Line On The Board

There is a rail outside a lifting floor nine hundred yards from a shed at a mill whose wheel has not turned since before she came, and there were forty-one lines on the board outside it on the seventeenth of a month, and she has read all forty-one of them down twice and taken none.

One of the forty-one is not a weight and not a length.

A leg, on a man of thirty-four, on the fifteenth of a month.

She had never looked at that one properly. She had read it with the other forty because it is on the board and she reads the board down, and she had taken it four times without seeing it, and on the morning of the fourth of a month, sitting at the foot of a stile with a bar across her knees and seven lines in the dirt, she read it out loud to herself for the first time in eight years.

There is a frame thirteen miles east. She has been to it. Nine men on it and four hundred years of oak and it comes down the wrong way, and a man of thirty-four goes the length of it thirty times a day with a bar over his shoulder and his leg wrapped to the ankle.

She fitted a leg once. She was seventeen and it was four years ago and it was a different leg and a different man and she did it out of a book with a hand in it that four people could not take, and it took her a morning, and the man walked out of it at the fourth hour.

Nobody has asked her to do anything since.

That is not the complaint. She has had four months of not being asked for and she said out loud at a rail on the seventh of the month that it was the best thing that had ever happened to her, and she is not going to take it back in the dirt at the foot of a stile.

The complaint is that she is eighteen years old and has no bread and no money and no wages, and there is a line on a board thirteen miles east that she can do, and no one is going to bring it to her.

So she is going to go and get it.

## The Decision

She said that out loud on the ground at the foot of the stile at the fourth hour of the morning, in the ordinary voice, standing up, once.

“I am going to walk to the frame and do a leg I was not asked to do, and I am not going to be paid for it, and that is the whole of what I have got.”

Then she got the point of the bar out and squared it against the post and put it on her shoulder, and she went east.

She was four hours on the road before the sun came up over flat ground, and she was on the made road by the third hour and at the seventh mile by the seventh hour, and the kerb starts at the seventh mile and she walked it with the bar in her right hand and her right arm in the strap of it for two hours, which is two hours of a body at the top of a realm doing nothing at all.

She ate nothing on the fourth, the fifth and the sixth of a month. She drank twice out of her own skin and once off the trough road. She did the ring on the morning of the fifth in a dish she made out of a piece of bark, and it came up a finger’s width and stopped, and she did not try it a second time.

And at the second hour of the sixth of a month she came off the made road onto the track that goes down to the cut, and there were nine men on a frame four hundred years old, and one of them had a leg wrapped to the ankle.
