# The Mistake They Made

The mistake arrived at the store on the twenty-third of a month and it arrived as a loaf of bread put down on a counter and not picked up.

She had been west for six days. She had a leg-mould scraped and a leg fitted and half a skin and no money and had walked about forty miles in five days, and she came into the store at the ninth hour in the morning the way she has come into it four hundred times, and put nothing on the wood, and the woman came round from behind the shelves.

“Good,” she said, and then she looked at her, and she said, “You are thinner.”

“I am,” Ning Yan said.

The woman put a loaf on the counter and put her hand flat on it.

“No,” she said.

That is what happened. She put a hand on a loaf and said no on her own counter in her own store, which she has not done in thirty years, and Ning Yan stood there with her hands at her sides.

“I have not got any money,” she said, which was a stupid thing to say, and she heard herself say it.

“I did not ask you for money.”

“I cannot take it,” Ning Yan said. “You know I cannot take it.”

The woman put both hands on the counter and looked at her for a second and a half.

“No,” she said, “I did not know that. I have known you two years and I did not know that.”

And Ning Yan said, “It is not a thing people can be told,” and the woman said, “Then that is a thing I have been getting wrong for two years,” and went behind the shelves and would not come out for four minutes, and brought out a cup of water instead of the loaf, which is not a gift, and set it on the counter, and said, “Drink that at least.”

## What Was Said To Her

Nobody had said one word to her of any of it. Not the carrier, who had stopped saying good. Not the man of twenty-two, who had pulled his horse up.

It was the woman at the store who said it, and she said it on her way out from behind the shelves, without stopping, in the ordinary way she has said good to Ning Yan two hundred times.

“If you have not got anywhere to go,” the woman said, “there is a room at the end of the lane behind the bench shed. I know the woman. You have only to say.”

“No,” Ning Yan said.

“That is all right.”

“You do not know what you are offering it for,” Ning Yan said, and she heard the edge in it, and the woman heard it too and stopped.

And then the woman said the thing that made it a mistake rather than only a kindness.

“You have got somebody,” she said. “Have you.”

It was not a question and she did not ask it as one and she gave it half a second of room, which is two thirds of what Ning Yan needs to answer a question of that shape.

## The Road Outside

She did not answer it.

She took the cup and drank it and put it down on the wood and said thank you, which she has said four hundred times in two years and has never once meant as anything, and went out into the road.

Then she stood in the road outside a store five miles west of a mill for the length of time it takes a person to get their breath back properly, which was a quarter of an hour, and she did not go and stand in the sun the way she does when something has happened to her.

She stood in the road and got it out.

She said it out loud, once, at the quarter of an hour, in the ordinary voice, on a made road with nobody within nine hundred yards, because she says everything out loud exactly once.

“They think somebody has got me.”

That is the whole of it. That is the sentence, and it is accurate, and it is the wrong sentence, and it is wrong in a way that took four months to get to and that she would not have believed in the fourth month.

## What It Was Not

She is not in a room. She has not been in a room since a night in a month that has gone in a lean-to of cut timber at the side of the made road.

She is not fed by a person. She has been eating at the leg-man’s bench one day in four for a fortnight and paying nothing, because he is not a kind man and he does not ask, and she cannot pay and cannot take and he puts it out and takes nothing for it, which is the only arrangement on nine days of road that has ever worked for her.

She is not answering to anything. She has refused three things in eight years and the last one went into the best book on nine days of road two months ago and she has not been refused by anybody.

And she is not being kept, which is the thing the woman at the store has got wrong, and the reason she has got it wrong is that it is the only shape there is on this ground for a woman of eighteen to be in, and everybody on nine days of road has been watching her be that shape for two years and then watching it stop, and they have filled the gap the only way anybody fills a gap.

## The Anger

The anger came up on the made road at the second mile going east and it was the second real one she has had in five months and it lasted longer than the first.

The first one, over a loaf of bread, was about wanting things, and it went off her in a quarter of an hour on a stone at a mile mark.

This one did not.

What she was angry with was not the woman, who has been good to her for two years and who was kind this morning for a reason that is nine hundred yards of her life and not one thing about her.

The anger was the arrangement and not the woman.

She had bought a silence and the silence had worked. Four months of it. She was the strongest person in every room and she walked out of every one of them, and she got to the end of that road with no money and no wages and no bread and nobody in the world who could say where she slept, and she has said out loud at a rail that it was the best thing that had ever happened to her.

And all of it turns on a piece of paper folded in four against her ribs.

That is the trade. Four months of the best thing that has ever happened to her, and it lasts until she opens a sheet of paper, and then she is a woman somebody is keeping, and a shopkeeper two hundred yards away can see it from the counter.

She said that out loud at the third mile, once, and then she worked it out properly on the flat, and the working-out took a quarter of an hour and it came to four lines and it is the shape of the rest of her life.

She cannot correct it and she cannot correct it without the book.

## What She Decided

She came back to the shed at the end of the lane at the seventh hour on the twenty-seventh and there was a bench and a post and a plank door and she put the bar round the post and she did not sleep, and she sat down at the ninth hour and said the decision out loud, once.

“I am not going to say one word of it,” she said. “Not to the woman at the store, not to the carrier, not to the leg man, not to the woman of fifty at the end of this lane. If I say one word about why I am the way I am, then there is one thing about me in a room on nine days of road, and I have not got one for two months and I am not going to have one for anything a shopkeeper did not ask me.”

And then, out loud, once more, and she is aware that she is using up the rest of what she has in one evening:

“If I stop being careful with them I have to explain. If I explain, it is the page. And the page is the only thing I have.”

That is the whole of it and it took her five days to get to and she did not cry and she was not going to, and she lay down on the bench at the eleventh hour with her clothes on.

And the thing she could not get past, and did not try to, was that a woman behind a counter had looked at her for a second and a half and said I did not know that, and had been wrong about her for two years, out of nothing but being good.

## The Price Of Being Known

She got it out on the flat at the third mile and it was worse than the anger and it came in under it.

If they think she has been kept, then she has been kept, in the only way she has ever been kept. That is not a room and not a chain and not a wage. It is a person’s attention. Four hundred times in eight years somebody has wanted a thing out of her and she has had to be in a place for it.

And eleven people on nine days of road have decided this month that somebody wants something out of her and that she is the one inside it, and not one of them is wrong about the shape, and every one of them is wrong about the person.

She said it out loud at the third mile, once, in the ordinary voice, on flat ground with nobody within nine hundred yards.

“They have got the shape of it,” she said. “They have not got one single thing about it right, and they have got the shape of it, and the shape of it is what I have been in since I was fourteen.”
