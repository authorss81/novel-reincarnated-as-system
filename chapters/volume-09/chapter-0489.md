# What She Read Inside

She read it again on the twenty-seventh of the month, in the afternoon, with the door shut and a bar under it, and it took her nine minutes both times and it came out the same both times.

Seven lines. Four marks. Four sentences at the foot.

She read the seven lines first on the twenty-second and she read them first again on the twenty-seventh, and that is the order she read them in because that is the order they are in.

The first is a place she knows and a thing that happened at it. The second is the same. The third and the fourth are the same.

The fifth, the sixth and the seventh are the same, and she cannot place any of the three, and she has now had five days with them and has not come up with anything.

## What It Does Not Say

What it does not say took her a day and a half and it is the whole of the finding.

It does not say how. There is nothing in it about a room and nothing a second pair of hands and nothing six days. It does not say what it is for. It does not say what is on the other side, or whether there is anything on the other side, or what a person does after.

It does not say she may.

There is not one word on that sheet about whether she has done enough, and she has looked at it four times in five days in daylight and in the dark with a bar under a door, and it is not there, and it is not going to arrive.

She has spent two years being handed things that told her what she was allowed to do. Every one of them had a room in it and a person in the room and a condition, and eleven times the condition was somebody qualified standing in front of her with the daylight on it.

This told her the price and not the thing.

## Reading Is Not Opening

On the twenty-eighth she worked out the part she had been carrying around for five days without looking at it.

It says it opens once. She has opened it. She has had it out of her coat twice and read it twice and there is nothing in her that has moved.

So it has not opened.

That is the whole of the arithmetic and it took her four seconds and then she had to put her hand on the boards.

Reading it is not opening it. She had been treating the two as one thing since she was sixteen because they are the same gesture — a piece of paper, two hands, a fold — and they are not the same gesture at all. One of them took nine minutes. The other one is not something she has done.

And there is no third thing. There is no state between the two where you have got it out and you have read it and you have put it back and something has started.

She sat on the boards with that and worked out what it means, and what it means is that there is no version of this in which she does anything at all on the twenty-second or the twenty-eighth or the nineteenth of a month.

It has to be a separate act and she has not done it and she cannot do it by reading harder.

## One Question Out Loud

She asked it on the twenty-eighth in the afternoon, with the door shut, standing up, in the ordinary voice.

That is how it works. She has known it since she was fourteen and she has never once found a way round it, and it has taken four hundred loads and lengths and one leg off a man of thirty-four in eight years, and she has never once asked it a question that was not about a thing that could be carried by two men and measured against a rate.

She stood in the middle of the boards and asked it, out loud, one question, about the paper in her hand.

Nothing happened.

Not slowly. Not a little. The same nothing that has come back for a hundred and twenty-nine days, except that this time it was not the silence of a man with nothing to spend. It was the ordinary quiet of a reach that has been asked a question it cannot act on.

She asked it a second time nine seconds later, to be sure, because the first time might have been her mouth.

Nothing.

She stood there for a couple of minutes after that and worked out what it means, and what it means is that the one thing in this world that does what she says has never once done anything for her that was not work, and it is not going to, and the door is not going to be a load of stone or a length of paving.

There is nothing in eight years that can help her do this and she has known that since a shed at a mill four months back and she has never been able to make it hurt the way it should.

## The Mark

She put the sheet up against the light of the lamp in the daytime at the store — not the one in the shed; she did not take it out in a store — she put it against the sky through the door gap, on the twenty-eighth, and looked at the four marks from underneath, and then at it from above, and then she got the point of the bar out very carefully and put it on the edge of one of them the way you put a point on a page to find out whether the writing is on both sides.

There is nothing on the back.

She has read a hand that four people cannot read for eight years and there is not one mark in that hand that is not made of ink going in one direction at one speed, and the four marks on that sheet are not made of anything she knows how to read.

They are pressed in. They go into the paper about the depth of a nail head and they have a shape that is regular in a way that handwriting is not, and she looked at all four of them for nine minutes and then folded the paper back up, which took her a while, because of the crease.

She has one instrument and it read a hand and it read forty-one lines twice, and it cannot read a mark the size of the head of a nail, and she has known both of those things about herself since she was sixteen.

## The Lane And The Counter

She went down to the lane on the twenty-ninth with the paper inside her coat and nothing bought, and she went as far as the trough road and back, and she went into the store at the ninth hour with an empty basket because the walking had got to be something she did on purpose.

“Good,” the woman said.

“That is a whole day you have given me,” Ning Yan said.

The woman did not understand that and did not ask about it and said good again nine minutes later, and she went behind the shelves, and that was the whole of it.

And on the way back up the lane she worked out that she had just told a shopkeeper that a day had been given to her, and that it was true, and that the shopkeeper had heard a stranger talking a day and had decided, correctly, that it was about bread.

## Eleven Hundred Years

She got the figure out on the thirtieth and she is putting it down here because it is the first number in five days that came from anybody else and not from a bar’s point on a board.

Eleven hundred years.

A woman of sixty-one stood at the end of a bench in a shed at a mill and said it, and said it while selling her something, and said it in the ordinary voice, and said it accurately. Eleven hundred years of practice at the thing she does, which is taking a sheet that four people cannot take and turning it into numbers and reading them out loud in a room and being believed.

Eleven hundred years, and in every one of them the reading has been done to a person by somebody else.

That is the finding, and she got it on the thirtieth of a month sitting on the boards with a paper in her lap that says a door opens for the person it is about and for nobody else.

In eleven hundred years of that practice there has not been one door in the world opened from the inside, because the inside of it has never belonged to the person the door is about.

And she is eighteen years old and servant-born and there is no office past nine days of road that would read a paper to her and she is not going to ask one to.

So: hers, or not at all.

She did not say that out loud. She has said out loud, once, everything she has worked out in five days, and there is one more sentence in her that is going to want saying, and she is keeping it.

## Nineteen Days

The first of the month came and went on its own and she wrote it on the board in the morning along with the housekeeping, and it is the second month in a row she has watched one turn without a person in it.

And that night she lay on the boards in a shed with the door shut and a bar under it, and she did not go to sleep for a while, and she did the thing she does now instead of the other thing, which is to add up what is in front of her.

Bread: none. There has been none since the twenty-first and she has been at the trough road every second day and she has been getting one day out of seven out of what that road has and it is not bread.

Paper: opened. Read. Not the act.

Ring: still the clay, still the dish, still asking for water and pressure and a day, and still not coming up.

Body: four fingers and a thumb, an arm at half, eight of eleven holds, four counts of breath, and a place behind her fourth rib that is open about the size of a coin.

And this, which is the part she had to stop counting halfway through and go and put her hand flat on the boards for.

She has never once thought of the shed as hers.

Not once in two years, and not this month, and there is a reason and the reason is that the last place she kept she had to give back and the year after that was not hers and has been on four floors since.

And a door that opens for the person it is about and for nobody else is a thing you are about to do alone, at the fourth hour, with a bar under a door, and she has got a body with a reach in it that answers a question and only a question, and she has never once asked it a question that was not about a load of stone or a length of paving or a man of thirty-four with a leg gone.

Day one thousand three hundred and ninety-four is the first of a month. Day one thousand four hundred and twelve is the nineteenth of the next one. That is eighteen days, by subtraction, and she has written nothing on the board about what is going to happen in them.

Eighteen days to find out what opening costs a place.
