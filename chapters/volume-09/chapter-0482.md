# The Store Five Miles West

She went out on the twenty-second and got to the store on the twenty-third and did not buy anything on the way, which cost her nothing, because water is free out of her own skin and there is nothing on that road to stop for.

She had one copper. She had half a loaf and a piece of bread that had gone the colour of the inside of an apple.

She walked in at the third hour with the bar against her shoulder and stood at the counter, and the woman came round from behind the shelves.

“Good,” she said.

Two years of that. Two hundred times of that, and Ning Yan had never once worked out before what it actually meant, and it took her four seconds to work it out standing there on the twenty-third.

The woman was not being kind. Kindness costs a person something and she has not spent anything since Ning Yan came through that door. The woman was being ordinary, in the way that a person behind a counter is ordinary to a person who comes in, and being ordinary to her had been going on for two years without either of them noticing.

## Bread Nobody Buys

“What happens to bread nobody buys,” Ning Yan said.

The woman put her hands flat on the counter.

“That is a strange thing to open with,” she said, and she was not wrong about it, and she answered it anyway, because that is what a person does at a counter.

“It goes stale. Two days, sometimes three in a cold month. After that it is not bread, it is a thing people look at.”

“And then?”

“Then it goes out on the trough road at the back, and there are goats on the trough road, and the goats are the reason that road exists and not the bread.” She stopped there. “That is the whole of it. That is what happens to bread nobody buys.”

## The Trough Road

Ning Yan had never once been down the trough road.

It goes out of the back of that store for a mile and a half, and it is a cart track, and it has been a cart track for four hundred years, and in two years of walking to that counter she has looked at it nine hundred times through a door and never once put her feet on it.

The goats were put on that road because the road was already there. The road is there because the stone was quarried. There are forty goats on it and there is somebody whose work it is to look after them, and the goats are not the point of any of it and have never been the point of any of it.

And at the bottom of that road, where it comes out at the trough itself, there is water.

Eleven miles from where she sleeps. The woman had said that and Ning Yan had believed it, and she had believed it on four separate occasions without ever once going and finding out.

She stood at the counter and put that together, quickly, the way she puts arithmetic together.

She had spent two years not being hungry in a body that does not want food, and there had been water four miles out of her way the entire time, and she had not gone and got it, and the reason she had not gone and got it is that there had never been one thing on that ground worth four miles.

That had changed on the fifteenth of a month, when she put two coppers down for a thing she wanted.

## The Second Question

“Then what happens to the bread a person buys and does not finish.”

The woman looked at the loaf half gone on the counter where Ning Yan had put it when she came in, and then she looked up.

“Then I take it home,” she said. “That is what anybody does with it. I have two children and a house four miles east of here and I do not throw bread away because a person carried it five miles to put it on my counter.”

That was the whole of the second answer and it was more than the first one had been worth, and it was said in the ordinary voice with no weight put on it at all, and it has been true in every store on nine days of road for a thousand years and nobody has ever said it out loud to Ning Yan before.

She stood there and let it go past her.

“That is fair,” she said.

“It is not anything to do with fair. It is a loaf of bread.”

## The Third Question

And then Ning Yan asked the third one, and she asked it before she had decided to ask it, which is the only way she has ever asked anything that turned out badly.

“Is there anybody ever in here who is not buying anything.”

The woman put one hand on the counter and looked at her for a second and a half.

“That is three questions,” she said. “You get two about bread. The third one is not about bread.”

“I am sorry.”

“You are welcome to it,” the woman said. “I am not going to stop you coming in for it.”

And then she went behind the shelves and put a loaf in a box and brought it out and set it on the counter, and Ning Yan put the copper down, and it was the last one, and that was the whole of what she had.

She did not tell the woman that. She is not going to tell the woman that and she has never told anybody that, and it is the last piece of coin she owns on nine days of road and she put it on the wood for a loaf of bread in a store on the twenty-third of a month.

The woman took it and put it in the box and said nothing wrong about it.

## The Name She Was Never Asked For

Ning Yan took the loaf and put her hand on the counter for a second the way a person does when they are about to go.

The woman said, “Good,” and went behind the shelves.

That was the end of it. That is how a store ends.

She stood there with her hand on the wood and worked out, standing up, in three seconds, that the woman behind that counter had served her two hundred times in two years and had never once asked her name.

Not once. Not in the first year when Ning Yan came in off a made road with a bar she could not hold at the heavy end and put down a coin every time. Not in the winter. Not on the day a man of forty swept four coppers back across another counter. Not last month, when Ning Yan was the strongest person in every room she had stood in for two years and walked out of one of them.

Two years. Not her name, not where she slept, not what she did with the days, not why.

And the thing she felt standing there on the twenty-third of a month at the third hour was not relief.

She wanted to be asked.

She wanted it so badly that she had to take her hand off the counter and go out into the road and stand in the sun for a minute, because she has wanted very little for four months and the wanting arrived all at once in a store five miles west of a mill whose wheel has not turned since before she came.

## What She Came Five Miles For

She got as far as the mile stone before she said it out loud, which she does once and only once for anything she has worked out.

“I went five miles to ask about bread,” she said, “because I could not ask about a piece of paper.”

And then she stood in the road and let that be true for nine seconds, because it was the only true thing she had said in four months and it did not get better with carrying and it did not get any worse.

There is a piece of paper against her ribs. It is the only door in the world. It is folded in four and it is soft at the folds and it says Foundation the first stage on the front of it, and it has been there four years.

She cannot put that question into the air in a room. She has known that since a room with a lamp in it and a man of forty reading her own entry back to her, and she has not been near a paper since.

But bread is a thing you may ask a shopkeeper about. Bread is the ordinary end of a conversation at a counter and it costs a copper and it gets answered.

So she spent the last copper she owns on a loaf of bread and three questions, and she got two answers about bread and one sentence about herself out of a woman who has been good to her for two years without once being curious about her, and she walked home in the sun at the fourth hour with the bar on her shoulder and one hand on a loaf and one hand on a door that does not shut properly.

And she was ashamed, and she was not ashamed of the bread.

She was ashamed because she had worked out that the reason she could not ask about the paper is that she has been read to, once, in a room, by somebody qualified, and she would rather go five miles and spend her last coin than put that question in the air in front of a stranger.

That is not a reason. That is a wound with a door on the side of it and she has put a counter in front of it and bought bread.

## Three To None

She was up to the shed before the light on the twenty-fifth and she did the ring and she did not go anywhere.

The fold in the inside of the coat is stitched shut and there was nothing in it. Not three. Not one. A hundred and twenty-three days of nothing bought and nothing sold, because there has been nothing on this ground to buy and nothing anybody on it has offered to sell her, and she has paid out thirty-two coppers in that time and earned none of them.

She got the point of the bar out and wrote the subtraction on the board, the same way she has written it nine times in two years.

Three. Two. One. None.

And she looked at the four lines for a while, because those four lines are the whole of what she has bought in four months and she wanted to see them together.

Two of them were a loan she will not take. One of them was a price somebody did not charge her. One of them was a loaf she wanted.

Then she put the bar back against the wall and lay down on the boards in her clothes with the wind coming in under the door at a foot a second, and the loaf beside her, and no money anywhere on nine days of road, and six days of bread if she was careful with it.

She has never once been in that position. There has always been something — a bench, a floor, a bench at the end of a lane that has been empty since the seventh of a month, a table in a room with a lamp in it.

There were eleven days to the end of that month and after that a new one, and she had no idea at all what she was going to do with any of it, and she slept.
