# The Keeper And His Stone

Wei Ran’s team came out of the west at the second hour on the eighteenth with boards on the cart and stopped two hundred yards short of her, which it always does.

“Where,” Wei Ran said.

“West, nine miles, and back.”

“Nine coppers a head,” Wei Ran said. “And the bar goes in by itself.”

That is the second time she has said it out loud to Ning Yan in ten days and she said it the same way both times, at the same speed, which Ning Yan has noted and is not going to make anything of.

“You said that on the seventh.”

“I say it to everybody the same,” Wei Ran said. “That is what a rate is. If I said it different to you, it would not be a rate, and you would know it, and so would the next person after you.”

She said the number out loud a third time, to the woman on the front bench, who was about forty and had been on that team for six years and who was looking at the bar.

“That is her,” the woman said.

“That is her,” Wei Ran said, and got the cart in motion.

She was carried nine miles west in two hours and got off at the post on the strip road with a leg-mould in a box under her arm and a bar on her shoulder, and she had not eaten since the sixteenth and she had been walking since the fourteenth.

## The Post

It is a post with a board on it, a ladder against the side of it that goes up to the board and not further, and a rail for tying a thing to, and a trough at the foot of it with a dipper on a chain.

The keeper is fifty-five or near it. He has a paste pot and a rag and a piece of board a foot square, and he fills the board off a cart every nine or ten days, and the cart is somebody else’s business.

She has stopped here twice in two years and both times she had nothing, and both times she had gone on.

He had the board half full when she came up, which means it is between days.

“Water,” he said, and put the dipper in the trough and hung it back without looking at her, and she drank a quarter of an hour standing up, and it was the third time in a month that anybody had given her water.

## The Ladder

She saw the stone while she was drinking.

It is a flat stone the size of two hands together under the bottom of the ladder, where nobody walks, and there is a book on it, and the book is the size of a man’s two hands and it is not a book about boards.

She did not touch it and she did not ask what it is and she did not read the spine, and there is a reason for all three of those and the reason is that she is the only person within nine days of road who can read a hand four people cannot take and she has spent eight years reading things that were not put in front of her.

She looked at it for nine seconds and then she got her attention off it deliberately, the way she got it off the board outside the lifting floor in the four months when she did not want the work.

## The Five Sentences

He said them at the fourth hour, standing at the rail with his rag in his hand, without being asked and without stopping what he was doing, and she counted them and they are five.

“I keep it under the ladder,” he said. “Nineteen years, under that ladder, on that stone.”

“Nobody asked you to.”

“No.”

“Three names in it.”

“Three,” he said. “There have been three names in it in nineteen years and every one of them I put there myself.”

He wrung the rag out.

“No office knows it is there and no office has ever sent me a thing about it and no man has ever come up this road and asked me for it, and it is going to be here after I am dead and whoever has this post next will not know to look.”

He put the rag over his shoulder.

“That is all of it and I am not going to say the rest of it.”

That is the whole of what he said and he said it in forty seconds and he did not look at her while he was saying any of it and he went back to the board.

## What She Did Not Do

She had it in her hand to ask him for the stone, on the strength of nine seconds of looking at a book on a stone, from a man who has told her he will not say the rest of it.

She did not ask, and it is the hardest thing she has not done in eight years, and she wants it said in her own words and not as a virtue.

And what stopped her was not that it was his.

What stopped her was that she has four things of her own written down in the world and three of them were put there by other hands, and she worked that out on the third of a month at the seventh hour while a boy put a chain up and down forty feet off, and she has not been able to put it down since.

She is going to add to it.

That is the whole reason and it took her nine seconds standing at a post at the fourth hour with a skin and a bar, and then she asked him something else instead.

“When the cart comes, do the boards go up or down.”

“Down,” he said. “They come off the long road. Everything goes down this road.”

## The Thing He Said Going

He did not say anything else for two hours. She filled the skin and sat on the rail with the box at her feet and waited for the cart the way she waits for nothing now, which is to say she did not wait for it.

And then, as she was getting up to go east with a leg-mould under her arm and nine miles of road in front of her, he said, without turning round and without any weight on it:

“You have been hungry for about a month and you have not been eating and you have been walking. There is a leg man at a cut four miles east of the turn at the end of this road. He has a loaf on his bench at the seventh hour and he is not a kind man and he will not ask you a single question about anything.”

She stood there.

“That is the second time somebody has told me where there is bread in eleven days,” she said.

“I did not know that,” he said.

“No,” Ning Yan said. “You did not.”

And she went east with a bar on her shoulder and a full skin and one leg-mould in a box and about nine days of walking left in her, and she did not look back at the post, and there was nothing behind her but a board and a rag and a stone with a book on it under a ladder.

## The Board

It is a foot square and it is filled off a cart every nine or ten days, and there is no rate on it and no body on it and nothing about a person on it at all.

She read it standing up the way she reads everything, and it took her a minute and a half, and it was thirty-one lines and every one of them is a thing going down a road.

She took none of it, and she stood there and looked at it for nine minutes afterwards, and it was the first board in five months she had wanted something off.

That is the part she has kept. There are thirty-one lines on a foot square of board at a post nine miles west of a lane, and there is not one floor on nine days of road with a line on it for her, and a man with a paste pot fills that board off somebody else’s cart and clears it with a rag, and it is the same instrument as forty-one lines outside a lifting floor and it has given her the same answer.

She read it twice and got it the same twice.

## Twenty In The Spring

“You have been on that board nineteen years.”

“Twenty in the spring,” he said.

“Nobody holds a post for twenty years for the boards.”

“No,” he said. “There is a house four miles west with four people in it and I eat there on Sundays and there is a woman in it who has been putting up with this since before I came.”

He looked at the rail and not at the stone.

“You have understood the thing about this post in four minutes and it took me nine years,” he said, “and I am not going to tell you what I do it for, and you did not ask me, and that is the whole reason I have had this conversation instead of the other one.”

She left before the cart came and she did not ask him for the stone, and she has thought about that twice since.

## The Cart, When It Came

It came at the fifth hour, up the strip road from the west, and it was somebody else’s cart and it went on down the long road.

The keeper put the rag down and went to it and worked for a quarter of an hour without speaking, taking the board off the post and scraping it down the side, and she stood eleven paces off and watched him do it because there was nothing else to watch.

It is not a careful job. It is a rag and a pot and a flat board and a man who has done it nine hundred times, and he does not put the pot down in the middle of it and he does not look at his hands.

And after it was done he washed the rag in the trough and hung it on the rail and put the pot on the board and stood there four minutes doing nothing at all, looking up the strip road at a strip road with nothing on it.

She had seen a man stand and do nothing at a post four hundred times in two years and had never once known what he was doing it for, and it is the same shape as a ring in a dish on boards in a shed, and she said that out loud once, at the fifth hour, on the strip road, in the ordinary voice, to nobody.

“Everything anybody keeps keeps something that is not for them,” she said. “That is the whole of what a day is.”
