# Nothing Holds Her Here

She was sixteen nights into a room nobody had given her, on the nineteenth of a month that began on the seventeenth of the one before it, and she worked out everything there was to work out before the light came off the flat.

## The Count

Day one thousand five hundred and sixty-one is the day the clay came up out of the dish at a turn in a lane and the third cost landed. Day one thousand five hundred and ninety-eight is the thirty-seventh day after it, and one thousand five hundred and ninety-eight less one thousand five hundred and sixty-one is thirty-seven.

Thirty-seven days, counted one at a time off the back of the last one, in her head, in the ordinary way.

She has counted days since she was six. For eight years of it she counted them against a stick with a notch on it for every week and a longer one for a month, and the stick went into a furnace in the fourth month of a year she could name with the rest of it.

This is the first run of days she has had to count with nothing to count them against. She works them out against the light coming off the flat, because that is the only thing in a nine-foot room that happens.

## What Is In It

Nine feet by eleven against the west face of a wall. Boards under her, a chimney that draws, a window nine inches by twenty facing west, a shelf with a jug on it and a blanket folded on the shelf in a manner four hundred years old.

A chair. A table with four legs on it. A book on the table.

A bed frame against the west side with nothing on the frame at all, and there is not one inch of straw in it and there is not one nail driven crooked.

That is the whole of it and she has been through it nine times in sixteen nights and counted the objects, because counting the objects is what she does.

None of it is hers.

## The Thing That Is Not New

Here is the part she had to put in the right place and it took her most of the first morning.

A shed with boards on the floor four miles east of a mill stopped being hers on the nineteenth of a month in a year she could name, for having slept in it for two years.

She has known that for a hundred and fifty days. She has carried it the way she carries four fingers and a thumb and a hole behind her fourth rib, as a fact about the world, and she was not going to stand in a room and find it out again like it was news.

So the finding on the nineteenth was not the finding. She knew it before she opened her eyes.

The finding was what it is like to be in a room where nothing holds at all, and to have that be somebody else’s doing.

## A Place Somebody Lets You Have

That is the difference and it is four sentences long and she said them out loud once, standing up, at the fourth hour, in the ordinary voice, to a room.

A place you can hold holds because of something you do in it, and a place you cannot hold stops holding the moment you stop, and a place that somebody lets you have never held at all and never will, and the letting is the whole of what there is.

Then she stood there and listened to what she had just said, because it came out in the shape of a rule and she does not use that word of rooms.

And it is not a rule of rooms.

It is about a wall, and a man on the wrong side of it, and a room built against the side of that wall with a chair and a table and a jug and a folded blanket in it, all of which were put there by a person who is not going to be in it, for a person who has not been asked.

She has got nowhere else for thirty-seven days. That is why she is in it. It was not offered to her and it is not being kept from her and both of those are the same fact here.

## She Slept On The Boards On Purpose

On the sixteenth night she had lain down on them because a bed frame with nothing on it is not a thing to lie down on and there was a floor.

On the nineteenth she lay down on them because she had chosen the floor.

Same act. She got through four minutes of the difference before she stopped being able to see the point of it, which is the point: a choice about where to put your body does not make a room yours, and the twenty-second morning she would have been no worse off on the bed frame than on the boards and there is no frame to put anything on.

## The First Shame, And It Is A Small One

She found it on the twentieth at the second hour and it took her nine minutes to be certain of it and then it did not go away for two days.

She has been counting the nights.

Not the days — the nights. Sixteen of them, one at a time, and she has not missed one and she would not have missed one, and she has been doing it in the way a person counts the nights in a place they are working in, because she wanted to know how many were left.

She has counted days since she was six. She has counted coppers and holds and counts of breath and lines on a board she cannot take. She has never once in eight years counted how many nights were left in a place before she had to be out of it, and she did not notice herself doing it because in eight years she has never before been somewhere she was going to be required out of by a date.

That is the whole of the shame and it is small and it is the ugliest thing she has put her hand to in four months, and there is nobody in the world she could tell it to.

## The Fourth Hour

At the fourth hour she went out through the opening.

It costs her nothing. It has cost her nothing every one of the eleven times she went through it in three days before this and it will cost her nothing the next time or the one after that. There is no bar on it and no threshold and no door and the boards are the same boards on both sides of it.

She stood on her side of it, four hundred yards short of a gate, three hundred yards east of the flat with a bar on two posts in it, a hundred yards off a wall and a hundred yards from a hut with one small window and a light in it that has not gone out once since the ninth hour of the eighth of a month.

She did not knock.

She stood there for a quarter of an hour with her left hand on the jamb, which is cool at that hour, and she did not knock, and she did not say his name, and she wants it written down that she could have said his name and did not, and that it was a decision and not an inability, and that she is not going to be thanked for it by anybody, least of all herself.

Then she went back in and shut the door of the room and waited for the latch, because that is a thing she learned in the ninth hour of a month that has gone and it is the only piece of furniture in the world she has any feeling for.

## What The Thirty-Seven Days Are For

Here is what thirty-seven days are for, and she worked it out on the twentieth and said it out loud once, and it took her the whole of a morning to get to and four minutes to say.

They are for finding out what a person does with thirty-seven days when there is nothing on nine days of road that wants anything out of her.

And the second half of it, which she found on the twenty-first and which is why the first half took a whole morning, is this: there is a difference between thirty-seven days and every other stretch she has ever been alive through, and it is not that there is nowhere to go.

She has had nowhere to go since the second month of a year she could name. She had four coppers then and a shed and a rate of a sort and a body that would carry a bar for eleven miles, and she had nowhere to go and she managed, every day, for a year and a half.

What there is now is not nowhere to go. It is that the thirty-seven days are hers to fill and there is nothing to fill them with, and a person who has nothing to fill a day with does not rest in it. She rests badly. She has rested badly in better rooms than this one.

So they are going to be used. She is going to make them do something, and she is not going to be able to say afterward what.

There has not been one thing wanted of her since a woman of fifty shut her door on a lane and has not gone back to it. There is one man within a hundred yards and day one thousand five hundred and sixty-two less day one thousand two hundred and fifty-six is three hundred and six, and he asked her for nothing on any of the three hundred and six of them and she has seen him once through a window and did not see him at all.

So the thirty-seven days are going to go somewhere. She is not going to lie in them.

## The One Thing She Has Not Got

There is one thing left that she has not got, and it is not a place and it is not bread and it is not the road.

In eight years she has been entered four times by other people’s hands. She has been read to, rated, kept, refused and read back. She has stood in a room with the shutters up and said no out loud and had it read back to her before it went in, which was the best thing anybody has ever done with her.

Not one person in eight years has ever said to her: do this, I need it done, here is why it has to be you.

She worked that out on the twenty-first at the fourth hour, sitting on the boards with her back against a wall that is four feet high and does not go over her head, and it is the first time in four months that the thing she wants is not a thing she can go and fetch.

On the twenty-second she starts the work of it, and she is going to be eleven days at it, and she is going to do it on her own in a nine-foot room with a bar of a wall across the back of it and a man who is alive on the other side of it and not coming back.
