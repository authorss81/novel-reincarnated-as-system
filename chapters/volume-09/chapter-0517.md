# East With A Number In Her Hand

She went out of the lane at the second hour of the second of a month with a bar at the light end and a third of a loaf in a bag and a figure on a bench at a crossing eleven miles behind her, and she did not stop for five days.

## The Second Of A Month

The decision was made on a bench on the first and it is worth putting down exactly, because everything after it is walking.

She is going to go past a bar on two posts at the end of a made road she has measured twice, and she is going to find out what is on the far side of it, and she is going to say yes out loud, once, in the ordinary voice, to a man of nine hundred years who has already declined to answer her about it.

She is not going to be met half way. There is no arrangement and there is no date in anybody else’s book and nobody on nine days of road knows she has a figure on a bench in her head.

That is the whole of it and she said it out loud once, at the end of the lane, at the second hour, standing up, in the ordinary voice.

“I am going east for five days,” she said. “And I am not going to tell one living person where I am going, and I am not going to sleep in one place twice.”

She has not told anybody where she sleeps since the third of a month and she is not starting at a number.

## The Road She Has Walked

It is a made road and she has walked every yard of it in one direction or the other and there is nothing on any of it.

She went out past the shed at the end of the lane and did not look at it.

She went past the crossing with its painted board and its chain and the boy of seventeen, and he was on the chain and he did not ask her where she was going.

He has never once asked her anything. She has never once told him anything. There is not one other arrangement on nine days of road that has lasted two months without anybody being spoken to.

Then four miles of made road, then the long road, then a gate at the end of it, and a bar on two posts past the gate.

She did it in five days. That is better than she expected and she has said so to nobody.

## Not Correcting It

The woman of fifty was on the step of the shed at the end of the lane at the seventh hour on the second and Ning Yan went past and the woman came down off the step.

She did not ask anything.

“You have gone west four times in a month,” the woman said, “and east once, and you were four miles west of here this morning and now you are going out of the lane, and I am not going to be the one to say anything about it.”

“All right,” Ning Yan said.

“I offered you a room and a bolt on a west door in the second month and I offered it without asking and I have not taken it back,” the woman said. “That offer stands. It does not need an answer this month or any other month.”

“Thank you,” Ning Yan said, and she meant it, and she did not stop, and she is aware that she said thank you to one living person this week and has not said it to one in eight years except once.

The woman of fifty went back up the lane.

She had not asked. She had not said anything wrong. She had made the ordinary mistake out of the only shape there is, and she was correct, and she is the fifth of them.

## What The Ground Thinks

The man of fifty with four boards came up the long road behind her on the fifth at the ninth hour with his load, and he did not catch her up because he goes slower than she does.

He saw her going east.

He has been watching a girl with a bar since the ninth of a month because he is sure that something has come for her. He has been careful with her for twenty-six days. He told a stranger three things about her on the twenty-eighth of a month that has gone and could not carry it by himself any more.

And now she is going east.

She walked three hundred yards past him before she let herself turn round, which took four seconds and was the hardest four seconds of the five days and she has said so.

He had got his bar down across his shoulder and he was standing in the middle of the made road in the middle of eleven miles with four boards on it, looking at her back.

And she understood, at three hundred yards, standing up on a made road in daylight, what would happen if she turned round and said one word to him.

He would not ask a question. He would simply understand that something had come for her and that she was walking away from it.

And that would be the last thing anybody on nine days of road believed about her, and it would be the truest thing anybody had said about her in a month, and she could never take it back.

## A Number She Cannot Argue With

That is what she could not fix, and it stood in front of her for five days on the same two feet, and it is why she did not correct it.

She cannot correct the misreading. Correcting it means the page. The page is a shed at the end of a lane and a date and forty words in a nine-hundred-year-old hand, and there is nowhere on nine days of road she can put it.

And a number of one is a number she cannot argue with.

Forty-one lines a month she argued with for two years and took none of them. A rate behind a counter she argued with four times in eight years and never once got a change. A bench outside a store she stood at two hundred times and never once asked for anything, and was right not to.

A number of one is different. It is one stroke standing on its own foot with a flag on the top of it, and it is in a hand nine hundred years old, and it has no heading and no date and no second line, and there is not one argument in the world that can be made against a figure.

She has been arguing with figures for eight years. She has never once won.

And she is not going to win this one either, because the only thing she has to put next to it is the thing she said on a bench on the third of a month in daylight, in her own words, once, with a boy of seventeen forty feet off.

She named a place to a man with a book and it was the price of forty words and she knew it was the price at the time.

This is a bigger price and it is the same shape and she has already done it once.

## The Body On The Road

It is worth putting down what she was walking on, because she kept it and because it is the reason five days took five days.

Thirty-three days since she ate on the third of a month that has gone, and four times in that time. A third of a loaf on the second of this month, standing up, by a ditch at the fourth mile.

Her right arm was open on two days out of the five and she had not written down which two. Her fourth count on flat ground took four seconds and it took nine on the fourth day and she sat down twice for that.

Eight of eleven holds, and she went through them standing in a ditch at the fourth mile on the fifth and got eight, and she tried the ninth and put it down.

The open place behind her fourth rib had not changed since the eighteenth of a month in a year she could name and it is what the thing comes out of and it does not close.

She is eighteen years old and she is thinner than at the start of the month and she walked eleven miles on the sixth without any of it in her and she is not going to write down what that is, because whatever it is, it is a number of one as well.

## The Fear Named Once

She said it out loud, once, on the sixth of a month, at the eighth hour, sitting down on the flat four miles short of the gate with her bag beside her, in the ordinary voice, with nothing within nine hundred yards.

She was not careful and she was not brave and she was not calm and she was not sure.

She was frightened.

That is the sentence and she put it down whole and she has not wrapped anything round it, and no person has ever said a sentence like that out loud in the open in eight years.

She is frightened of a figure on a bench.

She is frightened because she does not know whose one it is, and she is going to have to say yes to it standing up.

And she is frightened because she has spent four months making sure that nothing on this ground could find her, and she did it on purpose, and it worked, and she is now going east to spend the rest of it on a man who keeps a book and will not tell her what she has agreed to.

Then she set it down beside the decision, which is the only place she has ever been able to put it, and went on walking.

## The Deadline

She made one on the second and it was a real one and she had put it to herself and not to anybody.

The eighth of this month. She will be standing in front of a bar on two posts at the fourth hour of the eighth, on the far side of a chain on a gate, with a bar on her shoulder.

She has three days after the seventh to cross eleven miles of made road and a gate and four hundred yards of flat, on four fingers and a thumb, an arm open four days in five, and no bread in her since the second of this month.

She got there at the second hour of the eighth, having slept once in six nights in a ditch at the fourth mile, and she was two hours early.

## What Is Past The Bar

She stood on the far side of the chain at the eighth hour of the seventh and looked at it from two hundred yards.

A bar on two posts. No chain on it. No cup. No boy.

And past it, three hundred yards east, out in the flat, a wall set in the ground.

She could see it because the light was behind her and the wall is four feet high and it is pale stone and it runs north and south further than she could see in the half hour she stood there looking at it.

And behind the wall, about a hundred yards east of it, a hut with a low roof and one small window, and there was a light in the window.

She stood on a made road at the eighth hour of the seventh of a month with a figure in her head and a bar on her shoulder and she looked at a light in a window behind a wall she had never been to, and there is nobody on nine days of road who could have told her what was there.

It is the only man who has ever tried to tell her what a door is.
