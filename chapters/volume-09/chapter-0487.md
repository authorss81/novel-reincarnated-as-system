# The Door That Does Not Shut

She went to bed on the seventeenth of the month at the ninth hour with a day of bread on the board and the bar strapped across the head end and the door standing where it stands, and she lay down in her clothes because it is easier than taking them off and she has done it more nights than she has counted.

The wind came in under the door at a foot a second. She had not measured it since a winter two years back and she did not measure it tonight either, and the difference between measuring a thing and knowing a thing is four seconds and she has been saving them.

She was asleep by the tenth hour.

## Woken

What woke her was the bar.

Not a person. The strap had gone slack on the loop over about an hour of wind and the bar had rolled four inches down the boards and it was lying against her hip and the noise of a bar rolling four inches on boards is not much, and it was enough.

She came up out of it four inches off the floor with her right arm open and her left hand going for the bar’s light end, and her breath went to three counts and stopped, the way it does when she is caught.

Then it came. Then the fourth.

Then she had her hand on the bar and she had put it back across the head end and pulled the strap up and found the post and pulled it again, hard, the way she does four times a month, and it held, because it has held for four months.

And then she lay there on her back in the cold with her heart going and nine seconds to get her breath back to four, and that is the whole of what the door does to her.

## The Fourth Count

She lay there and waited for it to settle, which takes nine seconds standing and two minutes lying down.

That is the thing about the open place. Standing up it is a place behind her fourth rib that is open about the size of a coin. Lying down in a coat on boards with the wind coming in under the door, it is not that size. It is the whole of the back of her ribs and it takes the fourth count and she has to go and get it.

She got it forty times before the light.

She was not hungry, which is a thing that has been done to her and is wearing off, and she was cold, which is real, and she had one arm at half and four fingers and a thumb and a bar that she cannot hold at the heavy end and she is eighteen years old.

She lay in the middle of the night and worked out that if this had happened at a floor nine days of road west she would have been on a bench, and if it had happened at the lane she would have been in a room with a bench at the end of it, and there is nobody within eleven miles of this shed at the second hour of the eighteenth of a month and there has not been for two years.

She was not frightened of the wind.

She was frightened of the second time.

That is what a night in a shed with a door that does not shut is, and she has had four hundred of them and this is the first one she has been able to name.

## What She Learned In A Room With A Lamp

She lay on the boards and looked at the light coming up under the door, an inch of it, a bar of it across the floor of the shed, and she thought about a room she was in for two minutes two months back.

Shutters up. Forty people at tables with a lamp on every one of them. A man of forty at the end of the counter reading a column out loud in his own sentence, and a line in it that was about her, and the date against it, and the room, and her name in the best book on nine days of road afterwards because she had asked for it.

That is what it is to be read to.

She has never been able to put that into a sentence that does not sound like a complaint and she is not going to try tonight. Here it is in one sentence, and it took her two years to get it out and it is not even the whole of it.

Somebody stands in a room and reads a thing out loud about you, in daylight, and you are the only person in it who has not got a copy, and whatever you say next is the thing that goes in.

That is it.

That is what she learned in a room with a lamp in it, and it is the reason there is a piece of paper against her ribs that she has carried for four years and has never once opened.

## The Plan

She said it out loud on the boards at the fourth hour of the eighteenth, with the light coming in under the door and the bar across her head end and the strap holding, and she said it once because she says everything out loud exactly once.

“I am not going to be read to again.”

And then she lay there for a while and let that be a feeling for nine seconds, because it had been a sentence for four years and on the eighteenth of a month it turned into a plan.

The plan took a minute and it was this.

She was going to open it. Alone. In this shed. With the door shut, on purpose, not on the way out and not on the way in — shut, with the bar across it and the strap bound, and herself inside.

And she was not going to tell anybody.

That is the part she lay there and looked at for a long time. She was not going to tell a woman at a store who has been good to her for two years and has never once asked her name. She was not going to tell a man on a floor who asks her about a rate four times a year. She was not going to put a column up a road, and she was not going to write it on a board in a shed.

And she was not going to tell him.

The three days were not a plan for the paper. The three days were for her hands, and she said that too, once, at the fourth hour, out loud, on the boards.

She opened her right hand and looked at it in the dark. Four fingers and a thumb and a hand that has not closed on anything given to her since a man of forty swept four coppers back across a counter eight months ago.

It has shaken when she is cold. It shook through the whole of that night and most of the next two days, and she could not do anything about it, and she was not going to be able to do anything about it at the fourth hour of the twenty-second either.

She had no second pair of hands. There was none on this ground and there had not been for sixty-nine days and the whole of the plan depended on four fingers and a thumb that shook when she was cold.

She worked that out on the eighteenth and she did not change the plan.

## The Reason She Gave Him

She gave the reason to the boards at the fifth hour and it came out much shorter than she expected.

“He has nothing to spend,” she said. “He has had nothing for one hundred and twenty-five days and there is nothing to answer with, and I am not going to spend a thing on a piece of paper that says Foundation the first stage on the front of it.”

And that was true and it was about half of it.

The other half was this, and she said that too, because she says everything out loud exactly once.

“If I tell him, then it is a thing we are both in,” she said. “And the last thing that was read to me in this world, somebody read to me because it was a thing we were both in.”

She lay there.

She did not say the rest of it, which was that she would rather find out what is written on a piece of paper by herself, alone, with four fingers and a thumb, at the fourth hour of the morning, than have it come to her out of a room with anybody in it. She was not going to say that part out loud at all.

And she lay there a while longer with the wind coming in under the door, and she worked out one more thing about the three days in front of her, which is that she was going to spend them doing the ordinary part first.

She washed in the trough. She ate the last of the bread on the twenty-first and nothing on the morning of the twenty-second, because she was not going to have her hands full of food at the fourth hour of the twenty-second.

She walked to the trough road and back and she did not go past the store.

And on the twenty-first at the ninth hour she took the bar and got it down and squared it and put it against the wall where it goes, so that her hands would be free.

## The Twenty-Second

She got up at the sixth hour and she did the ring and she drank and she had a third of a loaf and nine hours of daylight in front of her.

And then she did the arithmetic of the days, because that is what she does now instead of the other thing, and it came to one hundred and twenty-five at the sixth hour of the eighteenth, and she checked it twice.

One hundred and twenty-five days since he put it down and did not pick it up.

Four days. On the twenty-second at the fourth hour she would shut the door.

She had three days of daylight and one day of bread and a shed with a gap under the door a finger thick, and she had been in this shed for two years and she had never once done anything with the door.

On the twenty-second she would do the whole of it.
