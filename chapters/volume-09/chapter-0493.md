# Where Staying Stops Counting

She did not eat on the sixteenth, the seventeenth or the eighteenth, and she drank twice from her own skin and once off the trough road, and she was not doing it as a discipline. She was doing it because there was nothing to eat and she had run out of anything else to put in her hands for four days.

On the nineteenth of the month she got up at the fourth hour.

It was the coldest night of the run of them. The wind came up under the door at a foot a second, which is what it always does, and she had the bar under the door with the light end of it, and the strap round the ring holding, and she had put the bar down and squared it four hours before because she wanted her hands free.

She had the clay. She had the dish. She had four fingers’ worth of a thumb and nine hours of dark in front of her.

## What Was Holding It

She worked the lump for forty minutes and centred it and set it in the dish and put her left hand flat on the rim to hold it, and it did the thing it has done four hundred times in two years and it moved a very small amount.

Then she asked the question.

She said it out loud, in the ordinary voice, on the boards, at the fourth hour of the nineteenth of a month, alone, with the door shut and a bar under it.

“What is holding this dish.”

That is the whole of it. It is a ninth of a finger’s width, the whole of it, and it is four hundred times she has done this in two years, and it is the first question out of her mouth in eight years that has not been about stone, or a chain, or a length of paving, or a man of thirty-four with a leg gone.

The reach went out and took the dish.

Not a finger’s width. It did not move the clay. It went to the dish itself, the thing her left hand was on, and it took the weight of it out of her hand, and she felt it go, and her left hand came up off the rim about an inch and stayed there in the air because she had not told it to move.

A second pair of hands. Made out of the only thing in the world that has ever done work for her, and it cost her nothing, and it was hers, and it had been sitting in her for eight years waiting to be asked about her.

She put her left hand back down on the rim on purpose and it was holding nothing at all.

## What Came Up Through Her

And the ring came up.

Not a finger’s width. It came up three inches off the boards of a shed four miles east of a mill whose wheel has not turned since before she came, and it came the way the ninth stage came in a long room with the shutters up, which is to say that it did not ask her and she could not have stopped it and she was not consulted.

She did not agree to it. She wants that written down somewhere in her own hearing, because she has only done this once in her life and it was two months back in a room with forty people in it and she stood at the end of a counter and said no out loud in the ordinary voice and meant it, and the no went into the air of the room and did nothing.

She said no again. Out loud, on the boards, at the fifth hour of the nineteenth of a month, four fingers and a thumb, no food in her since the fifteenth.

It did not go anywhere. Nothing in the world failed. No door opened, no book shut, no person in a room looked up, because there was not one person in the room.

It came up out of the ground under her feet the way everything has ever come up out of the ground under her feet, and it took nine seconds, and it was not a trial and there was nothing in the air and nothing in the shed but her and the clay and the bar under the door.

## The One Sentence

And it said the one sentence, into the ground, the way it has said ten things in eight years, and it did not say anything after it, and it has never once explained or advised or begun.

[Ning Yan, past the line, servant-born, under no ceiling any county holds. Severance, stage one, is held.]

Then nothing.

And she stood on the boards in the cold with a dish in the air and did not move for four minutes, and then she put her left hand down and the dish came down three inches and stood on the clay and was steady.

She listened for nine minutes afterwards for a second sentence, because that is what she has done every time one of these has ever arrived, and there was no second sentence.

Ten sentences in eight years and it has never once said a second thing, and she has always found that frightening, and on the nineteenth of a month she stood in a shed with a bar under the door and found that she did not.

It said the thing and it stopped. Whatever is on the other side of it was never going to be announced to her, and it never has been, and the only reason it ever frightened her was that she expected a person to be told.

There was nobody to tell.

## The Cost

She did not find out what it had cost for two hours, and when she did it was not dramatic and it was not in her body.

She sat on the boards with the ring at three inches and the dish on it and the wind coming under the door, and she put her hand flat on the boards, the way she does, and worked out what had changed.

Nothing in the world had changed. She still had four fingers and a thumb. Her right arm was open at the side and it stayed open. She had eight of eleven holds and she went through them standing up and got eight.

It was the boards.

She had been in this shed two years. She had barred the door with her own bar and worked the clay and slept nine hours in it and put her hand on it and begun, somewhere between the fifth and the sixth of the month, to think of it as a place where she was.

That was over.

She tried it three times on the morning of the nineteenth, out loud, by putting her hand on the boards and saying “this is mine” in the ordinary voice, and the reach did not answer it, and that was the finding.

She could not keep a place by staying in it.

That is the whole of what it cost. Not a finger, not a hold, not a count of breath. A shed she has slept in for two years stops being hers for having slept in it. Staying stops counting. She can lie down anywhere and no lying down holds.

She said that out loud once, on the boards, at the seventh hour of the nineteenth of a month, and then she did not say it again for a long time.

## The Morning After

She slept in it that night anyway.

That is the part she has not been able to make tidy since. She lay down on the boards about the eleventh hour with a bar under the door and the dish on the clay and her clothes on, and she was tired in a way that goes past tired into something else, and she slept for six hours.

And she woke on the twentieth of the month at the fourth hour and the light was coming in under the door in the bar of it that always comes in, and the boards were cold and the shed was a shed.

It was not hers.

She lay there and let that be a thing for nine minutes, and she did not weep, and she wants it written down that she did not weep, because she has wept twice since she was fourteen and both of those were for a person and this is not a person.

What she did instead was get up and do the ring, and put a lump in it, and drink, and pack the bar, because there was nothing in the shed but the bar and the ring and the boards and a piece of paper against her ribs, and she had the trough road six miles away and four hours of daylight.

And that is the whole of what she had at the twentieth of the month at the seventh hour, standing in a doorway four miles east of a mill whose wheel has not turned since before she came, at the top of a realm, with no money and no wages and no bread and no season and a bar she cannot hold at the heavy end.

Four fingers and a thumb. The right arm open half the time. Eight of eleven holds. Short breath at four counts everywhere, including inside a loaded reach. A small open place low behind her fourth rib that came open on the eighteenth of a month two months back and is not going to close. A bar with a strap she bound out of her own coat lining. A roof cord she tied herself.

She was eighteen years old.

And from that morning she was not going to be in that shed again, and there was not one person on nine days of road who would ever know that she had been in it for two years, and she had opened the only door in the world on the twenty-second of a month with a bar under the door and four fingers and a thumb, alone.

She went up the lane at the seventh hour with the bar on her shoulder and she did not look back at it, because she is a person who uses a place until it stops being useful and then goes, and she had got there first.
