# Four Sheets And A Lock

She got the four sheets out of her coat at the sixth hour of the eleventh of the month beginning 2,054 and put them on the bench under the window, and the boy stopped copying and came and stood at the other end of the bench with his hands behind his back, the way he stands when there is a sheet on the bench.

They were the four sheets of rice paper folded four times along four old folds, and the folds were soft as cloth now, and the fair hand on them was the hand of a man of sixty-seven who had written them at a bench at the sixth hour in four days with a boy of sixteen four feet away.

She did not open them. She squared them against the edge of the bench in the order she had taken them out, which was fourth sheet, first sheet, second sheet, third sheet, and that order had been in her coat since the twenty-seventh of the month beginning 2,024.

## Four And Seven

She counted them anyway.

She said, out loud, standing at the bench in daylight with a boy of sixteen in the room: “Four sheets. Seven sentences. One hand.”

She said, “I have counted them nine times in ten days and I counted them again twice on the road coming up the hill this morning and I did not read one word of them, and I am going to tell you why I counted them, because I have not worked out why I counted them.”

The boy said, “Because you wanted to know they were still four.”

She said, “Because they are not mine and every day they are in my coat they are a thing I am carrying that belongs to a man who is three feet from the back wall of this building, and a count is the only way I have ever had of holding a thing that is not mine without taking it.”

She said, “And it is four. It has been four every time.”

He said, “You read them out loud on the twenty-eighth.”

She said, “I read them out loud once, in this room, in daylight, with you in it and you had not been asked, and that was the correct thing to do and it is why I have been able to carry them for thirteen days.”

## The Reason She Took Them Out

Then she said, “I am going up past this floor before this month is out.”

He said, “I know. You have told me once and I am not going to ask you how many times.”

She said, “And I am not taking those up.”

He said, “Why not.”

She said, “Because I have not got a word for what is up there and I have been up a hill all day with a sentence that has no object in it, and I am not going to carry a dead man’s handwriting into a room I have never seen on the strength of a sentence with nothing in it.”

She said, “If something comes up those stairs that I have not got words for, then I want the four sentences to be in a drawer in this building and not in my coat, because a person who has been handed another person’s writing cannot be shown to have used it, and I would rather it was in a drawer with a lock on it that has the name of a man on it.”

The boy said, “The drawer does not have a name on it.”

She said, “No. It has his key.”

## The Hand On The Drawer

She carried them to the far corner under the shelves and she did not look at one of them and she put them in in the same order and she shut the drawer and turned the key and put the key in the front of her bag where it has been since the twentieth of the month beginning 2,024.

And then she stood in the middle of the floor of the copying room with her hand still flat on the top of the drawer and found out what she had actually done, which was that she had for the first time in ten years put a thing down in a building instead of on her own person, and that the building had a lamp in it that ought not to be burning and a boy of sixteen in it who had not asked her for anything.

She said, out loud, in the room, standing:

“For ten years I have carried two things against my ribs and I have not called either of them anything, and I have put one of them back in a locked drawer in a building with no keeper in it and I gave the key to nobody and I am telling you it is there because a boy of sixteen should not have to find that out from an empty drawer.”

The boy said, “I am not going to open it.”

She said, “I know you are not. I am telling you so that if you ever do, you will know that a person told you, and you will be able to say so.”

He said, “What do you want me to say if it comes to that.”

She said, “Say that a woman of nineteen brought it back herself and locked it and told me where it was. That is all. Do not say a name and do not say what is on it and do not say why.”

He said, “That is three things and you have asked me for three things at once.”

She stopped.

She had said one at a time, in daylight, standing, since the fourteenth of the month beginning 2,024, and she had just handed a boy of three separate instructions in the ordinary voice, and the boy had heard it, and he was right.

She said, “You are right. That is three. Take the first.”

He said, “Say that a woman of nineteen brought it back herself.”

She said, “Yes.”

## What He Offers And What She Does With It

He said, “I will come up with you.”

She said, “No.”

He said, “You have told me once and you are not going up it four times alone.”

She said, “I am going up past this floor on my own and I want to be on my own when I get there, and that is not a rule about you, it is a rule about what I have come here to find out.”

He said, “Then I will stand at the top of the third landing.”

She said, “You will not.”

He said, “Why not.”

She said, “Because that is a place I could stop at, and if I can stop at the third landing then I will stop at the third landing, and I have been stopping at landings since I was fourteen years old and every one of them was a reason to turn round and go down.”

He said, “All right.”

She said, “I am not sending you away. I will be on this floor when I come down, and if you hear something from up there that sounds like two people then you come up, and if you do not hear that then you stay where you are, and either one of those is a correct thing to do and I am telling you both of them because I have learned that in this life I am bad at telling a person one thing when they are about to do something for me.”

He said, “That is two things.”

She said, “That is one thing with a second half on it. I am not going to pretend I did not hear you say so.”

## The Boy’s Own Column

He went back to the rack and she stayed at the bench, and after a while, without turning round, he said:

“There is a column in the register that has my days in it and not my name.”

She said, “Say it again.”

He said, “The column goes down four flights on a boy’s arm every three months. My days are in it. My name is not in it anywhere. I have looked in it twice and it is not there and I did not take it out with a knife, I have read it standing up, in daylight, which is the only way I have been taught to read a thing that is not mine.”

She said, “Why have you not asked.”

He said, “Because of what you said on the twenty-eighth about a thing walled behind four county copies.”

She said, “That was not about you.”

He said, “It was about a column that cannot be found, and I am a column that cannot be found, and the difference is that he wrote five lines in it and I have not written one.”

She stood at the end of a bench and listened to a boy of sixteen put himself next to a dead clerk’s private column in a tower four miles up a mountain, and she did not tell him that a person cannot be given anything and had never been able to be, because he had got there on his own in two years without a word of it from her, and because being told would have taken it off him.

She said instead, “When the bundle goes down on the first, stand in the yard and watch the carter take it, and if you want to know what is at the bottom of it then that is the one time in a quarter that you will find out, and you will find out in daylight standing up and not with a knife.”

He said, “That is advice and not a promise.”

She said, “It is advice. I do not have anything that is a promise and I have not had one in ten years.”

He said, “Then I will do it as advice.”

## What She Is Going Up There For

She got the book out of the other side of her coat and put it on the bench, and it is a book of eight leaves made out of a blanket, and the boy has seen it once, on the twenty-eighth.

She opened it on the fourth leaf. The fifth was blank. The sixth was blank. The seventh was blank. The eighth was blank.

She said, “There. That is what I have got in the world that has not got another holder of it, and it is five blank leaves and a stub of a nib and a cake of ink I opened on the seventh of the month beginning 1,874 and have never let dry.”

She said, “And a hand that cannot write a false line, which is not a gift. I have wanted that line three times in two days and it does not come, and it will not come for a dead man either and it is not going to come for me at the bottom of this stair.”

She shut the book and put it back against her ribs on the side where the four sheets had been, and it was lighter on that side now and she was aware of the weight of it in a way she had not been aware for ten years.

She said, “I have put one of the two things down in a building and I have got the other one against my ribs and five of its eight leaves are blank, and I am going up four flights before the month is out and I am going to find out what is in a room.”

She said, “If there is nothing in it then I have put a man’s handwriting in a drawer for nothing and that is the cost of the day and I will pay it and I will not do it twice.”
