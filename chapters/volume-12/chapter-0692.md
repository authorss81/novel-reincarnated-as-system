# The Slate Goes East

The third first of a month came at the first hour of the first of the month beginning 2,144 and she was two miles west of a ground with two hundred people in it, at the north end of it, standing, in daylight, with a bar on the ground at her feet.

She has been at the frame for one first of a month in nine years and she was at it on the first of the month beginning 2,084 and she counted eleven people going past in an hour and ten minutes and not one of them looked at the grit.

She was not at it for the first of the month beginning 2,114 either. That is the second time and it is the one she will be asked about, and she does not know yet who will ask.

## What A First Does

She stood there and put the whole of it down out loud, because that is the only way a thing gets to exist in a place where two hundred people can hear it and one of them is keeping a record.

A man of forty-nine comes on the twenty-fourth with a rag and a stick of white chalk and writes four columns. A name goes in one column and the days of a month go in the next. On the first hour of the first, the slate comes out of the frame and goes on a cart east, and the fifth comes off the days before it goes up, and it does not say so on the board, and it does not come off the name.

And it comes back down before the twenty-fourth, and on the twenty-fourth somebody wipes it.

She said, at the first hour, to the board and the barrows and the two hundred rows of stones:

“A month since the first of the month beginning 2,114 and I have not been at either end of it, and the slate has been up for eight days with my name on it and thirty of my days in beside it, and I have been two days of road from it.”

She said, “And it does not care, and I have known that for nine years, and it is the first time it has cost me anything to know it.”

Then she did the other half of it, which is what she has always done.

She went out of the north end of that ground at the first hour and down to a made road two miles west of it and stood in the ditch at the shoulder of it at the second hour, with the bar laid along the ground in the grass, and watched the carts go east.

She did it for about an hour and forty minutes. She counted nine carts and two carts with a load of stone on them and a man on foot going east at a pace she timed, and one cart stopped at a gate four hundred yards off and came back, and every one of them went east and not one of them went west.

A made road two miles west of a ground with two hundred people in it has four carts on it a day. She counted that in the month beginning 2,114 and she counted it again in the month beginning 2,144 from the same ditch and it was four again, and there is no hand in nine days of road that keeps that figure and neither of them is a keeper of anything.

And not one of those nine carts was the cart that takes a slate east at the first hour, because she watched it go and she knows what it looks like from the eleventh, and it did not come past that road at all.

She had it wrong in her head and she worked out where the wrong was standing in a ditch in nine seconds. The slate does not come this way. A slate from the turn goes east along a store road that is thirteen miles from the wall, and that store road is not the one she is standing on, and she is two days of road east of the wall. The cart that took her name off that frame at the first hour went into a county she has not been in.

## The Cart At The Second Hour

At the second hour a cart came east along the made road and it did not go past the ground the way the four carts a day go past.

It stopped at the north end of the rows.

The driver was a man of forty with a rope over his shoulder and he had a flat stone on the seat beside him. He did not get down. He put the stone down on the edge of the frame, and off the stone a strip of pale paper nine inches long and two across, and he put his hand out flat and said one word at her and kept the hand out.

She gave him the thing he wanted, which was nothing, and she took the paper, and the cart went on east at a walk and did not come back for two hours.

The man was forty. He had a rope over his left shoulder and the hands of it were worn shiny in two places, and he had done that run a long time, and he did not get down and he did not ask her a single question and he did not look at the strip once while she was looking at it.

## The Strip

She stood at the frame with it in her hand and did not open it, because there was nothing to open. It was folded once in the middle and the fold had been opened and folded again, and the creases were soft.

She unfolded it. There was nothing written on it.

Not a line. Not a number. Not a name, and not a date, and not a place. A strip of paper nine inches long with a square in the middle of it the size of the flat of a hand, and the square was darker than the rest of the paper and it was not wet.

She turned it over. The back was clean too.

She held it up at arm’s length in daylight and looked at it for the length of a count of thirty, and a cart came off the made road and went by and did not stop, and a woman of forty with a barrow asked her whether she was going to stand there all morning.

She said, “I am going to stand here a bit longer.”

The woman said, “There is a queue at you most afternoons.”

She said, “There is not today.”

And the woman of forty went away with her barrow and did not ask one more question about the paper, which is the correct thing to do and cost her something anyway, because there has not been a person on that ground in nine years and the whole of the last five days has been a woman with a bar standing at a frame doing nothing at all.

## The Man With The Rope

She asked him one question, standing, in daylight, at the north end of the rows, to a man of forty with a cart and a rope over his shoulder.

She said, “Where have you been this morning.”

He said, “Up the hill and down. Lowwater.”

She said, “And before that.”

He said, “And before that I was at a turn, and I put a slate on a cart and I brought it back, and that is the job.”

She said, “How long is the run.”

He said, “Up is four days and down is two and a half and I do it when there is stone or paper or a thing that has to be where it is going. Today it was a stone and a paper.”

She said, “A paper that came down off the top of that hill.”

He said, “I did not see where it came from.”

She said, “And you did not ask.”

He said, “I did not ask.”

He said it in the ordinary voice and he did not ask her a single question and he did not look at the strip, and she stood there and understood that he had looked at the strip and had decided that it was not his business, which is the correct thing to do and cost him something anyway.

## The Decision

And then she said the thing out loud, which is a thing she has never said out loud in nine years of boards.

She said, standing, in daylight, at the ninth hour of the first of the month beginning 2,144, at the north end of two hundred rows of stones, with a strip of paper in her hand:

“The first of the month beginning 2,174 is thirty days off. At the first hour of it I will be at the frame, and I will be there before the light, and the only reason I can say that is that I have known for twenty-four hours that I was going to say it, which is the first time in eleven years that I have known a thing a day in advance.”

She said, “And it is two days of road back and the arm is open four days in five and there is no strap on the bar, and I have not worked out yet how many counts a mile costs with no strap and one good hand.”

She put the strip into the front of her bag, on top of the dish and the clay and the key, and it lay flat there, and it was lighter than paper that size has any business being.

## The Evening Of It

The four men put their strokes in at the seventh hour, one each, the way they have done for nine years, and she was at the frame and she counted them go in because she cannot be in a room with a number in it and not count it.

Four, on a first of a month, which is an ordinary night. Nine years of that is thirteen thousand lines on a board at the north end of a ground of two hundred people, and she has read the one that is hers four hundred times and she has never once read one of the other thirteen thousand because there is nothing on them.

A man of twenty-five came and looked at the board while she was standing there and said, “That one is yours.”

She said, “It is.”

He said, “I could not read it when you wrote it.”

She said, “I can read yours.”

And he went away, and she stood at a frame with nine inches of blank paper in the front of her bag and four strokes a night going in beside a line in her own hand, and she worked out at the ninth hour standing in the dark that she was going to be two days of road west in thirty days and that she had not worked out one single thing about how yet.

That is a thing she has not done in nine days of road and she noticed it and did not know what to do about it.
