# The Name In The Book

She went into the building at the fourth hour of the tenth of the month beginning 2,084 and there were about a hundred people at tables in it and a lamp on every one of them, and the sun was on the floor through the shutters, and none of it was strange.

She had been in it four years back and had sat in it with a sheet in front of her and had said no to it out loud, and the whole of the four years since had been the road afterwards.

## Through The Rows Again

She did not go straight there. She went back to the rows first, at the second hour, because she had stood at a board there two days before and had kept a figure in her head in daylight in front of two hundred people, and she had been walking away from it ever since, and she is not a person who walks away from a thing like that and then walks into a room.

Two hundred people and a stone at the head of each row and a board at the north end with a stroke and a number under it for four men.

Two days after she stood at that board she had a figure in her head and did not say it, and she had thought about it every mile of the two since, and it was still the right decision and she was still carrying it and she is not going to put it down.

She went down the second row.

## Third Row

He was about forty feet in, in the third row, doing what the four of them do.

He has hands like their father’s and he is nineteen and he has carried stones for four men for nine years and he has not said her name to her since a road five miles west of a mill nine years ago, and he did not say it now.

She stopped.

He looked up for about two seconds and went back to the stone, and then he said it, in the ordinary voice, to the ground rather than to her:

“In the sixth year there were six on that board. In the second year before that there were two.”

She said, “Six.”

He said, “And now there are four, and it has been four since the eleventh year.”

She said, “So the board has changed twice.”

He said, “That is what I am telling you.”

Then he carried his stone four paces and put it down at the head of the row in the place it goes, which she watched him do, and he did not say one more word and did not look up again.

She stood in the second row for a count of twenty afterwards, in daylight, with two hundred people about ten feet apart, and worked out three things and they all came to the same place.

The board does not list people. The board lists work, and four men’s work has been four lines for eight years, and it was two lines and then six and that is a thing that happened to a piece of wood, and it was written up by somebody.

She has never once in nine years asked who writes on the board at the north end. It has never occurred to her that a person writes on it, because a board is a thing that is written on.

Then she went out at the west end and did not look back at it, and got the bar up off the ground in a count of twenty-two, and went east.

## Forty Feet Of Lane

The lane is about ninety yards. There is a gate at the end of it with a man at it who looked at her bar and at her hand and at her face, in that order, and then looked at nothing in particular, and she went through.

Inside the lane there is a yard. The yard is swept. There is a low wall round three sides of it and it is open to the lane on the fourth, and the open side is the side the carts come in on, and there are four carts in it at the fourth hour with bundles under tarpaulins.

She has seen a bundle go up a hill four times in thirty-one years in the hands of a carter of sixty-four and she stood in the yard of a tower and listened to him say where it went, and she looked at the four carts in that yard for nine seconds and worked out the whole of the rest of this.

A quarter goes up on the first of a month on a clerk’s arm. It comes down and it is unloaded into a yard. From the yard it goes into a room.

That is not a discovery. That is four carts under four tarpaulins at the fourth hour on a made road and it is the first time she has seen the other end of a stair.

## The Counter

The building is one storey. The roof sits low. It is a hundred and forty feet front. The windows stand in a row and every one holds a lamp.

The floor is board, swept, the boards fitted close. There is no hall in it and nothing in it that is called a hall. One building, one counter, one book, one rate, one rule.

The room runs the length of it. The counter runs down the middle with a passage behind it, and tables run down the room on the other side of the counter with a lamp on every one, and a hundred people are at them reading, and the sound is paper and breath and nothing else.

She has not been in a room with a hundred people in it since she was a child.

She stood four feet back from the counter for about the length of twenty counts and did not go in any further, and a man with a brush was squaring sheets at a desk and a woman of sixty-three at the far end had her hands behind her back and was reading and had not got any smaller than she was five years ago.

She looked at the rate board behind the counter. It was repainted. The paint was fresh where hands had not touched and worn where people brushed past.

Two coppers a page.

A second rate by the difficulty of the hand.

A third rate at twice the first, for a hand nobody in the building can read.

She had read that board four times, four years back, and she had been the reason the third line on it was ever needed, and she had stood in front of it with her hands behind her back for the better part of a minute and had put her own hand flat on the wood of the counter and taken it away.

## The Man With The Book

He was at the far end of the counter with the book under his arm. He is sixty-five and has been in this room since before she was born, and there are folders shelved behind him and the dates on the shelves run back forty-two years.

He looked up.

He did not look up the way a man looks up at a person who has come into a room. He looked up the way a man looks up when a thing he has been reading for a long time turns out to be standing in front of him, and then he said her name.

He said, “Ning Yan.”

She did not answer.

He did not ask her to.

## What Happened In The Next Two Minutes

Here is the whole of what happened in that room after a man said her name, and it took two minutes, and she has gone over it a great many times since:

Nobody else in the room looked up.

A man at a table near the door turned a page. A woman two tables along went on with what she had. The man with the brush squared another sheet. The woman of sixty-three at the end of the room did not turn her head, and Ning Yan has known what that is worth, because four years back she held a silence in a room for a full minute on purpose and it bought her one clear evening with everything counted true.

This one she did not hold. This one was given to her.

The Order stopped reading her. She bought it with her own usefulness at the fourth hour of a month four years back and it is the best thing that has happened to her in all that time, and she came through that gate this morning expecting a room that did not want anything from her, and she has got exactly what she paid for.

One man said her name, because he has the book, and the book has her name in it and the date and a line she read out loud to him herself, and he did not need to look at anything.

## What It Costs To Be Recognised

She worked it out standing four feet back from the counter at the fifth hour of the tenth of the month beginning 2,084, and it took her about nine seconds, and she has been wrong about most things in her life and she was not wrong about this one:

She has been recognised. Not by a person and not by a hand and not by a chalk on a slate and not by a cart and a piece of sacking. By a book, which looked up.

By a book. At the fourth hour of the fourth day of a walk, by a man who had no reason to look up, and it took ninety seconds, and there is not one thing in the world she can do about it, and it is the same as a name going on a board except that a board is a thing a person erases with a thumb.

She has been found, and she cannot go back to not being found, and she has known since the seventeenth of the month beginning 2,054 that she cannot, and she said out loud in a tower four miles up a mountain that knowing stops being a road back.

She stood there and she did not say one word, and the man with the book went back to his folders, and a hundred people went on reading, and nothing else happened for the rest of the hour.

## Why She Stayed

Here is the second half of it and it is the part she is going to be judged on.

She cannot be paid. Her hand does not close on a page-rate and it has not closed on one in four years and she is not going to get a room, because a room is the fourth line of a sheet whose fifth line she refused out loud in this building at the fourth hour of the third of a month, and no one here is permitted to give her the fourth without the fifth.

She can be given nothing. Not a rate, not a room, not a place at a table, not a word. There is no line in this building that does not begin on that sheet.

And she stayed.

She stayed because she was not asked to leave, and because she worked out inside about forty counts why, and it is this: a person who is not on the roll costs this room nothing. Not a lamp, not a lamp’s oil, not a sheet of paper, not a pair of hands. She can stand at that counter for a year and at the end of twelve months this building will not have spent one copper on her and will not be one copper the poorer, and that is the only reason she is allowed to be in it.

So she stood at the end of the lane outside at the ninth hour with the bar on the ground at her feet, and she said out loud, to the yard and the four carts and one man at a gate:

“I cannot be given anything and I cannot be paid and it has not been put to me that I should go, and I am going to be in this building tomorrow at the fourth hour, and if that is being kept then it is the first time in my life and I am going to find out what it costs before I decide what it is.”
