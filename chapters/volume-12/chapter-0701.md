# The Line She Had Not Seen

The light comes up on that frame last, and a woman of sixty who has sat on a bench for nine years told her so at the ninth hour of the twenty-ninth of the month beginning 2,144, and she was at the two posts at the last hour of the night and she believed her.

The first of the month beginning 2,174 came at the first hour, and the man of forty-nine was at the frame with a rag in his back pocket and the slate out of it and a stick of white chalk in his right hand, and he did not say good morning, because a man who does four columns a month does not have anything to say to a person standing in the road at the first hour.

She was six feet off, standing, in daylight, with the bar on the ground at her feet.

## Four Columns

He took the slate out of the frame and set it on the rail face up and wiped the frame where the slate sits, which is a thing no one has asked him to do and which he has done every month for nineteen years.

Then he wiped the slate from the left corner to the right corner with the flat of his thumb, in the time it takes a man to turn round twice, and she watched him do it and she had seen a carter of fifty do the same thing at the seventh hour on a road in the month beginning 2,054, and this is the whole of a board being maintained.

And then he turned it over.

Not to wipe it. He turned it over because the pencil side was the up side and the pencil side has never been wiped in nineteen years, and he does his two columns on the road side and then he puts his other two in and puts the slate back with the chalk side out.

She was six feet off and she saw it for nine seconds before he noticed her.

## The Third Column

There are four columns on that slate and she has read two of them for nine years.

The first column is a name and the second column is the days of a month beside it, in chalk, in a county hand, and he does four a month, one in every seven days of it, and it is the whole of his working life.

The other two columns are on the other side.

They are in pencil. The third column has a figure under every name, and every name in that slate for nineteen years has a figure under it, and the figures are not dates and they are not stones and they are not a rate.

They are days.

She stood at six feet in the light that had just come up over the hill and she looked down that column and she did not need to be told anything at all, because she has been reading columns since she was twelve years old and she read a column the way she reads a column, which is from the top and down, fast, and her mouth went open about half an inch and shut again.

It is one in twenty.

Not one name in twenty. Every name, every month, and the figure under each name is a fifth of a day of the days that name had in the column, added to what it had already had.

The figures in that column run from the top of nineteen years of slate to the bottom of it and they get bigger going down, the way they would get if they were the same figure accumulating, and the ones at the top are nine days and the ones at the bottom are a hundred and ten.

## Her Own Row

Her name is four lines from the bottom.

She found it and she read the figure under it in a count of four, and then she stood in the road at the first hour of the first of a month with a bar on the ground at her feet and did the sum out loud.

“Ninety-eight days. The twenty-fourth of the month beginning 2,054 is day two thousand and seventy-seven and this is day two thousand one hundred and seventy-four, and you count the first day as the first, which is how I count everything, and ninety-eight is what is under that name.”

She said, “Ninety-eight out of twenty is four and nine tenths of a day, and nine tenths of a day is twenty-one hours and thirty-six minutes.”

She said, “And that is what is written on a board at this frame in a county hand at the first hour of this morning, and I said a figure out loud in front of two hundred people on the fourteenth of the month beginning 2,144, and it was four days and one hour and twelve minutes, and it has been seventeen days since then.”

She said, “And seventeen days at one in twenty is twenty hours and twenty-four minutes, and four days and one hour and twelve minutes and twenty hours and twenty-four minutes is four days and twenty-one hours and thirty-six minutes, and it is on the board, and it is right.”

She stood there and found that the thing she had believed for nine years, which is that she was the only person in the world who could work this out, is a thing about nobody at all.

Every man of forty-nine in this county can work it out. He does it every month of his working life and he does not know he is doing it and he has never once put it in a column with a name on it.

## The Fourth Column

And the fourth column is names.

No figures under them and no strokes. Names, one under another, in pencil, in the same county hand, going down nineteen years of slate, and she read down that column the way she reads everything and her hand went out flat on the frame about a foot above the ground to keep herself from touching it.

There are a great many of them.

And she has never seen any of them, and she could not have seen them, and there is not one person in this county who could tell her what they are, because a slate is wiped with a thumb on the road side at the seventh hour by the first carter west and the other side faces the wall and has faced the wall since before this county had a cart on it.

She looked at the fourth column and worked out what a name in that column is, standing up, in daylight, six feet off a frame.

A name in that column is a name that came off that board.

That is the whole of it and it took her nine seconds and it is the first time in eleven hundred years of this county that anybody has seen the two halves of a board together.

## The Name She Knew

And she read down the fourth column until she got to the fourth one from the bottom, and she stopped, and she stood at a frame in the road at the second hour of the morning of the first of the month beginning 2,174 with her hand flat on the rail about a foot above the ground.

She has been walking past that bench for nine years.

The fourth name from the bottom of the fourth column is a name she knows, and it is not hers, and it is written in the county hand of a man of forty-nine in the middle of nineteen years of names, and it is the name of a woman of sixty who is sitting on a bench four hundred yards west of this frame right now, and who was the last name on this board for four years, and who told her about the light at the ninth hour of the twenty-ninth.

He finished his two columns and put the slate back with the chalk side out and put the rag in his back pocket and went east on foot four hundred yards to a bench, and he sat down on it beside her, and they were there together, and he did not say one word to her about it, and she has not worked out whether that was because he was being kind or because he does not talk.

She stood at the frame for a count of a hundred.

She had a figure in her head that no one in the world had ever been told and she had worked out where it came from and she had seen it in a county hand on a piece of slate in the open at six feet and it was right.

And she had a name in her head that she was going to walk past in a quarter of an hour and she was not going to be able to put it down.

## Three Things That Were Arithmetic

She did three things about it before she walked away from that frame, and all three of them were arithmetic, and none of them was about what to do.

The first was that she counted the fourth column. She read down it from the top of nineteen years of slate to the bottom and counted the names, and there are three hundred and one of them, and she counted twice and got three hundred and one both times.

The second was that she worked out what a column on that slate is. The man of forty-nine does four a month and he has done it for nineteen years, and four times twelve times nineteen is nine hundred and twelve, and there are nine hundred and twelve names that have been on that board since it was put there.

Three hundred and one out of nine hundred and twelve is very nearly a third, and not a fifth, and not a tenth, and she does not know what a third of a name is and she stood at a frame in the road at the second hour of the morning and worked that out twice and got the same thing both times.

The third was that she went and looked at her own row again, and at the figure under it in the third column, and at the four names below hers in that column, which are a man of thirty-four, a woman of nineteen, a man she has not seen and a man with a surname she knows.

And she worked out that she is the fifth name from the bottom of a third column of nineteen years of pencil on a slate at a turn on a store road, and that the third column runs under every name that has ever been put on that board, and that it will run under hers every month for as long as the man of forty-nine does his job.

That is what she did about it. Not one thing about the woman on the bench, because there is not one thing she can do about the woman on the bench, and she has been walking this road for nine years and there is not one person in it she is entitled to say that to.
