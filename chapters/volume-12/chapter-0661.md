# The Word She Does Not Have

She walked west off the turn at the first hour of the eighth of the month beginning 2,054 with the bar on her right shoulder and the light coming grey behind her, and the woman of sixty watched her go from the bench and did not say one word, which was the correct thing to do and cost her something anyway.

She had said a sentence yesterday on this road with nobody inside a hundred yards of her and she had not slept on it, and what it would not do is finish itself. So she walked, because the sentence wanted a west in it and there is one direction she has never walked on purpose.

She said, at the end of the first mile, out loud, on a made road, to the hedges: “I am going up.”

The road did not take it. She said it again at the end of the second mile and it came out the same, and by the third she was walking with her mouth shut, counting her breath, seven counts of it on a rise and four on the flat, and the rise was gentle, and seven was two more than she wanted.

## The Made Road West

The store road goes west out of the turn for nine miles and there is a post at the end of the nine. After that it stops being a road anybody pays for.

She had been past the post on the seventh and had not gone west off it then, because she had said a sentence on a bare board and had gone to a bench instead, and two days of that were behind her and she was not going to weigh them either. She had been past the mill five miles west, and the wheel on the stream out of the mill race had not turned in nine years, and she had looked at that wheel on the sixth and on the seventh and had not counted what it meant, and she counted it now.

Nine years. A wheel.

She did not think the two things were the same thing and she did not think they were different. She put the count down next to the wheel in her head where she puts everything, in the place where a number waits until something else arrives to go with it.

The cottage at the stopped lime bed was four miles west of the turn and she went past it at the third hour and the woman at the door was already in the yard, doing the same thing she does at that hour in that weather, which is standing with her hands behind her looking at the bed.

She has been past that cottage eleven times in nine days of road and the woman at the door has never once asked her for anything she can give, and neither of them has ever said one word that was not about the hour and the weather and the bed, and that is the correct arrangement between two people on a road and it is also the arrangement that has left her with two coppers in a bag in front of a bar.

She can buy two loaves. She has been eating one a day since the sixth and there is a half over. She cannot be paid, and has not been able to be paid for four years, and the reason is not a wound and not an injustice and will not come undone by anybody deciding to be generous to her.

## What The Sentence Is Missing

She stopped at a gatepost where the hedge gave her a mile and a half of road in front of her, and she stood in the mud of the gate and worked at it the way she works at everything, which is out loud and with her hands.

The sentence was five words long. *I am going up.* She had said it standing, in daylight, with a board on two posts four hundred yards behind her and a bench in front of her and no other living soul inside a hundred yards, and the sky had not answered and he had been still talking when she stopped.

The problem was not that the sky had not answered. She had gone nine years with the last of a ceiling gone into the ground in front of thirteen people and there was nothing over her head at any hour of any day, and she had made her peace with an empty sky in nine years of waiting.

The problem was that up had no object in it.

She said it out loud at the gatepost, flat, the way she says a thing she is taking apart:

“Up. Up to what.”

She waited for anything at all. A bell, a cart, the ordinary noise of a road with a woman on it.

She said, “I have said up nine days of road and I have not once said what I am going up to, and there is nothing in that sentence that a person could hand back to me.”

She said, “That is not a reason not to say it. That is the sentence with the hole in it and I am the one who put the hole there.”

And she found that she was not frightened, which she had expected to be for two days and was not, and that what she had instead was the particular flat feeling she gets when a thing she has been carrying for years turns out to be smaller than she has been holding it up to. Nine stages past a ceiling. A road nobody in nine days of road has ever been up. And the whole of it rested on a word with nothing behind it.

That was worth the walk. She said so, out loud, standing at a gatepost, which is the first use she has ever made of a sentence she said at the turn.

And then she said the rest of it, because a thing worked out in the first minute is not a thing worked out.

“I said a sentence yesterday on a road and I have walked nine miles with it and the whole of what is new in it is that it has no object in it, and I am the one who left the object out, and I did not leave it out by accident. I left it out because for eight years I had a word for where I was going and the word was a number, and I said a word with no number behind it and it turns out I do not have a second one.”

“You want to know what I am going up to. So do I. That is not a reason to stop walking.”

## The Hill With The Tower On It

She saw it from about two miles out, four miles up a mountain off the road, and she stood still on the rise and looked at it, which is the first time she has looked at it from this side.

It is a square tower and it is higher than anything else for thirty miles and she had known that since she was a child with a bar on her shoulder, and she had looked away from it every time she came down this road, because it is the one building west of the turn with a lamp in a cage in it that she has ever been inside, and being inside it had cost her two years of her life and one dead man.

She said, “Four miles of climb.”

She said, “And four flights of stair, and the third landing takes my breath, and I have known both of those things since I was fourteen and I have been down that road more times than I can count and I have never once gone up past the floor where the books are.”

She said, “That is what up means. It means a room in this building that I have not been in, and there is not one person alive who has told me what is in it, and the man who could have told me is three feet from the back wall of it.”

Then she stood there for a minute and a half, with the rain coming in off the shoulder of the hill and going down the back of her coat, and let herself want it, which she had not done since she was about eleven years old.

She wanted to see a room.

That is all of it. Not the thing above the room, not a name, not a hand, not the seven sentences that are against her ribs folded four times on the side where the book is.

She wanted to stand in a room at the top of four flights of stair in a building four miles up a mountain, at the seventh hour of a morning in daylight, and see what was in it. She had wanted that since she was a child and had given it up twice for other things, and it had cost her the other things.

She went up the hill.

## Seven Counts On A Steep Bit

The road stops a third of the way up and the rest is a cart track cut into the side with a wall of dry stone on the uphill side that somebody built a long time before the tower was a tower, and she has always thought the wall went up the mountain before the building came down off it, and she has no way of knowing and has never gone to find out.

By the time she had the track her breath was on seven and it stayed there for ninety yards, which is not far, and it came back to four on a flat bit and she let it and did not fight it.

She counted the things in her coat while she walked, because her hands were busy with the bar and because it is the first hour of a day and she does it at the first hour of every day.

A bar nine feet nine. A skin. A bag with a dish and a lump of clay and a ring a finger’s width, a stub of a nib and a cake of ink she opened on the seventh of the month beginning 1,874 and has never let dry. Two coppers she cannot count and has not tried to count since a woman behind a counter named her face.

A book of eight leaves made out of a blanket, with a date and a place on the first and four sentences on the second and four capital strokes on the third that are the worst four strokes in this world and are hers.

And four sheets of rice paper folded four times against her ribs on the side where the book is, in a fair hand, with seven sentences on them, and forty-one short strokes down the side of the fourth one that she is not going to find out about and has said out loud twice that she is not going to find out about.

She was lighter at the eighth hour of the eighth of the month beginning 2,054 than she was at the first hour of it, which is five hundred and one days of it, counting the eighteenth of the month beginning 1,544 as the first, and not one of them going back, and she was not hungry, and she would not be hungry at four days either.

At the fourth hour she was at the door of the tower with the rain running off the eaves in one place and a boy of sixteen on the top step with a bucket in each hand, going in to fetch water.

He looked at her for two seconds and then at the bar and then at her again.

He said, “You have got nine days of road on you.”

She said, “I have got nine days of road on me.”

He said, “Then come in out of it, and I have got the lamp going and I do not want to do it on my own.”
