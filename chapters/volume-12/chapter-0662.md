# Four Flights To A Burning Cage

She woke on the floor of the copying floor at the second hour of the ninth of the month beginning 2,054 with her back against the end of the bench and the bar under her head where she had put it, and for the length of four counts she did not know what building she was in, which has happened four times in her life and three of them were in this one.

Then she heard the bucket.

The room came back to her in the order it always comes back in. The shelves and the four hundred volumes on them and the working pile on the bench and the rack at the end with nothing on it but the frame, because the frame takes a sheet of a county’s own hand and not a clerk’s, and the lamp in its cage on the fourth landing overhead, and the boy going for water.

She had not slept eight hours in one place since she was about nine years old and she had slept eight hours here and woken with the cold in the left side of her, and none of that was the reason she could not get up.

The reason she could not get up was the lamp.

## The Cage And The Oil

A keeper’s lamp burns while the keeper is in the building. He told her that in the winter of her fourteenth year, unasked, because she was asking him about the oil.

She had put the cloth over that lamp on the twenty-second of the month beginning 2,024 and left it burning, and she had said out loud at the time, in front of the boy, that a lamp in a cage going with no keeper in the building was a thing she had done on purpose and could undo.

Seventeen days.

She lay on the floor and looked up at the bottom of the cage for a count and a half and did the arithmetic, because she cannot stop doing it, and seventeen days of a lamp with nobody feeding it is not a lamp. It is about a hundred hours of oil at the rate that cage burns, and he used a hundredweight of coal a year because he had a bad chest and it was a cold building, and a lamp of that size goes through a cake of oil in a night.

The boy had been in the building every one of those eighteen days.

## What The Boy Said About The Oil

She got up and found him on the landing with the buckets and she said, without any preamble, standing, in the cold on the fourth floor:

“How long has that lamp been going.”

He said, “Since you went down.”

She said, “Say it in days.”

He said, “I have kept it. There is a can under the bench with oil in it and I have been bringing it up twice a day, and I did that because it went out on the second and I did not want to be in the building in the dark at the second hour.”

She said, “You did not know about keepers.”

He said, “I know about keepers. He told me about keepers in the first month and he told me a lamp goes out when the building is empty and he said it the way he said things, which was once.”

She said, “And the building has not been empty.”

He said, “No.”

She stood on a landing with a boy of sixteen and found that the thing standing between her and a lamp burning in a cage in an empty building for eighteen days was a sixteen-year-old with a can of oil and two hours a day, and that he had done it because it was dark at the second hour, and not because he had understood anything about a rule.

That is the whole of it and it is the first thing since the sentence on the road that has made her want to be in a room.

## Four Flights And The Third Landing

They went up the four flights together at the sixth hour because she wanted the stairs with a witness on them, which is a thing she does now and does not explain.

First. Second. She was at four counts on the first and at five on the second and she did not make a matter of it.

The third landing is nine feet by eleven and the turn goes back on itself there, and she has known for five years that the third landing is where her breath goes wrong, and she came round onto it anyway with the boy in front of her carrying the oil can.

It came at her at the eighth step and stayed at seven and would not come down to four for the length of the landing, and she stopped and put her hand flat on the wall and stood there for a count of thirty with her eyes shut while a boy of sixteen waited on a step with a can of oil and did not say one word about it.

He said afterwards, on the landing, without weight on it: “You go slower than the oil does.”

She said, “I have been going slower than the oil does since I was fourteen. It has not mattered before today and it is going to matter four times between here and the top.”

He said, “How do you know it is going to matter.”

She said, “Because that is the second floor going and there are four, and I am going up all of them today and then I am going down all of them, and then I am going to do it again before the month is out.”

## The Fourth Landing And The Bench

The lamp is on the fourth landing in a cage of flat iron bars with the door of it on the hinge side, and there is a shelf under it for the can, and the shelf is the part of this building she trusts most, because a man built a shelf under a lamp for a keeper to put the oil on, and that is a thing you can stand in front of and understand.

The bench is nine feet long and it took two men to bring in and the bench that is not the copying bench is against the east wall under the window, and she sat on it with the key in the front of her bag where it has been since the twentieth of the month beginning 2,024.

The boy put the can on the shelf and topped the lamp up and did not spill any and did not wipe his hand on anything, which is a two-year-old habit in a sixteen-year-old and is the only thing in this building that tells her he was taught by somebody careful.

She said, “Is that the whole of your work.”

He said, “What is on the rack, and the days in a column, and the water, and the lamp, and I have four hundred volumes on those shelves and I have read nine of them.”

She said, “Nine in two years.”

He said, “I read them at the fourth hour when there is nothing to copy and the building is quiet and there is nobody to hear a page turn. It is the only hour in this room where there is not a person in it.”

She said, “Nine out of four hundred in two years.”

He said, “They are announcements. Every one of them is a thing that was going to happen to somebody on a certain day, and I copy them and then I read nine of them and now I know what this building is, which is that it knows things before they happen and writes them down in time.”

She said, “That is not what this building is.”

He said, “Then what is it.”

She said, “It is a room where a man writes down what is going to be said so that it can be said on the day, and the reason it is on a shelf in a tower is that the person who says it is not in the room, and he never was, and that is what a keeper is for.”

He looked at her for a moment and then he said, “Then who says it.”

She had not got an answer to that and she did not invent one, and it is the first time in ten years that a person has asked her a question about this building and she has not known the answer standing in it.

She said, “I do not know. Go and fetch the water and we will find out who said it about a bundle.”

## The Drawer And The Lamp Together

She got up off the bench and went to the drawer in the far corner under the shelves and put her hand flat on the top of it.

She said, “Is that locked.”

He said, “It has been locked since before you went down the stair.”

She said, “That is my doing.”

He said, “I know. I have been in this room seventeen days with a locked drawer I have not got the key to and I have not knocked on it once, and I want you to know that I have thought about it every day.”

She said, “I know you have.”

He said, “How do you know that.”

She said, “Because you put your hand flat on the top of it before you said it was locked, and a person who has not thought about a drawer does not put a hand on it.”

She said, “No.”

And then she stood in the middle of the floor of a copying room with four hundred volumes behind her and a lamp burning overhead in a cage that ought not to be burning and a sixteen-year-old boy with a can of oil on a shelf and a locked drawer under the shelves, and she said the thing out loud, standing, in daylight, in the room, to the boy and to the shelves:

“Nineteen years I have been reading boards, and I have not got one, and I have never once been on one, and the only reason I have read boards since I was twelve is that it is the only thing anybody has ever paid me for, and the building where the only man in nine days of road would have put my name on anything has been standing here empty with a lamp in it.”

He said, “You have not asked me anything.”

She said, “No.”

He said, “You are not going to ask me anything.”

She said, “Not today.”

He said, “All right,” and went down four flights with the water and came back up empty, because that is what the stair does, and she was left on the fourth floor of a tower four miles up a mountain with a lamp in a cage overhead and a boy of sixteen who had asked the carter a question on the first of the month beginning 2,054, nine days before, and had waited nine days with the answer in his mouth and had not said it on a floor.

He said it from the bottom of the stair, with his hand on the rail and his head below her feet:

“I asked the carter where it goes.”

She said, “Where what goes.”

He said, “The bundle. Every three months. He came up on the ninth to tell me and I have been waiting for you and I am not saying it on the fourth floor.”
