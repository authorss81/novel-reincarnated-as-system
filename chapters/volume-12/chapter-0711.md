# The Seventh First Goes By Empty

She stood in the grit at the foot of the two posts from the eleventh hour of the twenty-ninth of the month beginning 2,234 until the light came up, and the light comes up on that frame last, and it came up on it last.

## The First Hour

The seventh first came at the first hour and he was already there.

He is always already there. He has done four columns a month for nineteen years and he comes at the first hour on a first and at the sixth hour on a twenty-fourth and in between he is not on this road at all, and she has never once been here early enough to be wrong about it.

She was four feet back in the grit where a cart can go past without touching her, which is the place she asked for on the twenty-fourth of the month beginning 2,054 from a man with a stick of white chalk who said it is not a thing anybody does.

She took the slate out of her head and counted the days in her own column without touching it. Thirty, all of them, and she counted them the way she counts everything, from the top and down, and she got thirty, and there was no mark on that board anywhere that said a twentieth of anything had gone, and there has never been, and that is not a secret, it is just how a column is.

## Nine Seconds On The Other Side

He wiped the frame where the slate sits, which is a thing no one has asked him to do and which he has done every month of his working life, and he wiped the slate with the flat of his thumb in the time it takes a man to turn round twice.

And then he turned it over.

Not to wipe it. The other side has faced the wall for nineteen years and has never been wiped, and he does his two columns on the road side and then puts his other two in on the other side and turns it back with the chalk side out.

She got nine seconds.

## The Figure Under Her Name

Her name is four lines from the bottom and the figure under it is one hundred and eighty-eight.

She read it in a count of four with the light still coming up over the hill and she did not touch anything and she did not make a sound.

One hundred and eighty-eight days, counted with the first day as the first, from the ninth hour of the twenty-fourth of the month beginning 2,054 to the first hour of this morning.

One hundred and eighty-eight out of twenty is nine and four tenths of a day, and four tenths of a day is nine hours and thirty-six minutes.

Nine days, nine hours and thirty-six minutes.

She stood four feet back in the grit at a frame in a turn in the first light of a morning and did not say one word of it, out loud, to anybody, and it is the only thing she has ever come to this frame for and she had it in her hand in nine seconds and she did not spend it.

That is not modesty. There are four people inside a hundred yards of that frame this morning and two of them are nine years older than her.

## The Thing She Watched Him Do

And then she watched the rest of it, standing, in daylight, four feet back, with her arm open and the rain starting.

He put his two columns in on the other side, in a county hand, without hurry, the way a man writes what he does four times a month of his working life.

And she stood there and watched a man who does not know that he is writing a levy write four more lines of one.

Every name on that slate gets one in twenty of every day it has had in that column, added to what it had already had. He has been doing that for nineteen years and four times a month. He wipes it with his thumb in the time it takes a man to turn round twice and he does not know he is doing it, and he is not a cruel man, and he is not being cruel to anybody.

She has been working out where a figure comes from for nine days and she found it at a frame in the first light, and the answer was standing four feet off her holding a short stick of white chalk.

And there is not one person she is entitled to tell.

## What Went Up At The Second Hour

The cart came at the second hour with a man on it who has done that run for longer than she has been alive and who got off it and took the slate out of the frame and put it on the cart flat and put four small wedges under the corners and got back on.

He did not look at her. She was four feet back and she was looking at the ground.

Her name went up a road in a county hand with thirty days in beside it, and thirty days of hers are on it, and a twentieth of those days came off somewhere between here and a room she has never been in, and it does not say so on the board and it does not come off the name, and a man told her that on the twenty-fourth of the month beginning 2,054 and he was right.

And nothing else went up.

Four strokes at the top that were not hers. A column of days for a man of thirty-four. A column for a woman of nineteen. A column for a man she has never met. And her name.

## Why She Wrote Nothing

And she did not write anything, and she had decided that on the twenty-fourth of a month at the ninth hour standing in the same grit, and she kept to it, and it cost her the whole morning.

She wanted to. That is the part of it she has not been able to put anywhere.

She had a figure in her head that was true, and a date, and a place, and a hand that cannot write a false line, and a nib and a cake of ink in a bag that has not been let dry since the seventh of the month beginning 1,874, and she had thirty days to think about it, and she had four.

She stood in the grit from the second hour to the ninth hour of the first of the month beginning 2,264 with a bar nine feet nine on the ground at her feet and her right arm open and a board gone up a road with her name on it, and she wrote nothing, and the reason she gave herself at the ninth hour, out loud, in daylight, to a frame, was:

“I do not have a line yet. And a line is not a number I have not said. It is a thing I mean.”

## The Two Days After

And then she stayed in the grit for two more days and worked out the whole cycle of a board, which she has watched eleven times in nine years and had never once counted.

A board goes up on the first at the second hour. It goes up the road to the seat. It comes back down on the seventh of the month in the middle of the afternoon on the same cart with the same man on it, and the man puts the slate into the frame and wedges it and does not look at anybody.

And it sits in that frame empty for the three weeks between that and the twenty-fourth, with two posts and a rail and nothing on it but the chalk of the first carter west every morning of its working life.

Thirty days in every column when it is full. Her name in a county hand in the first column. Four strokes at the top.

And not one mark anywhere on it that says a twentieth of anything has gone, and there has never been, and there is not going to be.

She counted her own column out loud twice in her head, from the top and down, and got thirty both times, and then she did the thing she does with a figure she cannot check, which is to check it against something that is not it, and the only other thing she has is a bar nine feet nine lying along the ground at her feet, and a bar has not got a column on it and never will.

And then she worked out, standing, in daylight, with the light going off it, how many nights had gone by at the north end of a ground of two days of road east while she was standing in a gravel turn doing nothing.

Two thousand two hundred and sixty-six less two thousand one hundred and twenty-eight is a hundred and thirty-eight nights, and a hundred and thirty-eight times four is five hundred and fifty-two, and there are five hundred and fifty-two strokes on a board that four men put in four a night for a ground of two hundred people, and not one of the four of them can read the line that is hers.

She stood there with that figure for a count of forty and then said it out loud to the empty turn, because she cannot put a thing down that she has worked out.

## What She Spent

And that was the last first of a month she is ever going to get for nothing, and she got it on purpose, and she had said out loud on the twenty-fourth of the month beginning 2,234 that she would, and she is the only person in this county who knows she spent it.

Twenty-eight days. And on the twenty-eighth day from here it is the first of the month beginning 2,294, and a board goes up, and it has to have a first of a month on it or it does not go up at all, and there is not one other date in this county it can travel on.

She put the bar on her left shoulder in a count of twenty-nine and walked four hundred yards west to a bench where a woman of sixty was sitting and stopped at the end of it and said, standing up, in daylight, one question:

“The first hour of the first of a month that I have not got anything on. Did you have one of those.”

The woman of sixty said, “I have had about thirty of them.”

She said, “And what do you do with them.”

The woman of sixty said, “You stand at the end of them and you find out on the other side what you were doing, and it is always the same, and that is the whole of what a first of a month is.”

She said it in the ordinary voice, and then she said the second part, which she has not said to anybody before and which took her about nine seconds:

“Ning Yan. That was the seventh first since you gave him your name, and you have not asked me for one thing in nine years. I have been on that bench nine years and I am not going to call a question a kindness, because you did not ask it for that. But you are going to be standing here on the first of the next month after next and I am going to be on this bench, and you are not going to be standing here for nothing.”

And then she looked at the ground, and that was the end of it, and there was not one word after that about it.

Ning Yan stood four hundred yards from a bench for a count of sixty. Then she picked up her bag and went east down the store road, because she has got twenty-eight days and a line to find and there is not one person in nine days of road she is entitled to ask, and she has two days of road in front of her before she is anywhere at all.
