# The Ring Stays Cold

The fire did not come up on the fourth of a month either, or on the fifth, or on the sixth.

She got up at the fourth hour every one of those mornings and went and crouched at the low end of it and put a lump out of the box in with her own hand, and it came up a finger’s width and sat there and would not go against the brick.

By the eighth of a month she had stopped expecting it and that was worse, because a thing you expect every morning and do not get is a thing you get up for, and a thing you do not get up for is a thing you do not get up.

She got up at the fourth hour anyway. That was four days of it.

There was not a person on nine days of road she could have told, and there is no use telling a shed, and so the fourth hour had turned into a thing her body did on its own, the way a thing a body has done for four months does, and the body was the only party to the arrangement and was not answering.

On the fourth she caught herself standing in the doorway with her hand already out and a lump already in it before she had decided anything, and then she stood there with her arm out in the cold for about a minute before she put it in.

## What A Day Was

A gate is four feet of drain on the low side of Pei’s field and a short iron out of the gate house at dawn and the iron back in the box before she left.

A fifth of a reach, four feet down, ground coming up to the lip of the bank every four feet, one copper out of the fold in the inside of the door frame.

The iron is the gate’s and it goes back in the box before she leaves, and the box has a hasp on it, and the hasp is not locked, and it has not been locked in the whole time she has worked there.

She has thought about that hasp every morning for thirteen days.

Five miles west to the store on the day she went, bread for five days, eight coppers.

Three miles east to the works on the days she went, and the board full since the eleventh of a month about a month back, eleven lines on it, every one of them dressed stone priced by the ton, and no line on it she could take.

Four miles east to the bank on the days the man with the rule in his hand had a length on it, which was three days in seven, and a cup of something hot out of the office end from a woman who had never asked her name.

One mile and a quarter west of the shed to the flat past the last hedge on the days she went there, which was none, because there was nothing there for her and she was not on the board.

Four barrow-loads of clay against the outside wall of the shed where she had put them on the ninth of a month, and a fifth of a sack standing in a barrow on its handles, and the ring a finger’s width in the corner of a shed at a mill.

That was the whole of a day and it took about eleven hours of walking and about four hours of standing and it added up to one copper and about two thirds of a loaf and a cup of something hot three days in seven, and there was nothing on any of it for her hands.

## The Clay Going

A third of a sack out of every fifth of a reach was clay, and a third of a sack is three coppers at the yard and three coppers is three days of the gate, and she had half a box of clay left in the corner of the shed and had had it since the twenty-fifth of a month four months back.

Half a box is four days of the ring.

There was no ring.

There had been one every morning since the twenty-second of a month four months back, and she had not built a single one of them.

That was the thing she had been getting wrong for thirteen days, standing over the box at the fourth hour with clay going in under her own hand. She had not been making a fire.

She had been doing a thing beside a fire.

She had half a box of clay in the corner of a shed that could not be fired with anything in it, and she was getting clay out of a drain at the rate of about a box every ten days, and the yard paid three coppers a third of a sack in clay and a box paid the store four days of bread.

So the arithmetic of a dead fire is this: she can either carry the clay out of the drain and pile it against a wall, or she can put it in the ring and have nothing.

She worked that out twice in four days and got the same answer both times and did not like it once.

## What Her Hands Did

Cold.

That was all of it, and it went on being all of it.

Her hands had been warm for four months — warm the way a fire is warm, in a way she had never once been able to describe to anybody and had described out loud to a flat piece of stone by a door four thousand times, and the stone had been cold every one of those times and her hand had been warm every one of those times.

On the second of a month she had put four pieces of heavy stone up off her own chest with what was under her ribs and her hands had gone cold almost the moment it stopped.

On the fourth, the fifth, the sixth and the seventh of a month they stayed cold.

It did not hurt. She got up in the dark and her hands were cold and she put her palm flat on the stone by the door and the stone was cold and her hand was cold and for the first time in four years the two of them were the same temperature and it told her nothing because there was nothing there to tell her anything.

She had been out of a bound seven months, and for the four months before this the stone had been a cold flat thing that told her a warm hand was lying on it, every morning, at the second hour, four fingers and a thumb, and she had got more out of that stone than she had got out of anything else on nine days of road.

And now it was a cold flat thing.

She said that out loud at the second hour on the fifth of a month to the boards, because she had said things out loud to the boards at the second hour since she was eleven years old and there was no other place on nine days of road where saying a thing out loud did not cost something.

## What She Did Not Do

There were four things she could have done about a fire that would not light itself and she did not do any of them.

She did not walk three miles to a gateway and say to a man with a bench that she had a furnace and no way to bring it up. A man with a bench writes a thing down. That was the arrangement she had learned at the twenty-sixth of a month six months back and it had cost her a column of hours and a set of figures that went nine days of road in a hand she could not read.

She did not go to the yard and put herself in a place where a man with a rule in his hand had to think about her. He had found her in an order in two seconds on the fourteenth of a month and he was right about it and she was not going to stand in front of him and ask him to move her.

She had got a cup of something hot out of the office end of that yard three days in seven for three weeks, and a man with a rule had watched her take a hedge down and go back and do a hundred yards again, and there was a woman in that office who had never once asked her name.

Walking back in there and saying it out loud, in daylight, in front of nine men, would have been the third true thing she had put into a room that writes things down.

She did not take her own book off the stone under the keeper’s ladder at a post nine miles west, and she did not find out whether a man who cannot read a word in it would be willing to hold a book and look at the wall while something in a corner of a shed came up.

And she did not ask.

## Why She Did Not Ask

That was not a rule. She had asked twice in six months, both times in daylight and both times out loud to a man in front of other men, and both times it had gone into a book and come back down a road in a hand she had never been able to read.

The reason she was not going to ask a third time was about eleven hours long and she did it standing up on the fifth of a month at the eleventh hour with the bar across her knees in the gateway of the works.

A thing that comes into a room gets put in a room. She had worked that out on the twenty-second of a month about two weeks ago standing eleven feet off a man in dry grass, and it had come out at four words long: a room is where a thing is put so that four people can agree about it later.

A furnace is a thing. A second pair of hands is a thing. And a person standing in a shed with a woman and a ring in the corner of it is a thing that four people can agree about later, and it would be written down, and she cannot read the hand.

That was three things, and she had two of them solved, and the third one was the one she could not pay for.

She could put the clay in. She could stand in the room. What she could not do was buy the half of it that was a person, and there is no yard on nine days of road that puts a man on a barrow for a shed at a mill.

So she would do it on her own or not at all, and she had half a box of clay and eight days of bread and one copper a day and the rest of the month in front of her, and on the fourth of a month the fire had not come up and neither had anything else.
