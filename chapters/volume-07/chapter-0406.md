# Two Prices For A Line

The nineteenth of a month beginning 1,004 was the day a man of forty with a rule in his hand walked four miles east from a yard at a turn and stood at the works rail at the sixth hour and read his own line on it, and she was there before he was, because she has been at that rail at the sixth hour every morning for seven months and has never once been early for it.

There were twenty-nine lines on it and one of them had his yard at the head of it.

## The Ninth Hundred Feet

Cart track, east of the turn, nine hundred feet, by the load.

He read it the way he reads everything, which is not the way she reads it. He put a finger on the number and looked at the other end of the rail and then came back along the line with his hand four inches off it.

“Nine hundred,” he said.

“Yes.”

“East of the turn. That is the same bank the clay comes off. I put it in on the second and it is my own ground and I have got nine men there and four will do, and if it is not cut by the twenty-eighth I have got no way of putting it right after the twenty-eighth because the ground goes.”

He took the chalk out and looked at it and put it back without marking anything.

“It is the only line on this rail in two years that I have not been able to check myself.”

## Two Prices

She had been standing at the rail for an hour and a quarter and he came and stood at the end of it and did not tell her to move.

“You have read this board twice every morning for a year,” he said. “You read it in nine seconds. What are you doing at it.”

“I am looking for a line.”

“No.”

“I know.”

He turned round and looked at the twenty-nine lines from the other end.

“Then you have not been reading it for the lines,” he said. “You have been reading it to see how many there are, and there were eight, and now there are twenty-nine, and you are standing here at the sixth hour on a full board the way a person stands at a door.”

She did not answer that and he did not wait for it.

“There are two prices on this ground and people mix them up the way they mix up a load and a column,” he said. “The rail is priced off the ground. Everything on it is measured by the weight or measured by the length, and that is the whole of it and it was written at the head of the rail by a man who is dead.”

“Yes.”

“And a line of your own, in a book, in a hand, is four coppers on the third floor of the counting house and it has been four coppers for longer than I have been alive, and it is ten at the counter and it has been ten since before I came. And that is a different thing and it buys a different thing, and it has never bought a person a place on that rail.”

## What She Said

“I know that,” she said. “I asked the price of a line on the fourteenth of a month two months back and a woman of forty-five told me it was four coppers and it was four for everybody, and I have had more than four twice since and I have not paid it.”

He looked at her.

“Why not.”

That was the question and she had not expected it, and she answered it in the ordinary voice on a rail in daylight at the sixth hour of the nineteenth of a month.

“Because there is no hand on nine days of road that can write a line of mine and read it back to me. Not her. Not the man with a brush on the floor below her. Not you. Not the man at the counter. I could pay four coppers and have a line of myself in a book in a hand that is not mine, and there would be nothing at the end of it I could check.”

He put the chalk in his pocket.

“That is not a reason anybody on this ground would give me,” he said, “and I have been asking people things for twenty-two years and I have heard worse reasons than that.”

“It is the only reason I have.”

“All right,” he said.

## What He Said About The Hand

He was about to go and did not go, and the reason he did not go was that he had put his hand flat on the rail and was looking down it at nothing in particular, and a man who is looking down a rail is a man who is about to say a thing he does not often say.

“There is one hand on this ground that can read what comes off a road and two men on this side cannot make out at all,” he said. “I do not know his name. I know the trade and I know what it costs.”

“A thing comes down the mill road that two men on this side cannot make out at all, and it goes up to him, and it comes back down readable, and that has been the arrangement since before the wheel stopped turning.”

He took his hand off the rail.

“I have never once asked where he sits, because the day I ask that question I am a man who has been curious, and a man who has been curious about that gets asked what he wanted to know it for.”

## The Rest Of The Nineteenth

She dug the run at the gate from the seventh hour to the ninth hour and got a copper, and Pei came out at the ninth hour and stood at the end of it the way he stands, and there were six coppers in the fold in the inside of the door frame and bread for two days.

Then she walked four miles east to a yard at a turn because it is three days in seven, and there were nine men on the north end and a second course going on over a sill, and the woman in the office end came out with a cup of something hot in it and put it into her hands and went back in without stopping.

That was the fourteenth time in eight weeks and it was hot, and her hand did not do it at all, and it took a second and a half, and she did not have to stand there and watch it go cold this time because there were nine men on the north end and three of them had been watching that bench since the twenty-sixth.

She put it on the edge of the bench at the office end, which is where things go at that yard, and wiped her hand on her coat, and went and dug a length of drain at the low side for nothing, because there is a length on that ground that three days in seven is unpaid and that has never once been asked about.

## What She Did About The Seven

Nothing.

She had seven coppers at the end of the twentieth and the margin correction was four, and she had walked up the mill road with the strip in her left hand eleven times since the fourteenth of a month two months back.

She worked it out standing at the gate at the ninth hour on the twentieth with the bar in her right hand, and it came out the same way it had come out twice before, which is that a line of her own in the third floor’s ordinary hand is not a thing anybody inside nine days of road can check for her, and that four coppers buys the being in a book and not the knowing what is in it.

That is the third time. A rule is not worked out twice, and she had said that sentence to herself about four other things in seven months, and she stood at a gate and said it about this one as well, and it was the first time she had ever used it on a thing she wanted.

## The Plain Part About One Person

She is eighteen years old and there is a man on this ground who reads the only hand on it that she cannot read, and he is four miles west of her and she has never been in a room with him and she has not asked to be.

She has wanted that since a strip came down a road with a word at the head of it in a hand she cannot read. She had that strip in her coat for five months, and a column nine days of road east with two lines off the bottom of it, and a book of her own on a stone under a keeper’s ladder at a post she has not walked to, and a second line at a counter with a day against it.

Every single one of them is in that hand or is about that hand.

And on the nineteenth of a month a man with a rule in his hand told her, in about twenty seconds and for no reason at all except that he had his hand flat on a rail, exactly where he stood among the people on that ground.

## The Twentieth And The Twenty-First

She dug the run at the gate on the nineteenth and got a copper, and she dug it on the twentieth and got a copper, and she had seven in the fold in the inside of the door frame and bread for one day.

The store is five miles west. Four loaves is eight coppers and eight is bread for five days.

The twenty-first was the fourth gate day in five and the fourth copper in the fold, and eight was the number, and it could only be eight if she dug that morning, and if she dug that morning she could not be at the rail at the sixth hour, because the run is worked from the seventh to the ninth and the store is five miles west of the gate and the walk takes two hours each way.

She dug the run at the seventh hour. Pei came out at the ninth hour and stood at the end of it the way he stands, and she went west at the ninth hour with eight coppers in the fold in the inside of the door frame.

She had not been at the rail that morning, for the first time in seven months.

She was two miles down the road at the tenth hour and she stopped, and she stood on a bank in the middle of a made road for eleven minutes and worked out whether she was doing it because she was hungry or because she had worked out that she was hungry.

She had not eaten since the morning of the nineteenth.

And then she went on west, because a person does not get to choose the reason and then take the other thing, and the board was not going to be any different in four hours and the bread was.

At the second hour of the twenty-second she was at the shed with nothing in the fold and bread for five days, and she had not eaten since the nineteenth, and she ate.
