# Forty Thousand And One

There is no bread in the shed and there are fifteen coppers in the fold in the inside of the door frame, and the shop is five miles west, and it is the eighth of a month.

She went up the mill road at the third hour instead of walking west, and she stood at the edge of a table the length of nine men with forty books open on it and her strip in her left hand, and she was warm within ten minutes of getting there, the way she is every time.

A woman of forty-five with a thumb gone off the right hand at the first joint read down a column for an hour and a quarter without once looking up.

## The Fourth Hour

At half past the fourth she put the pencil down flat on the page and did not pick it up again.

“I am going to do something I have not done in sixteen years,” she said. “You are going to sit there and you are not going to help me and you are not going to say anything about it until I have finished.”

“All right.”

“And if I ask you a question in the middle of it, you are going to finish the sentence first.”

“All right.”

She did not have the column out. She had not brought one, and there was no way to bring one, because the column she was going to read belonged to a book that was not in that room and could not be fetched by anybody who was going to do it before the fifth hour.

She read it off the table instead, the way she reads forty thousand of them.

## The Set

“There are eleven sets of open books on this floor,” Rao said, “and I keep all eleven, and the set that came up on the first of a month is the set for the month, and I put it at that end of the table when it comes, and it stays open until the next first.”

She put her hand flat on nine inches of pages.

“This one is five months old. It is the earliest set on this table and it has been open since the seventeenth of a month and it is the oldest open set in the building. Sixteen years, and there has never been a person at this end of the table asking me what is on it.”

Ning Yan did not say anything.

“The name at the head of it is yours,” Rao said, “and it is in a hand I cannot read. I want that said first so that you know the shape of what I am going to do.”

She turned the pencil over in her hand and did not use it.

“I have asked twice in sixteen years for a person who can read a hand like that, and both times I was told that there was not going to be one. What I can do is read a column, and a column is not a hand. A column is dates, hours, a season, a place, a rule, and whatever a person put in it by hand.”

## What A Column Is

She went along it at the speed she read at.

“Name at the head. Ning Yan. Of Lowwater.”

“Days down the margin. Five months of them, every day of them, and against each day the hours and what the hours were on. That is a running column and it is the ordinary shape and four in ten of the columns in this set are that.”

“A season marked at each end. The first end is the seventeenth of a month five months back. The second end is not marked and there is no second mark on that page, and I am not going to invent one and I am telling you there is not a mark.”

“A place against it, in the middle. Four miles east of a building with a stove in it, and a point on a road, and a distance. That is a place and it is in the ordinary hand of this building and I can read it, and it is a third of the way down.”

“A rule underneath it. A line under a line with nothing at the end of it. Every column has one of those and it means the line above it goes on past the end of the page, and in one in six there is a name at the end of it.”

She stopped.

“And there is a line at the foot of the page that is not in the same hand as any of the rest of it.”

## The Line At The Foot

“That one is dated the twenty-sixth,” Rao said. “And it is in the ordinary hand of this building, and it is the only line on that page that is in a hand from this room.”

She did not hurry it. She read it the way she reads everything else on that table, at the speed she reads it, with the pencil lying flat beside her hand.

“Ning Yan, of Lowwater. The holder is not presented. Refused at the counter. The twenty-sixth.”

Then she said:

“It says no.”

## The Short Part

The room was cold at the north end and it had been cold at the north end since before she came on it.

“It says no,” Rao said again, “and there is nothing in the shape of it that I have had to work out, and I have had no trouble with it at all.”

She turned the page a quarter round so the line at the foot of it was square to the two of them.

“That is because a man at a counter wrote it out in a hand I have watched him write in for eleven years, and then sent it up with the bundle on the first. It came up the mill road like everything else and it went into a column the way every line goes into a column.”

She put both hands flat on the pages.

“I have read forty thousand columns,” she said. “I have never read one back to the person it belongs to.”

She looked up for the first time since she had put the pencil down.

“You asked me for four hours a month of my own company and you did not ask me for that, and you have not asked me for it, and I am doing it because I have been at this table for sixteen years and it has taken me sixteen years to work out that it is the whole of what the table is for.”

## The Thing She Said After

“Then I will tell you the other half of it and you will not like it.”

“All right.”

“You have spent a month letting men with bars work out what you can pick up,” Rao said. “That is what a bar is for. They are right about it and they have been right about it every time.”

She squared the near edge of the book against the table.

“A bar does not read anything,” she said, “and every man holding one of them has done the correct thing with it.” “And there has never been one of them inside nine feet of you that would have found out one thing about you in six minutes that I have just read off a page.”

She turned the page over and put her hand on the next one.

“The strongest thing in this building is a column,” she said. “I have had one in my hand every day for sixteen years and it cannot lift anything, and it has never once carried a book up a stair by itself.”

She left her hand where it was.

“And when it wants a thing it says the word, and men go and do it, and that is all it is, and I am not going to say it a second time.”

## The Plain Part About One Person

She is eighteen years old.

She went up that stair four times in the last month and read dates at a woman for nothing, and she dug a drain at a gate for a copper a day, and she stood in a yard at a turn three days in seven and was the ninth of nine men and the smallest thing in it, and a man with a rule in his hand has twice told her exactly what she can do and exactly what she cannot.

And the whole of that is a way of asking what she is worth.

And there has never been a question asked her, on nine days of road, about what her own column says.

She wanted to hear the hours. That was the want, and it had been the want since the eighth of a month in the morning, and she had not known it was the want until she had been listening to it for an hour and a quarter.

## What She Felt About It On The Walk Down

She was angry for about two hundred yards and then she was frightened, and the fright was worse and she worked out why on the mill road with her hand on the rail.

If the strongest thing in that building is a column, then she has been standing next to it for two months of months and working at the wrong end of it.

Every room on nine days of road that has priced her has priced her right. Every price has been for the hands and the arm and the four feet of a bar, and every one of them has been measuring a thing that is not what they wanted.

She put her left hand flat on the rail going down and it was cold, because the room had been the only warm thing in four miles and she was two hundred yards past the last wall of it.

She is eighteen years old and she has spent five months being found small by men who do not know what they are looking at, and the whole of what she has to sell is a page.

## What She Said At The Sixth Hour

“You did not read the second line at the head of it.”

Rao stopped with the pencil an inch off the page.

“No,” she said. “I did not, and I am not going to, and you did not ask me to and that is the correct arrangement.”

“It is a name.”

“It is a name and it is not yours and it is not in your hand and it has been under that page since before you came up this stair for the first time,” Rao said. “It is the reason the rule underneath the middle of your column has nothing at the end of it. I have looked at it four times in sixteen years from where I am sitting and I have never once had a reason to do more than that.”

She put the pencil down.

“And I am going to keep it there for the rest of my working life,” she said, “and on the first of a month it goes up the mill road with everything else, and I am not going to tell you one word about it, and you were right about that when you asked me for it in the first place.”

Ning Yan sat at the edge of that table until the woman of forty-five put both hands flat on the pages and said the fifth hour was coming and the room was going to be read out of in forty minutes.

And then she said, out loud, to an empty table, at ten minutes past the fifth hour:

“Everything that has ever been done to me on nine days of road came out of a book in a room, and I have never once been in the room while it was happening.”

The books stayed where they were. The pencil stayed where it was.

And on the table at the near end, with forty books open on it, there was a page with a refusal at the foot of it and eleven sets of open books around it, and there has never been a second refusal in any of them, and there had never been one.
