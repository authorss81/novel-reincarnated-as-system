# The First One Has Not Finished

A thing came down the mill road on the twelfth of a month beginning 1,064 that had been on it since the ninth, and the man behind the counter put his hand flat on the seasonal book and said the whole of it in four sentences before she was through the door.

“There is a question on this floor about a woman at a gate and it is not finished.”

“All right.”

“It went up with the tray on the first and it came down on the ninth and it is the second one in eleven hundred years that has asked the same thing twice.”

He took his hand off the book.

“And the way it goes away is that the holder answers it again.”

## What He Asked Her

He had the sheet folded once in his hand and he did not open it.

“I am not going to read it to you,” he said. “I have read two of these in eleven years and both of them were about a person who had gone nine days of road before I got to them. A reading about a person who is not standing in the room is the one thing my trade does not allow, and both of those books shut with nothing written in them.”

He folded the sheet over once more and put it in his pocket, and his thumb stayed on the pocket.

“One of those two people is alive and I do not know what she is doing this afternoon.”

“All I am going to ask you is whether the holder will do it.”

“That is not yours to ask.”

“No,” he said. “It is yours. I have said it to you and I would like to have asked you and I have.”

She stood at the counter of the only office inside nine days of road that she knows the name of and she did not answer him, and she went out of the door and up the made road two miles to a shed at a mill whose wheel has not turned since before she came.

## The Second Hour

She lay on the boards at the second hour with the strip against her ribs and she said his name out loud, once, in an empty shed, the way she had said it about forty times in seven months and had never once got anything back.

Then she asked him the thing.

## His Answer

He did not take any time at all about it, which is the thing she has learned to look for in a man who answers her.

“No.”

She lay there with the boards under her back.

“They will send it again,” she said.

“No they will not.”

“They have sent it twice.”

“They have sent the question twice and it has not been answered and there is a difference and I know what it is and so do you.”

## The Reason He Gave

He gave it in three sentences and he gave it in the present tense and he did not explain it and he did not advise her and he did not buy anything with it.

“A second use is a second time to be sent for. That is the whole of it and it has been the whole of it since a room with doors in it was first put under anybody.”

“The first one has not finished.”

“The first one finished on the twenty-second of a month two months back at the eighth hour on a step in daylight,” he said, “and what it has not finished is coming back down that road, and I am still in it.”

He did not say anything for a while.

“You have a want in this and I have watched you carry it since a strip came down a road and it is a real one and I am not going to tell you that you are wrong to want it. I am telling you what it costs me and it is the only thing I am entitled to put in this room.”

## The Plain Part About One Person

She is eighteen years old and a man she cannot see said no to a thing that would have made her safer, for a reason of his own that she would not have thought of, and he was right about it, and it cost him something, and he told her the price before he said the answer, which is what every man on nine days of road has taught her to look for since the eighth of a month four months back.

And she lay on the boards at a mill whose wheel has not turned and worked out that the thing she had wanted for five months would have cost a man who is already answerable a second time to be sent for, and that she would rather have the six months the man on the cart track told her about than that.

## The Tenth Hour

She went up the made road at the tenth hour of the twelfth of a month with the strip against her ribs and nothing in her hands.

Two miles up that road, with the mill on the left of it the whole way, is a brick-and-timber building a hundred feet long with a wide door standing open at the near end. She had stood in that doorway once, at the seventh hour of the twelfth of a month two months back, with a yard page in her left hand and a man on the north end of a yard who had said four words to her about coming in, and she had not gone in.

She went in at the tenth hour of that same day with no message behind it, and she had come of herself, and a man of fifty-one did not comment on it.

## Inside

It is the biggest room she has ever stood in.

The bench runs the whole length of the right-hand wall in oak four inches thick, and it is long enough for nine men sitting down, and there is a bar against the far wall, five feet of it, and a chain on a hook, and eleven dressed stones standing on edge along the left wall with their faces out.

There are nine men in the room and eight of them are between her and the door.

She is five foot two, with four fingers and a thumb on one hand and an arm that stands open at her side, and she is the smallest thing in that room by a long way, and every other person in it can lift the end of a thing.

Geng came off the frame with the chain in his hand.

“You have come back,” he said.

“You said it was a trade.”

“I said it was a trade and it was not an offer and I was not asking you to do it then. It is still not an offer.” He put the chain on the hook. “Sit at the bench. There is a sheet of faces on it and I have got four hundred and six pounds going out on the sixteenth and I have found nine numbers wrong on it since the second and I cannot leave the room.”

She sat at the bench.

## The Two Hours

She read four hundred and six pounds of dressed stone off a sheet in a hand of chalk and figures, out loud, in the ordinary voice, dates down, numbers against them, stop at the end of each one.

It took about two hours and she got two of them wrong, and Geng corrected her twice, and neither time was unkind and both times he wrote the figure she had said on his own sheet with his own pencil before he said the other one out loud.

Four men at the frame stopped what they were doing for the second one and listened.

At about the ninth minute she put her left hand flat on the bench and tried it.

Nothing happened.

She tried it twice more at about the fortieth minute and once more at the hundredth, in the middle of reading out loud a stone that weighed ninety-one pounds, and nothing happened at all, and no man in that room looked up off his work and no man asked her what she was doing with her hand on the oak.

She did not try it a fifth time.

## What She Did About It

At the hundred and sixth minute she put the sheet down and squared it to the edge of the bench and said, in the ordinary voice, in a room with nine men in it:

“It has not gone once in two hours.”

“No,” Geng said.

She did not ask him why and he did not offer, and there were eight other men in that room and the question had not been asked once all morning, and every one of them had worked out where she stood in the middle of that work in the time it takes to pick up a bar.

She stood up off the bench with her left hand flat on the oak the whole time, in case.

## Wood

A man at the far end of the room said, “Wood,” in the voice a man uses when a thing is not sitting true.

He was about forty and he had been on that floor eleven years and he got off the low frame, went along the bench, and picked up the bar off the wall where it lives.

The stone he was working on was a dressed stone standing on a low frame and it had been standing four inches off true since the second of that month, and there were two men on the chain and the near end of it was a hand’s width out over where the frame ended.

She was nine feet off it.

He got the bar under the near end and leaned and the bar did not move the stone, and he took his shoulder to it, and the stone did not move, and that was not a thing nine men were surprised by, because a stone that has been out of true since the second has a position and the position is the problem.

Then he set the bar down against the wall and went back to the bench, and nothing had happened and the bell on the floor had not gone.

She stood at the bench with her hands in front of her and worked out that she had been in the room two hours and a half, and that she was the only person in it who could read the sheet, and that she was the person it had been quietest to be next to.
