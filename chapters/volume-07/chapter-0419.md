# Nine Feet From The Bar

A thing came down the mill road on the twelfth of a month beginning 1,064 that had been on it since the ninth, and the man behind the counter put his hand flat on the seasonal book and said the whole of it in four sentences before she was through the door.

“There is a question on this floor about a woman at a gate and it is not finished.”

“All right.”

“It went up with the tray on the first and it came down on the ninth and it is the second one in eleven hundred years that has asked the same thing twice.”

He took his hand off the book.

“And the way it goes away is that the holder answers it again.”

## What He Asked Her

He had the sheet folded once in his hand and he did not open it.

“I am not going to read it to you,” he said. “I have read two of these in eleven years and both of them were about a person who had gone nine days of road before I got to them and one of them was about a woman and she said no to it and the no is still in that book and she is alive and I do not know what she is doing this afternoon.”

He put the sheet in his pocket.

“All I am going to ask you is whether the holder will do it.”

“That is not yours to ask.”

“No,” he said. “It is yours. I have said it to you and I would like to have asked you and I have.”

She stood at the counter of the only office inside nine days of road that she knows the name of and she did not answer him, and she went out of the door and up the made road two miles to a shed at a mill whose wheel has not turned since before she came.

## The Second Hour

She lay on the boards at the second hour with the strip against her ribs and she said his name out loud, once, the way she has said it forty times in seven months into a room with a stove in it or onto a gate post or into the ground.

Then she asked him the thing.

## His Answer

He did not take any time at all about it, which is the thing she has learned to look for in a man who answers her.

“No.”

She lay there with the boards under her back.

“They will send it again,” she said.

“No they will not.”

“They have sent it twice.”

“They have sent the question twice and it has not been answered and there is a difference and I know what it is and so do you.”

## The Reason He Gave

He gave it in three sentences and he gave it in the present tense and he did not explain it and he did not advise her and he did not buy anything with it.

“A second use is a second time to be sent for. That is the whole of it and it has been the whole of it since a room with doors in it was first put under anybody.”

“The first one has not finished.”

“The first one finished on the twenty-second of a month two months back at the eighth hour on a step in daylight,” he said, “and what it has not finished is coming back down that road, and I am still in it.”

He did not say anything for a while.

“You have a want in this and I have watched you carry it for two volumes and it is a real one and I am not going to tell you that you are wrong to want it. I am telling you what it costs me and it is the only thing I am entitled to put in this room.”

## The Plain Part About One Person

She is eighteen years old and a man she cannot see said no to a thing that would have made her safer, for a reason of his own that she would not have thought of, and he was right about it, and it cost him something, and he told her the price before he said the answer, which is what every man on nine days of road has taught her to look for since the eighth of a month four months back.

And she lay on the boards at a mill whose wheel has not turned and worked out that the thing she had wanted for five months would have cost a man who is already answerable a second time to be sent for, and that she would rather have the six months the man on the cart track told her about than that.

## The Tenth Hour

She went up the made road at the tenth hour of the twelfth of a month.

Two miles up that road with the mill on the left of it the whole way there is a brick-and-timber building a hundred feet long with a wide door standing open at the near end, and she stood in that doorway at the seventh hour of the twelfth of a month beginning 1,004 and did not go in, and she had not been asked to, and she was right not to have been.

She went in at the tenth hour of the twelfth of the next month with no message behind it, and she had come of herself, and a man of fifty-one did not comment on it.

## Inside

It is the biggest room she has ever stood in.

The bench goes the whole length of the right-hand wall and it is long enough for nine men sitting down. The bar is against the far wall, five feet of it, and the chain hangs from a hook, and eleven dressed stones stand on edge along the left wall with their faces out, and there are nine men in the room and eight of them are between her and the door.

She is five foot two with four fingers and a thumb on one hand and an arm that stands open, and she is the smallest thing in that room by a long way, and every other person in it can lift the end of a thing.

Geng came off the frame with the chain in his hand.

“You have come back,” he said.

“You said it was a trade.”

“I said it was a trade and it was not an offer and I was not asking you to do it then. It is still not an offer.” He put the chain on the hook. “Sit at the bench. There is a sheet of faces on it and I have got four hundred pounds going out on the sixteenth and it is wrong in nine places and I cannot leave the room.”

## The Two Hours

She sat at the bench and read four hundred and six pounds of dressed stone off a sheet in a hand of chalk and numbers, out loud, in the ordinary voice, and got two of them wrong on the first day and got them right on the second, and he corrected her twice both days and neither time was unkind.

At about the ninth minute she put her left hand flat on the bench and tried it.

Nothing happened. She tried it twice more at about the fortieth minute and once more at the hundredth, in the middle of reading out loud a stone that weighed ninety-one pounds, and nothing happened at all.

She did not try it a fifth time.

## What She Did About It

At the hundred and sixth minute she put the sheet down and said, in the ordinary voice, in a room with nine men in it:

“It has not gone once in two hours.”

“No,” Geng said.

She did not ask him why and he did not offer.

And then a man at the far end of the room said, “Wood,” in the voice a man uses when a thing is not sitting true, and got off the frame and went along the bench and picked up the bar.

## The Bell

The bell is on the floor.

It is a thing about the size of a cup with a tongue in it and it goes off when a stone that is standing on a frame goes past a quarter inch out of true, and it has been on that floor since before the wheel turned and it is not a bell that anybody rings.

He had the bar under the near end of a dressed stone that had been standing four inches off true since the second of a month, and there were two men on the chain, and there were nine feet between the near end of that stone and where a woman of eighteen was standing at a bench.

The bar went.

It went out of his hands sideways the way a bar goes out of a hand, and the stone came down a quarter inch on the frame and went past the quarter inch, and the bell went off on the floor.

A dressed stone came down on the leg of a man of thirty who had his fingers under the frame to keep the face square, and nine men in the biggest room she has ever stood in heard a bell go off about nine feet from where she was.

Four men got to it in about two seconds. One took the leg and two got the bar and the fourth went down on his knees on the low side, and they did not lift it. They waited.

She was nine feet off, and she had two seconds, and there was a man with his leg under a dressed stone in a room full of men who could lift what she could not lift with two hands.
