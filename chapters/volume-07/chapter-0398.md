# Eleven Years Of Clay

The tray came down the mill road at a quarter past the sixth hour on the ninth of the month and everything that had gone up it on the first came back on it, and there was a cart behind the tray with nothing on that one at all.

She was three miles west of the counting house at the sixth hour with a bar in her right hand and four fingers and a thumb on it, digging the run at the near end where the ground is hard, and she did not see it come down, and she worked it out afterwards from the sound of the wheels on the made road that it had gone past a quarter of an hour before she got in the ground.

That was the whole of the ninth.

## What The Man With The Brush Said About It

He came down three steps with a book open in his hand at the eighth hour and he stood at the bottom of the stair with it and he shut the door above him behind him, which he does not usually do.

“There is a name in the book that came up,” he said.

“Yes.”

“It is a woman’s name and it is at the left of a line with a day against it and an hour against that, and it is in a hand from the floor above mine, and it is the only name in sixteen years of that book that has got more than one word at the left of it.”

He turned the book round and held it out a foot and she could not read it from a foot away upside down, and he did not offer to read it again.

“That is the whole of it,” he said. “There is not a word on that line about work. It is not a line of work. It is a stair book and it is a woman’s name and the day and the hour, and I have copied it out into the book on this floor, which is the ordinary place for anything that comes off that table.”

“You cannot read her hand.”

“I cannot read her hand,” he said. “I can read mine and I can read the man behind the counter’s and that is the whole of what is in that building. I have said so three times to two different people and I am saying it a fourth time to you.”

## What He Said Next

“I have not asked what it is for and I am not going to,” he said. “It is a stair book and it is not my book and there is one line in it with two words at the left of it, and I have put it in the book on this floor in the ordinary hand because that is what I do with everything that comes off that table.”

He shut the book and held it against his chest with one arm.

“And it is in two books on nine days of road now instead of one, and both of them shut, and there is no hand in this building that can take a line out of either of them.”

He looked at her.

“You gave her the name yourself.”

“I did.”

“I did not need to be told that,” he said. “I have been on that floor eleven years and I can tell you which day the tray goes up without looking at a calendar.”

## The Eleventh, At The Fourth Hour

She went up the mill road at the third hour on the eleventh with the strip in her left hand and she was warm from ten minutes after she got to the table, and she sat at the edge of it for an hour and read nine columns out loud in the ordinary voice.

Rao had the stair book at the near end of the table with the cord still round it and a leaf standing up in it at about the eleventh line, and she did not move it out of the way once in an hour and a quarter.

At the fourth hour she put her pencil down flat.

“It came back on the ninth.”

“I know. The man on your floor has a copy of it.”

“He has a copy of it and I have the one it was copied from, and there is not a word on that leaf about why your name is on it,” Rao said. “I wrote a line about a day and an hour, because that is what a stair book is, and I have been looking at that line for nine days and it is the emptiest line I have ever written in.”

She squared it.

“I am not going to take it out, and I want you to hear me say that in my own voice, because I told you when you gave it to me that there was no hand in this building that could take it out, and neither am I.”

“All right.”

“And I am going to put your name at the left of a line on four days a month for the rest of my working life,” she said, “and I am not going to be thanked for it and I am not going to be honoured by it, and you knew both of those when you said it.”

## The Plain Part About One Person

She is eighteen, and her name is in two books inside nine days of road instead of one, and she got that by asking a woman out loud for eight hours a month, and a man with a brush on the floor below has just told her in the ordinary voice that both of them shut.

And she wanted to say thank you and she did not, because a man behind a counter told her on the twenty-ninth of a month six weeks ago that it was not for that, and because this one had not offered her anything.

What she wanted, standing at the bottom of a stair on the ninth of a month, was to know whether there was a word at the right of the line. There was not, because he had read the whole of it out to her and the whole of it was a name and a day and an hour.

Six weeks and she has a name in two books and not a sentence of why.

## The Tenth, At The South End

The second course had gone on top of the first at the north end that morning and she went down to look at the low side of that yard at the fourth hour, where there are four dressed stones in the soft ground and two and a half feet of clay round the two that were in it when she was last there.

There was more clay round them than there had been.

That was all. It was the tenth, and a second course had gone on and the low side had been done again by nine men who work on that ground every day and have never once said anything to her about any of it.

## The Woman Who Drives The Clay

She came up the track from the clay bank two hundred yards off with a barrow and a plank on the handles and half a load of clay in it, and she was fifty and she had a forearm like a man’s and she was pushing rather than pulling, which is the way a person drives a barrow they intend to come back with.

She tipped it into the clay stack at the edge of the yard, took the barrow back down the track, and came up again eleven times over an hour and ten minutes. On the ninth time she stopped, because there was a woman standing at the low side of that yard looking at four stones in the clay.

“You are the one from the gate.”

“Yes.”

“The man with the rule has had men asking him whether it was you and he says the same three words every time, and I have heard the same three words eleven times this morning,” she said. “I have driven clay up this track for eleven years for that yard and for four others and nobody has ever asked me anything.”

She set the plank against the barrow and took hold of the handles again.

“There is a household nine miles west of me that has paid me in clay every month for eleven years.” She tipped the barrow as she said it, the way a person talks while working. “It is not in this county and I do not know anybody in it. I have never seen the house. I go where the clay goes and I come back. And it has never once been late.”

She stood with the handles in her hands.

“You are the second person in a month who has told me something in this yard that they did not have to tell me,” she said. “The other one filled a hole in with his own barrow at seven in the morning and did not look at me while he did it.”

## What Was Not Said

She drove up the track again and she did not come back for another twenty minutes, and when she came back up she had a barrow-load of clay in it and she tipped it and took the barrow down without stopping.

What the four sentences had been sitting in the middle of a thing she does eleven times a day, between one load and the next, with the handles in her hands and her weight on them, was as close to nothing as she has ever heard anybody come to.

Eleven years of a monthly clay going nine miles to a house that is not in her county, out of a person she has never seen, for a woman who has never once been late and who asked one question in her first year and was told, correctly, that it was not her business.

## What Was New In It

That is four sentences and they are four different sentences and they are the first four anybody has said to her about where anything on that ground comes from.

Somebody nine miles west of that clay bank has been paying a woman nine miles east of it for eleven years, in clay, out of a place she has never seen and is not able to go, and the paying has never once been late, and the woman has never asked a question about it that was not closed on her the first time.

Rao pays a column out of somewhere she has never seen. The yard pays a woman nine miles east out of a house nine miles west. The gate pays a copper a day out of a man who has a cup and one thing he is allowed to say.

Nobody on nine days of road has ever paid anybody out of anything they have been in the same room with, and she has been living inside that fact for five months and had not seen it from the outside until a woman of fifty with a barrow stood in front of her and said four sentences about a house.

## What She Said Back

She said nothing for ten seconds.

“You are the second person in a month who has told me something in a yard that I did not ask about,” she said, “and the other one filled a hole with his own barrow at seven in the morning.”

The woman looked at her.

“Do not thank me,” she said. “I have a clay bank and eleven miles of road and I am going up it again.”
