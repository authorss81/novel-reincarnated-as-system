# What Her Hand Would Not Do

The ground on the low side of that gate had gone soft since the twentieth of a month and she lost about half a copper a day off the twelfth of the month beginning 914, and she found it back inside three days.

Here is how.

A drain is dug along the low side and the depth is set at the near end, and the near end is the high end of the ground, and the bar goes in at the far end where the ground is softest and where the last two feet of a four-foot cut are the last two feet of the whole of it.

So she dug it in two lengths.

Four feet for the first six feet of the run, and three and a half for the last fourteen, with a step down between them the width of her foot. The low side stayed three and a half, which is what Pei had told her twice, and the bar went in at the near end where the ground was hard, and the bar is only ever in the ground at the near end because that is where you stand.

She worked it out at the gate on the twelfth at the ninth hour with a bar in her right hand and four fingers and a thumb on it, and she went back down the road a hundred yards and came back and dug the first six feet at four feet in an hour and a quarter and got the rest at three and a half in an hour.

That is the same copper and it is two hours instead of two and a half.

## The Third Of A Month

The thirteenth, at the third hour, with her strip in her left hand, because going up that stair with something in her hands had been the arrangement for a week and she was not going to break it now.

Rao had the same open book in front of her and the same pencil and did not look up, and then she said:

“The eleventh is still the wrong one in the copy.”

“It is.”

“You put a ten in the margin on the eleventh,” Rao said. “With your mark under it. That was right.”

“Yes.”

She turned a page.

“I have looked at it twice since,” she said, “and it is the only margin correction on this floor in a year that is in a hand I cannot check, and that is not a complaint about your hand, because you write like everybody writes. It is that I cannot look at it and know.”

## What She Said About It

She said the thing she had worked out on the walk up the mill road, which took an hour and a half and which was true.

“If I put a wrong date in a margin here,” she said, “I cannot. I do not know when that stopped. I found out on the eleventh and I have not found out anything else about it.”

Rao put the pencil down flat.

“That is not a strange thing to say to me,” she said, “and it is not a thing I can help you with, and I want you to understand the second one before the first one.”

She put the pencil flat on the page.

“This floor does not check hands,” she said. “There is one hand on it and it is the one that holds this pencil, and it has been the same for sixteen years, and everything anybody has ever relied on up here was relied on because there was no other hand in the room to put a false thing in.”

She picked the pencil up again.

“If you are telling me that you are not safe to put a margin in, then you should not be putting margins in, and I am not going to ask you to, and I would rather you worked that out for yourself than took it from a woman of forty-five with a thumb gone.”

## What She Asked For On The Way Down

She asked it at the bottom of the nine steps with her strip in her left hand and the door open onto the room below and the man with the brush three steps up.

Not out loud. She had said out loud twice in seven months and it had cost a column of hours and a set of figures going nine days of road in a hand she cannot read.

She said it to the boards at the second hour on the fourteenth and got it out in one line, the way she gets everything out.

A line of her own, in a book on that ground, in the ordinary hand.

## What That Is

On the thirteenth she stood at the works board and read it eleven times and the ditch line was still second from the bottom, by the length, two weeks, and a man with a rule in his hand four miles east had put it there and had not put her name anywhere near it.

On the fourteenth she went up the mill road again and sat at the edge of the table with her strip and twenty books open in front of her, and at the fourth hour she asked Rao the question she had been carrying for four days.

She had decided on the walk up that she was going to ask it out loud, because a question said out loud on a floor with open books on it is not a thing entered in a room. That is the difference between the two, and the difference is that a question is a question, and she has been putting questions into rooms for seven months.

She said it out loud, because she had worked out standing at the bottom of the stair that a question said out loud on a floor with open books on it is not the same as a thing entered in a room, and that there is a difference and that the difference is that a question is a question.

“How much is a line.”

Rao did not look up for four seconds.

“Four coppers,” she said.

“For a line of my own. In the ordinary hand. In a book.”

“For anybody’s own,” Rao said. “That is the price on this floor and it has been four coppers since before I came on it, and it is four coppers because a line is four coppers and not because of who it is. That is the whole of the pricing on this floor and I have never known a person try to move it.”

She put the pencil down.

“You have eleven coppers.”

“I have eight. I bought bread on the sixth of the month and I have got more than four.”

“You have more than four,” Rao said, “and that is not the part you came up here to say.”

## What She Came Up There To Say

“I cannot read it,” Ning Yan said.

“There. That is the part.”

“If a person writes a line of mine in a book on this ground, it goes in and I never see it. I do not know what shape it is. I do not know how many lines are at the foot of it. I do not know what somebody would have said about me in whatever room drew it up.”

She put her left hand flat on the table.

“And nobody on nine days of road can write it and read it. Not you. Not the man on the stair below you. Nobody inside nine days of road would sit at this table and say to me what it says.”

“That is what I have spent seven months learning,” she said. “How many lines. How wide. Whether there is a rule under the last of them. Two lines came off a column nine days of road east about five weeks ago and I could not read one word of it, and it was the first thing in six months that came back toward me.”

Rao listened to the whole of that with her pencil down.

Then she said: “I am not going to talk you out of that and I am not going to talk you into it. It is four coppers and it is four coppers for everybody, and you have got more than four, and that is the end of what I know about it.”

## The Plain Part About One Person

She is eighteen years old and there is one line on nine days of road that has a price on it and she has asked about it, and the price is four coppers, and she has eight.

And there is no hand on nine days of road that can write a line and read it back to her. Not the man with the brush, who has told her so. Not the man behind the counter. Not the man with a rule in his hand four miles east. Not the man with the paste pot two miles up the mill road.

She could have paid it. She worked that out standing at the edge of the table, and she could have put four coppers down at the end of that afternoon, and that is the part she is going to be carrying for a while, because for seven months the reason she could not have anything has been that she has nothing, and it turns out that on the one thing that has a price on it she has eight coppers and the price is four.

And her own hand, which could have been one of those hands, sat at a table on the eleventh of a month and wrote a four five times over when it was set to write a seven.

So the thing she wanted, which is a line of her own that she can check, is the one thing on that ground that has a price on it and no way of being read, and the two facts are the same fact and they were the same fact before she asked.

## What She Carried Down The Road

She walked down the mill road at the fifth hour with her strip in her left hand and eight coppers in the fold in the inside of the door frame, and got to the shed at the seventh and put a lump out of the box in at the low end of the ring.

It came up a finger’s width and stopped.

She did it four times in an hour and it stopped at the same place every one of the four, and it was the same fire it had been on the eleventh, and she did not do it a fifth time because she had a number.

Thirty-one days.

She has had one fire in fifty-two days come up, and it came up in a low building at the sixth hour on the first of a month with a man’s hand flat on the floor of it.
