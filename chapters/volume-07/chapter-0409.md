# Copied Upward

The first of a month beginning 1,034 went up the mill road at the sixth hour with a tray on it and a cord round the tray, and she stood a hundred yards short of the counting house and watched it go, and she did not follow it up the hill, because she has followed nothing up that road in seven months and she was not going to start on the first of a month.

The tray comes back down on the ninth at the sixth hour and a man with a brush turns it over on the copying floor and copies what is on it down into the book under it, and the book under it goes up on the ninth with the next one.

In between those two things, on the first of a month, the copying floor is a room with a long table in it and a man of forty-eight at it.

He had a sheet in front of him and three copies to do before the seventh and a bundle behind him with a cord round it.

He is forty-eight and he has been on that floor eleven years and he copies down.

## What Was On The Table

The sheet in front of him had a woman’s hours on it against thirty days in an ordinary hand, and he was copying them down into the book under it and he did not stop when she came in.

He did not look up for about a minute and a half and then he did.

“You have been on this floor,” he said.

“Three times.”

“I know. I copy what is on the tray down into the book under it and the book under it goes up on the ninth with the next one and it has gone that way since before you came.”

He turned the sheet round.

“This one has not got the ordinary thing on it.”

## Four Sheets Going Up

There were four sheets on the long table that were not the ordinary copying, and they had been on it since the second.

They were clean and square and they had been ruled into a column and a margin, and the column had four hundred and some lines of dates and hours in them, and the margin had a rule under the last of them with nothing at the end of it, and every one of those four hundred lines was in a hand he did not have.

He had ink on the side of his right hand and there was a blot on the second sheet at the bottom of the margin where the pen had sat.

“I do not know what this hand is,” he said. “I have been on this floor eleven years and I cannot read one word of it and I do not want to.”

“You are copying it out.”

“No. They are already written. Somebody else wrote those four and I am not being asked to make them, I am being asked to put them in the bundle.”

## Mouth Five

He put the brush down in the groove and squared the second sheet.

“I will tell you what I can tell you about it because you have not asked me and I would rather say it than have you work it out. Four sheets go up from here on the first of a month. They have gone up on the first of a month since before I came.”

“I do not make them. I am not told to make them. They come off the cart on the second already written and I put them in the bundle, and the bundle goes up, and I never see the rest of it.”

He picked the brush up again.

“Something has come down on a road this month that wanted copies made of it going the other way, and the copies are on this table, and there are four of them, and no one in this building has asked me for them.”

## The Ordinary Work Of The Floor

The copying floor of that building is a long room with three desks in it and a table along one wall and a boy of sixteen who brings the rolls up nine steps and puts them down and goes back down, and it smells of ink and hot paper and has done for eleven years.

A man of forty-eight does about nine hundred lines a month down into the book under the tray. Every one of them is a day and a set of hours against it, and every one of them goes up on the ninth with a bundle and comes down on the first, and he has never once had to read any of them to do it.

He does not like that and he has never said so out loud in that room, and on the first of a month she sat at the end of the long table for an hour and a quarter while he worked and he talked about a copying floor the way a man talks about a job.

“If you can count and you can copy and you cannot write, you can work here for the rest of your life and there is not one thing that will ever come to you about it.”

“All right.”

“That is not a warning. I have been doing it since I was twenty-nine and I have never wanted anything else, and I would not know what to do with a thing that came to me.”

## The Tray And The Cord

The tray goes up on the first at the sixth hour and comes down on the ninth at the sixth hour and it has a cord round it, and it has gone that way for longer than anybody in that building has been alive, and nobody carries it by hand.

It goes on the cart. The cart goes up the mill road with the mill on the left of the whole road and comes back down the same way at the ninth hour, and the tray comes off it at the gate and a man with a brush turns it over.

She asked him who put the cord on.

“The boy brings it up and I cord it and it goes out and it comes back and I take the cord off and the boy takes it down.”

“Who opens it.”

“Nobody. It is not a sealed thing. It is a bundle of pages and it has been eleven hundred years without a seal and everything anybody on this ground has ever wanted to keep out of a book has got into it anyway.”

## The Part He Would Not Say

She asked him what the hand on them was, because she has carried a strip in her coat for five months with a word at the head of it she cannot read, and there are four sheets on that table in a hand she cannot read, and she has never in her life been in a room where a thing she cannot read was being put into a bundle to go up a road.

He looked at her for about two seconds.

“That is the hand you have had,” he said. “I cannot tell you that. I have been asked twice in eleven years by two different people to say what it is and I have said both times that I do not know, and both times they went away satisfied, and both times I did not know.”

He turned the sheet back over.

“But I will tell you what I do know, which is that whoever it is can read it, because somebody is paying for it to go up, and on nine days of road the only hand anybody pays for that I have never been able to read is that one.”

## The Plain Part About One Person

She is eighteen years old and she has spent five months carrying a word she cannot read against her ribs, and she has spent two volumes wanting to be in a room with the man whose trade it is, and on the first of a month she has been in a room where a hand she cannot read was being copied four times over into four clean sheets, and the man doing it could not tell her what it was and did not want to.

There are four sheets of her on a table on that floor and they are going up.

She did not ask to read them and she did not ask who had sent them, and she stood at the end of the long table for about a minute and a quarter and then she went down the stair and out of the door.

## The Second

She was at the yard at the turn at the sixth hour on the second at the low end of the sill course with the second course going on over it, and there were nine men on the north end and the man with a rule in his hand had her page.

He had it because the bundle had gone out with it at the fourth hour that morning for the turn, which is a yard four miles east of the counting house and is one of four yards whose pages go up that stair once a month.

He did not hand it to her. He put it on the low side facing up with his foot, the way he does.

“There is a fourth line on it,” he said.

“Yes.”

“In chalk. In my hand.”

“Yes.”

“I have written the margin on that page since the spring before the wheel stopped,” he said, “and I have never once written a thing on it that a man at the north end could read as being about the yard, and I did that on the twenty-seventh of a month with four words and a straight stick of chalk and a yard full of men, and I have not stopped thinking about it since, and I would do it again.”

He put his foot on the corner of it.

“It goes up on the ninth and a man with a brush will copy it into a book that shuts, and in about four days there will be a thing in a book on this ground that a woman at a gate said out loud.”
