# Four Columns Off The Road

The third of a month beginning 1,034 was the fourth hour and there were four columns off the road on the top floor of the counting house for the first time since the wheel stopped turning, and she was at the near end of the table with her strip in her left hand, because going up that stair with something in her hands has been the arrangement since the eighth of a month four months back and she was not going to break it on the third.

Rao had the same pencil and the same knife and forty books open on it and she was at the far end of a table the length of nine men.

“Four,” she said. “Four yards. Yours is not on that list and has not been on it since before you came and is not going to be on it this season.”

“All right.”

“You are here to read.”

“Yes.”

## What She Read

Thirty days and the hours against them, and at the head of the hours one word in an ordinary hand that said good, and a season marked at the first end and not marked at the second end because the season had not finished.

She read the first one out loud from the top, dates down, hours against them, stop at the end, in the ordinary voice, and it took four minutes and there was one other living thing in the room and it got warm for the length of it and went cold when she stopped.

“You are quicker than the boy,” Rao said. “I said that on the ninth of a month and it is still true and it is still not a compliment.”

“Yes.”

“Read the second one. From the top. And I want the days only. I do not want the hours, I have the hours.”

She read the days.

## The Four Of Them

There are four yards on that ground whose pages go up the nine steps once a month, and there have been four for as long as there has been a third floor, and they are the yard at the turn, a yard four miles west on the mill road, a yard nine miles east where the clay is loaded, and a yard at the end of the made road where two men cut drain and have never had a season.

Rao set every one of them against the book underneath it with a pencil and a knife, and the setting takes forty minutes a page and she does it standing up, and she does not read the writing while she does it.

“The hours are the hours,” she said. “I do not look at them. I look at where the pen lifted and whether the page has been folded and whether the hand has gone small at the end of a run, and that tells me whether the person was still writing it by the thirtieth or whether somebody else finished it for them.”

She turned a page over.

“A column is a day and an hour repeated for thirty days and then a season written at the head of it, and it gets read out loud exactly twice in the whole of its life. Once when the man who keeps the book on that floor reads it to check it, and once when he has to stand in a room and say out loud what a person did in a month.”

“Who is the second one.”

“You are, apparently, on the fourth, the eleventh, the eighteenth and the twenty-fourth, at the fourth hour, for nothing, because this ground does not pay for reading and I have been saying that for sixteen years to anybody who stands at that end of this table.”

That was the whole of the arrangement and it took about ninety seconds and she had said it without looking up.

## What She Felt About It

She had been reading a page out loud in front of nine men at a yard, and she had been asked to do it by a man who could not stop her getting a date wrong.

On this floor there was a woman of forty-five who had been reading them for sixteen years, with forty books open on a table the length of nine men, and she had just handed the work to a girl of eighteen for nothing, in the ordinary voice, on a day when four columns came off the road.

Both of those were reading. One of them cost money and one of them did not, and she worked out at the near end of that table that the one that cost money was the one that put a line in a book, and the one that did not was the one that put her in a room four times a month with a woman in it.

## The Thing She Found

Twenty-nine days out of the second one were there in a row and the thirtieth was not, and that is a column that has not finished, and Rao had it open and had known that before she came up the stair.

But there was a gap in the middle of it, on the seventeenth, and the seventeenth of a month beginning 974 was the day a dressed stone went the length of her own arm out of a bed at the south end of the yard at the turn with a man’s leg under it.

There were no hours against the seventeenth. There was the date and the mark of a man at the head of a line, and nothing under it at all.

“I do not think that is a day off,” she said.

“No.”

“It is the seventeenth of a month and it has a man’s mark on it and there is nothing under the mark.”

“No.”

“That is not a day anybody did nothing.”

“That is the correct reading of it and you are the first person to give it to me in three months,” Rao said, “and I have had that page on this table since the second and I have decided three times that I was going to stop looking at it.”

## The Fourth Line

She put the page down and turned over the sheet that had been under it.

There were two lines at the foot of that one in the ordinary hand of a building two miles west, and under them, in the margin, in a yard-book hand, in chalk, four words.

Read by a person at the rail.

Rao read it. She read it the way she reads everything, which is without looking at the writing.

“There,” she said. “That is a margin note in a yard-book hand about something a woman said at a gate. It is in my table this morning and it came off a cart and it has not been in this building before, and there is not a yard-book hand in it.”

“It’s his hand.”

“It is a man’s hand and he was at a yard at the turn and I have been setting that yard’s pages against the book underneath since before you came.” She put the pencil down. “He does not write many. He wrote two in three months and one of them was a correction of my arithmetic.”

She turned round and looked at the wall at the end of the table, which is what she does when she is about to do something she has decided to do.

## What She Did About It

She opened the drawer under the table and took out a slip of paper about the length of her hand and she wrote on it in pencil, in the ordinary hand, one line.

It says, in the ordinary hand of that floor: read out loud at the south end of the yard at the turn, at the sixth hour, the twenty-seventh of a month beginning 1,004. Said by a person at the gate.

And she put the slip in beside the column and squared it to the edge of the table with the back of the knife and turned round and picked the pencil up again.

“There,” she said. “That is a date on it now. It goes in with the season’s papers and it is in a book on this floor and it shuts and it is not a line of yours and it is not four coppers.”

## The Plain Part About One Person

She is eighteen years old and she has wanted a line of her own in a book on this ground since the fourteenth of a month two months back, and it is four coppers, and she has had more than four twice and has not paid it.

And on the third of a month, at the fourth hour, a woman of forty-five wrote down a day and a place and what was said, in a hand that is not hers, and put it in beside a column, and did not charge her for it and did not ask.

She has one piece now. She did not ask for it, she did not refuse it, and she cannot put it back, because a thing in a book with a date against it is not a thing anybody takes out.

That was the fourth time in four months that something had come to her out of a room she was standing in, and three of the four were about a cup.

## The Plain Part About A Woman Of Forty-Five

Rao did not say thank you and she did not say anything else about it for about eleven minutes.

Then she said, without looking up and in the ordinary voice:

“Do not come on the first of a month for this. The first is a cart day and there are nine men on that floor by the fourth hour and I have to have my hands free. Come on the fourth and the eleventh and the eighteenth and the twenty-fourth, at the fourth hour, and read what I put in front of you and nothing else, and do not read down the margin.”

“No.”

“I have told you that to keep you off the margin and not because I do not want you on it.”

She turned the page.

“There is one line on this floor that is about you and it is a refusal and I read it back to you on the eighth of a month and you have not asked me about it once since, and I want that to go on being true, because the day you ask me about it is the day somebody up this road finds out that there is a person in this building who has asked.”
