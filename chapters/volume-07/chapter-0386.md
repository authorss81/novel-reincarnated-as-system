# The Ditch Line Is Listed

The word the man who kept the list wrote in the ruled margin at the left of that board on the nineteenth was a date. The date was the twenty-third of a month, and he wrote it at the ninth hour with four men round him and did not read it out, because a date in a margin is not a line on a board.

She read it at the tenth hour from four feet off with her hands in her sleeves.

## The Yard, The Twentieth

Four miles east at the turn: brick and timber, a covered bay, a bar against the end post, and nine men on a board where four would do.

He was at the bench with the cover off when she came into the bay, and the rule went against his leg before she was four feet in.

“The ditch line came up off the works board on the thirteenth,” he said, “and it is at the north end, and it is four hundred yards by the length, and it has to be off on the twenty-third.”

“It is two weeks.”

“It is two weeks for four men,” he said, “and I have four men on it, and it is the twenty-third because the low side of this ground goes at the end of a month and there is no ditch dug in wet that will stand past it. That is the whole of it and it is not going to move because a woman reads boards.”

He went to the bench.

“The sill line goes four miles on the twenty-second,” he said, “and it takes the cart and the man on the ditch, because he is the only one of the four who can stand a cart bed, and I am not stopping a sill line for a ditch. That leaves me three men on two weeks of ditch.”

“I know that.”

“You know it because you stood in that gateway and read it,” he said. “I am telling you what I am going to do about it and I am telling you now so that it is not a thing that happened to you tomorrow.”

## What He Was Going To Do About It

“I am going to ask you to come down that ditch on the twenty-first,” he said, “and I am not putting you on this board and your name is not going anywhere in this yard, and you are not on my nine, and if you come down that cut on the twenty-first you are a woman who came down it and the four men on it will be on it and the ditch will get done.”

He picked the rule up.

“I have asked you twice in two months for two things,” he said, “and I have been right about both of them and I have not asked either of them twice. This is the first time I have asked you for something I can measure.”

## The Twenty-Third Is Three Days Off

She did the gate on the twentieth, went back up the mill road at the third hour, and sat at the edge of the table for an hour. She read three columns of dates out loud because a woman of forty-five asked her to, and her hands were warm for the whole of it and cold on the road down.

And on the twentieth at the second hour she lay on the boards and worked the whole of it out standing up, which she could do about a ditch in nine seconds.

Four hundred yards by the length. Four feet. A bar in the left hand because the bar takes two hands and she has not got two hands, and the right arm open the whole way, which is the arm that has been open half the time since a face let go on her in a cut a long time back and is open every minute of every day.

Three days off. Six hours of it a day.

## The Twenty-First, The North End

There were three men in it when she got there at the sixth hour.

The ditch is at the north end of that yard and it is four feet and it has been standing open since about the tenth of a month and there is about two feet of soft in the bottom of it where the frost has been at it.

She got down into it with the left hand on the lip and the right arm up over her head, which is how a person gets into a four-foot cut with four fingers and a thumb.

They did not send her to the far end. One of the three looked down the line, said “there,” and pointed ninety yards off, and she went.

The work is by the length and it is the only work on that board she could ever have taken. A bar under the soft, and a heave, and it comes up in length, and you put it on the bank. It is not heavy. It is not stone. It does not weigh anything and it is not two hands and it is not paid by the ton.

She did sixty yards in the first three hours and it came up in length nine times out of ten. The tenth time she got the foot of it up wrong and forty yards came up in pieces instead, and she went back and did it again with her right arm hanging and her left hand doing everything.

## The Fifth Hour

She was at a hundred and forty yards when the side came in.

It came in off the north face of the cut, above her head, about a yard and a half back from where she was standing, and it came in the way a face that has been standing open since the tenth of a month comes in, which is all at once and without a noise.

It went onto her from about the left shoulder and it was about a yard and a half of wet clay in about a second and a half.

Her right arm was between it and the wall.

She got out on her knees with her left hand. That is the whole of it, and it took four seconds.

What she did with her right arm on the way was put it above her head, because that is what a person does when there is something coming onto them. It is the arm that was already open, and it went from open to the other thing, where it does not answer at all, for two seconds, and then it came back.

She came out on her knees on the bank with clay from her hair to her boots and one of the three men standing about four feet off looking down at her and not moving.

“I am all right,” she said.

He said, “I did not say anything.”

## What She Did After That

She sat on the bank for ten minutes.

Then she got back down into the cut with the left hand and went back about fifteen yards and started again on the face that had come in, and she did the last hundred and sixty yards of that ditch alone over about five hours with her right arm hanging against her side and her left hand doing the whole of it.

She got the foot up right the first time on every one of the hundred and sixty, and she put every length of it on the bank, and there was nothing in a barrow about it and no barrow on that ground.

She came out of the cut at the fourth hour on the twenty-second, and the man with the rule in his hand was standing at the top of the north end with the rule in his pocket and his arms folded.

“You should have come out,” he said.

“I came out.”

“You came out and then you got back in,” he said. “That is not the same as not coming out. I am not going to make an argument with a woman about her arm and I have not got the right to.”

## Four Feet, Four Hundred Yards, By The Length

It was off on the twenty-second, a day early, and the man with the rule in his hand walked the whole of it at the seventh hour with two of the men who had been in it. It took him two hours and he did not ask her to walk any part of it with him.

She sat on the bank at the top of the north end for the two hours with her right arm across her knees and watched, and there were four hundred yards of four-foot ditch in front of her that were not there on the nineteenth of a month, and no person on nine days of road was ever going to know that she had done two hundred yards of it.

Then he took the cover off the board and rubbed the line out with his own thumb and wrote nothing in the margin, and there was no rule under the last of it and a rubbed patch where it had been.

That is a listed piece of work closed and checked and off: a listed line, by the length, four feet, four hundred yards, north end, between the twentieth and the twenty-second of a month.

She was not on the board and there is no name of hers in that yard and there never has been and there is not going to be.

He came past her on the way out and stopped for a moment.

“You were not on that line,” he said.

“No.”

“It got done,” he said, “and it got done a day early, and there is nothing on that board or in this yard that says you were on it, and I am going to walk out of here and not think about that again, because I have got a sill line on the twenty-second and four men in a yard and no room in my head for it.”

He walked out.

## The Plain Part About One Person

She is eighteen. She has been in a four-foot cut for two days with a right arm that is open every minute, and it went into the other thing for two seconds on the twenty-first at the fifth hour, and she is not going to lose it and she does not know yet whether she will be able to close a fist with it before the end of the month.

No person inside nine days of road could have told her that was coming, and she could not have told a living soul inside nine days of road afterwards.

A man with a rule in his hand walked two hundred yards of a ditch at the seventh hour on the twenty-second and checked it and rubbed it off his own board with his own thumb, and a listed piece of work is off a listed board, and the whole of it is going to be gone by morning.

That is the fourth time in seven months. That is what a yard is.
