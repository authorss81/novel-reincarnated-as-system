# The Rule With Nothing At The End

She walked five miles west at the sixth hour on the ninth of a month with fifteen coppers in the fold and came back with four loaves and seven coppers, and she was at the gate by the eighth hour with a bar in her right hand and four fingers and a thumb on it, and she worked the two lengths on the way up in her head and got the same answer as the day before.

It came up easy. She filled in the four feet at the near end and did three and a half the rest of it and it was a copper by the ninth hour and Pei was inside and did not come out.

She went up the mill road at the third hour on the eleventh with the strip in her left hand and she was warm inside ten minutes and stayed warm for an hour and a half.

## The Thing She Went Up There For

She had one question and she had had it since the eighth of a month at the fifth hour, and she had walked four times with it.

The rule underneath the middle of her column had nothing at the end of it. In one in six of the columns on that floor there is a name at the end of that rule, and in hers there is nothing, and the nothing is six months old at that sitting and it was four months old the first time she had been in the building.

She sat down at the edge of the table with her hands flat on it and did not open a bundle.

Rao read for an hour and then put the pencil down flat.

“You have brought me a question and you have had it since Friday,” she said. “Ask it.”

“Where is the money that pays for a column.”

## What Rao Said

Rao did not look up and she did not stop to think about it, and she said it the way she reads a page, four sentences and no more, and then she went back to the page.

“What is on the line is paid for out of a place I have never seen and have never had a road to.”

“A column came up off that line once in eleven years with nothing at the other end of the rule. I have never found out what that meant.”

“The room it is paid out of is not on any strip I have ever been sent and it is not in this building.”

“And whoever sends it has never once sent anything to ask.”

## The Part She Said Afterwards

She turned the page and squared it before she went on.

“I will give you the rest of that, and I am giving it to you because you are going to find it out inside a week and I would rather you had it from a person than from a page.”

“Every column that comes up this stair is a bill somebody else is paying. Not me. Not this building. The season at the head of it is their season and the hours down the margin are the hours they are buying and I do not know the price and I am not paid by the hour.”

She looked up.

“That is the whole of what I know about where the money comes from and I have known it for sixteen years and I have never once been able to make it plainer than that.”

## The Column That Came Up Once

“Eleven years.”

“Yes.”

“What happened to the page it came up on.”

Rao put the pencil down flat again and this time she left it.

“It is not in this room.”

“No.”

“It went up the mill road on a first, into a book that is not one of mine, and it has not come back down in eleven years, and that has never happened with any other page of anything I have ever kept.” She said it without weight, the way a woman reads a line that is out of the ordinary. “I asked twice in sixteen years for a person who could read the hand at the head of it, and there has never been one in this building.”

She put her hand flat on the open book.

“And the line on it said no. Not a refusal. No, the rule underneath it had nothing at the end of it and the nothing was eleven months old by then and a man at a counter had written something about it in the ordinary hand and I have never been told what he wrote.”

## What She Did With It

She sat there for a while and let it be a room.

## The Tenth

The tenth was a yard day and she was at the turn four miles east at the sixth hour with her strip in her coat and nothing in her hands.

The man with a rule in his hand was at the office end of the yard with the board propped against the wall outside it, and he was rubbing the works lines off it with his own thumb, one at a time, the way a man rubs a thing off a board he has been using for years and does not intend to use again.

Eleven lines went that morning and eight came up. Two of the eight were for the low side of the ground and one of them was a sill line with no name on it at the top.

The woman in the office end came out with a cup of something hot in it and put it into her hands and went back in without a word, and that was the ninth time in six weeks, and she has never once asked her name and she is not going to be asked.

The man with the rule looked at the board and then at her, and he did not say anything about any of it.

She did not ask him about the sill line either. That was a thing she worked out on the walk back and not on the stand: a sill line is by the weight, it is measured in dressed stones, and the whole of the ground under it has to come out and go back, and every line on that board is a thing she cannot hold.

She is the ninth of nine men at that yard and there is no line on it with her name on it and there is not going to be.

The second end of her column was not marked. Six months of days against it and nothing at the end, and at the end of a rule under the middle of it there was nothing as well, and now there was a second woman on nine days of road whose rule had come up empty and had gone on going up a road every month for eleven years and this ground cannot say what became of it.

Which meant that an office with a page like that is not an office that gives up.

She is eighteen years old and she has been refusing something since the twenty-sixth of a month six months back, and she had worked it out that the refusal cost her nothing because it was standing in a book she could not reach. That was wrong and it had been wrong since the eighth of a month at the fifth hour, and there is nothing on nine days of road that can tell her how wrong.

## What She Said In The Room About One Thing

“You have told me where the money comes from,” she said, “and you have told me that a page went up a road eleven years ago and has not come down, and you have told me that a woman cannot check any of it.”

“Yes.”

“That is three things a person could pay a great deal not to know and you have given them to me for nothing.”

“You did not pay me,” Rao said. “You did not even ask me for them, and I am not going to be thanked, and I told you on the twenty-eighth of a month that I would not be.”

## The Plain Part About One Person

She wanted to ask whether the page was still up the road. She did not ask it, because a question asked at the fourth hour on the top floor of one building goes into a room, and she had four questions left in her that she had not asked and was not going to spend on one she already knew the answer to.

The answer was yes. It had been going up on every first for eleven years and every person between the mill and a gate four miles east of it is in no position to stop it.

## What She Carried Down The Road

She was down the nine steps and eleven and a hundred yards down the mill road at the fifth hour with seven coppers in the fold and four loaves in her coat and the strip against her ribs.

The fire came up in her shed at the seventh hour and stood at the width of a finger.

It did not stop there.

It came up the brick to the height of her chest and filled the corner the way it had filled a corner on the first of a month six months back, and the shed was warm for the first time since the second of a month, and she stood in the doorway of a shed with a barrow-load of clay against the outside wall and did not go in for about a minute.

Then she went in and put another lump in at the low end of the ring and stood there until it had burned down to nothing.

Nothing came up with it.

She had forty days of that and she had told herself, and been told, that it was because there was not another living thing in the shed. She had never once gone and stood in a room with a woman and wondered whether it was the room.

A woman of forty-five on the top floor of a building two miles down the mill road had put her pencil down flat and said it was warm under her hand, and she had not gone on about it, and that was the whole of it.

And now the shed was warm, and the shed is not a room, and she stood in a doorway and understood that something had come up under the brick while there was nothing else inside nine days of road to put a hand on the floor.

She did not work that out that night. She worked it out on the eighteenth of a month, in a yard four miles east of the mill, with nine men on it and two of them nine feet off her, and what she worked out was about a yard, and it cost a stone that cannot go back.

A yard is a room.
