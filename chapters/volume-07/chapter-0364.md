# Read Out Of The Room

She had worked out on the twenty-seventh that a thing an office cannot fetch a person for gets read where the person is. She did not know that was a rule. She had it the way she had most things on that ground, which is that she had stood next to it twice and could not make it be anything else.

What she had seen twice was a man with a brush reading a column out of a book to another man across a counter, so that the other man did not have to be able to read it.

The first time had been in a room with a stove in it, on the wrong side of a counter, and she had been the thing being read to. The second had been from the bottom of a stair nine days of road west, through a doorway, with a man reading a column out of a book to another man who did not want to come and look at it.

Both of those had been a person being read to a person. Neither of them had been a board on a wall.

She wanted to see whether it happened out of doors.

## The Twenty-Ninth, The Ninth Hour

She was in the gateway at the works at the ninth hour on the twenty-ninth because that is where she went now, and the man who kept the list was doing a job on the bench with his sleeves turned up and did not look up, and she stood at the wall with the board cover on beside her and nothing to do.

Two men came up the made road out of the east at the ninth hour. One of them she knew, because he had taken a ditch line off that board in the second month of the season she was not living through. The other one was in a yard’s coat with the sleeves sewn down at the wrist, and she did not know him at all, and he was stopping at the board.

The one she knew came past her to the wall and did not look at her. The other was about thirty and stood with his weight on one hip the way a man stands when it comes off carrying timber and not from a manner, and he waited about ten feet short of the bench, which is what a man does who has come for one line and not for a day.

He stopped at the board the way a man stops at a well.

The man who kept the list put the brush down.

“I have a line for you.”

“Read it.”

## Read Out Of The Room

He read it out loud.

Not to the yard’s man. To the gateway. He turned round off the bench and read it into the air of the gateway, standing up, at about the volume of a man saying a price.

He read the length and the place and the day and the word at the end of it that said what it paid in, and he read all of it at once and he read it fast, because he had read it out loud in that gateway before and it was a thing he had got quick at.

The yard’s man listened with his head on one side and then said it back.

He said it back with the figures in it. Four hundred and something of length, at the low side of a piece of ground the name of which she knew and the man she had never seen, and it paid in a length of timber and not in a box, because it was not a cut.

That was all of it. It took two minutes and a half and it happened at the wall of a gateway on the made road with the light going.

The man who kept the list wrote it on a slip afterwards, and the slip went into the yard’s man’s hand, and the yard’s man put it inside his coat and went east.

## What She Watched

She had watched two men agree about a thing in a room nine days of road west of here, four times in six months, and she had not been able to do one thing about any of them.

That was not the same as what she had just watched.

A man in a room gets a line out of a room by being fetched to it, or by a book going up a stair to somebody who can read it, and every one of those had her name at some part of it, and every one of them had ended at a counter or at a board in a building she could walk to.

That had not ended at a board. That had ended with a slip inside a yard’s coat going east up the made road at the ninth hour, and the man who had taken it had said the figures back from memory and had not written anything down, and there was nothing on that slip but the length and the place and the day.

The reading had left the room and gone to a person.

A book goes up a stair to somebody who can read it and the answer comes down the same road a fortnight later. That wants two rooms and two men and a cord round it, and it takes a month of days.

What she had just watched wanted one man, a gateway and ten feet of made road, and it took about nine words.

That was the whole of what happened on the twenty-ninth and she stood in that gateway for about four minutes after the two of them had gone and let it arrive.

## Why She Had Not Seen It Before

She worked that out on the walk west, by the eleventh hour, with nothing in her hands and the last of the light on the flat.

Because for six months every reading that had touched her had been about a person. Her column, her holding, her refusal, her name at the left of a page with a day against it. Every one of those had a person inside it, and every one of those had wanted a person brought to it, and she had been the person, and she had refused, and the refusal had gone into the only book on nine days of road that shuts.

This one had no person in it at all.

Six months, four pieces of paper, and every one of them had had a hand on it deciding where she would go next.

That is what a reading is for. A reading is a person being sent somewhere. And she had spent six months in rooms where the person was her, and the last of those had been the twenty-sixth of a month when she had said no in front of nine men.

A line of four hundred and something of length at the low side of a piece of ground is not a person, and there is nothing in it to fetch anybody for. It sits in a book and waits for the ground to want it, and when the ground wants it the only thing anybody has to do is read it to the man who is going to carry it away.

She had been reading a room wrong for six months and it had taken a board on the wall of a gateway to show her.

## At The Second Hour

She said it out loud on the boards at the second hour with the ring burning nine feet off.

“They do not have to send for anybody.”

“You have been carrying that for two days,” he said. “A man on the twenty-eighth tells you he cannot read it, and you have known since the twenty-eighth what a reading does when it cannot get the person it wants.”

She lay there and did not argue with it.

“That is it,” she said.

“Then stop asking the question and watch the thing,” he said. “You are going to get more out of the next four weeks out of a man at a board than out of every office you have walked to since you came past this mill.”

That was three sentences and every one of them was in the present, and he had not said one word about the strip, or the fine hand, or the four fingers, or the eight holds, or the four counts of breath, and he had not bought one thing.

She lay on the boards and listened to a fire that had come up on its own at about the second hour that morning and had not needed her at any hour of the day.

## The Thirtieth

She was in the yard of the works at the ninth hour on the thirtieth because she had walked three miles for a board with a cover on it and the day was the last day of a month.

The counter’s boy came out with the tray on his own and put it on the cart and put a cord round it, and the cord went over the top of a book with a page fanned open two inches from the back, and he did not tie it twice, because it had been tied three times and come down three times and there was no longer any point in the second knot.

“The sixth hour in the morning,” the boy said, out loud to the yard, the way a boy says a thing he says every month.

“I know when it goes,” she said.

He looked at her and then went back in.

There was a stack at the back of that yard four times the height of the boy, with a tarred cloth over the top of it and the corners of the cloth moving in the wind, and the light was still on the top of it.

It was the last day of a month and the whole of that building had stopped for a minute and a half and then had not stopped at all.

She stood in that yard until the light had gone off the top of the stack and then she stood there a while longer with her hands in her sleeves, because she had decided something about half an hour ago and had not noticed herself deciding it.

The sixth hour is early. The sixth hour is before the gate and before the store and before the reach, and it is eleven days of walking from the ninth hour of the twenty-ninth.

She was going to be standing in that yard at the sixth hour on the first of a month and she had a week’s bread and half a box of clay and one copper a day, and the reason she was going to be standing in it was a strip folded in two against her ribs with four lines in the middle of it in a hand she cannot read.
