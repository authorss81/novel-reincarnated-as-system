# A Margin In His Own Hand

The twenty-seventh of a month beginning 1,004 was a yard day and she was at the turn four miles east at the sixth hour with four coppers in the fold and nothing in her hands, and there were nine men on the north end and a second course going on over the sill that had gone down at the end of the month before, and it was holding, and a man checked it every morning and had never once mentioned it to anybody.

There is no reason for a woman of eighteen to be at that yard on the twenty-seventh. There has not been a reason since the fourth of a month two months back, when the man with a rule in his hand looked at her arm for two seconds and worked out where she went among nine men.

He told her what she could do and what she could not, out loud, in front of all of them, so that it would not be a thing that happened to her later.

He came down the yard at the seventh hour with a page in his hand and did not say good morning, because he has never once said good morning.

## What He Wanted

“Read that.”

He put it on the low side of the sill course with his own hand flat on it, and it was a yard-book page and it had been off the mill road and back, and it had two lines in an ordinary hand at the foot of it under his own margin note.

The margin note is in his hand. It is the same hand that has been writing on the margins at that yard for twenty-two years and it says that two holders are now required against the seventeenth of a month and that there is one holder on this ground.

Under that there were two more lines and neither of them was in his hand.

“All right,” she said, and she did not pick it up, because he had his hand flat on it.

“The two under mine.”

“Those are not in your hand.”

“No.”

“And they are not in anybody’s hand in this yard,” he said, “and I have had that page on the bench twice and there are nine men on this ground and the nine of them cannot tell you what is written under me.”

## What She Could And Could Not Do

She read the top of it out loud, in the ordinary voice, because she has read a page out loud in front of nine men before and it is the only thing on this ground she can do that a bar is not for.

Twenty-nine lines of work, two of them by the weight and twenty-seven by the length, and the days against them, and the margin note in his own hand at the foot.

That took two minutes and eleven seconds and four of the nine men turned round and listened to it, which they had not done the first time and which she had noticed and had not decided to notice.

Then she got to the two lines under his hand and stopped.

“These are not in a hand I can read,” she said.

“You are sure.”

“There are nine letters in the first one and I can see that it is nine and I cannot tell you one of them. It is not a bad hand. It is a hand that goes up and down on the page the way writing does, and I have never seen it before.”

## What He Said About It

He did not say anything for about nine seconds, and then he sat down on the low side of the sill course with the page on his knee, which is not a thing he does.

“I am not asking you to read it,” he said. “I have had two men past this ground who read hands for a living and I sent neither of them anything, because there is a man for it and he is not on this ground and he does not come to this ground.”

“When a thing comes off that road in a hand that none of us can read, it goes up to him, and it comes back down readable, and it has been that way since before the wheel stopped.”

He put the page down again and squared it with the edge of his boot.

“That is not the thing I want.”

“All right.”

“What I want is the twenty-nine lines and the days against them read out loud, twice a month, at the sixth hour, at that end of the yard.”

## The Price Of It

“I have got nine men here and four will do,” he said, “and there is a page a month come off that road with two hundred and some lines on it that I set against the book underneath it with my own pencil, and I have been doing it since the spring before the wheel stopped and I have never once read it out loud to anybody because there has not been anybody who reads it out loud.”

He took the chalk out of his pocket and looked at it and put it back without marking anything, which he does about four times a week.

“There is no money on this ground for reading,” he said. “Everything that is on a rail or a board or in a book in a yard is priced off the ground by the weight or by the length, and in twenty-two years I have never put a line on anything that was neither of the two, and I am not going to start at forty-eight with a woman who cannot lift the end of it.”

“Yes.”

“So it is twice a month, at the sixth hour, at that end of the yard, and there is no money in it and it is not on the board and I will not tell anybody that you do it.”

## What Changed That Morning

That was the whole of it and he said it in about a minute and then he got up off the sill course and went back to the north end and nine men went back to work.

And then he stopped about ten feet off and came back and picked the page up and wrote on the margin of it, under his own hand and under hers, in his own hand, in chalk, four words, and turned it round and put it back down facing her.

He read it out himself, in the ordinary voice, and it says: read by a person at the rail.

That is not a line of her own and it is not a column and it is not four coppers and it is not on the third floor of a counting house with forty books open on a table. It is a margin on a yard page, in a yard-book hand, in chalk, at a yard at a turn.

It goes up the mill road on the first at the sixth hour with the bundle and a cord round the tray, and it comes back down on the ninth, and a man with a brush on the floor below will copy it into a book that shuts.

## The Second Reading

She read the twenty-nine lines again on the twenty-eighth at the sixth hour and got the same both times, and on the second reading she did something she has never done at a yard, which is that she read the dates out loud and stopped at the end of each one before she read the hours against it.

That is a slower way to read a page and it takes eleven minutes instead of four, and four men at the north end of that yard stopped what they were doing for it on the second day and listened to the dates.

The man with a rule in his hand let her finish and then said one thing.

“You did that on purpose.”

“Yes.”

“The fourth hour man does it wrong. He reads the whole line and then goes back for the dates and gets them out of the shape.” He wrote a figure on his own page. “Do it that way again on the sixth of a month and I will not have to read them at all.”

That was the arrangement, and it made no money and put a line in no book at all, and it was the first time in seven months that a man on that ground had asked her to do the thing she is actually good at.

## The Plain Part About One Person

She is eighteen years old and for seven months everything anybody has written about her has been written by somebody else, in a hand she cannot read, in a room she has not been in.

And on the twenty-seventh of a month, at a yard at a turn, in chalk, a man wrote down a thing she said, in his own hand, under his own hand.

It cost her nothing. Her name was not asked for and she was not put at the head of a line and there was no money in it and there was no line in it.

And it is the first thing she has ever said in this world that is going into a book that shuts, and she did not have to pay four coppers for it and she did not have to say yes to anything.

## The Twenty-Eighth

She read twenty-nine lines out loud twice on the twenty-eight at the sixth hour at that end of the yard and got both of them the same both times, and the man with a rule in his hand did not correct her once.

She dug the run at the gate from the seventh to the ninth hour and Pei came out at the ninth hour and stood at the end of it the way he stands, and she had five coppers in the fold in the inside of the door frame.

And on the twenty-ninth she dug the run, and on the thirtieth she dug the run, and on the first of a month the tray goes up the mill road with a cord round it, and there was a name in it and there had been a name in it on every first of a month for two months.

There would be a fourth line on that page now, in chalk, in a yard-book hand, and it was not a name and it was not about her hours and it cost four coppers nowhere on nine days of road.

She had asked to be sent up to the long table at the top of that stair twice in four months and had never once been sent, and on the ninth of a month it was going to come back down the mill road and go into a book on the floor below, and there was going to be four words in that book with her standing at the rail in them, and she was not going to be in the room when it happened.
