# The Smallest Person On The Floor

She was at the edge of that table on the ninth of a month at the third hour with her strip in her left hand and forty books open in front of her, and the woman at the far end had not spoken to her for twenty minutes.

Rao reads with a pencil and not a pen, and she reads the way a person reads who is looking for one thing in a page of nine hundred: down the margin, up the column, across, and on.

She does not read the whole page. She has read forty thousand columns in sixteen years and she does not need to look at the writing to know what it says.

## Being Asked To Read One Out Loud

At the fourth hour she put the pencil down and turned a book round so the column faced Ning Yan.

“Read that one out loud,” she said. “From the top. Dates down, hours against them, and stop at the end.”

It was a yard column. Thirty days and the hours against them, and at the head of the hours one word in an ordinary hand that said good.

She read it out loud in the ordinary voice, the way she had read twenty-nine dates to her on the eighth, going down. It took four minutes. There was a room forty feet long, forty books open on it, and one other living thing in it.

“That is a yard column,” Rao said. “Four of those come up this stair every month from four yards and I set every one of them against the book underneath. Say it again from the twenty-second.”

She said it again from the twenty-second and got it the same both times.

“You are not reading it,” Rao said. “You are getting it out of the shape. You did the same thing to me on the eighth with a date and I made you do it twice to find out, and here you have done it thirty times without knowing that you did it.”

## The Line That Was Not In Rao’s Hand

“You are quicker than the boy,” Rao said. “That is not a compliment, it is a fact about you. You are quicker than a boy of twenty-two who has been on this floor two years, and you have been on it three days, and I do not know what to do with that, and I have not decided anything about it.”

She turned the page with the edge of the knife.

“Read the last line.”

There was a last line under the thirty days and it was not in Rao’s hand.

Ning Yan looked at it for three seconds.

“That is not the same hand as the thirty lines above it,” she said. “The thirty lines are square and that one is not. And there are two words on it and I cannot tell you either of them.”

“You cannot read it.”

“No.”

Rao put the knife down flat in the groove.

“I cannot read it and the man on the floor below me cannot read it, and neither of us has ever been able to read it, and it has been the same for as long as I have been standing at this table.”

She said it the way she said everything else, which is the way a person gives a price.

“I have been on this floor sixteen years and I have asked twice for somebody who can read that hand,” she said, “and both times the answer came back that there was nobody to send, and both times it came back that the work was not waiting.”

## What Was On The Table At The Fifth Hour

At the fifth hour a boy of about sixteen came up the nine steps with a roll under his arm and put it on the middle of the table and went down again without saying a word.

That is a bundle of strips. It is about the size of a barrow wheel and it has a cord round it, and it goes up and down this stair once a month whether anybody upstairs wants it or not.

Rao stood at the end of the table, looked at it, and did not go to it.

“That goes down to the counter to be cording checked and comes back up at the sixth hour in the morning,” she said. “I cannot carry it down nine steps or up nine steps, and there is a rail on the landing with a turn in it.”

She put both hands flat on the table.

“You are the only thing on this ground within a mile that has got two arms,” she said.

## What She Said

She stood at that end of the table with her hands flat on it for four seconds, and then she said the thing that was in her, which was that she could not carry it.

And then she said the other half of it, out loud, which is what actually happened.

“I can carry it,” she said. “I cannot carry it up a landing with a turn in it nine times in a row.”

“That is nine times in a row about once a month,” Rao said.

“Yes.”

“You will do it once, and then you will tell me at the fifth hour on the next first of a month that you cannot do it, and I will believe you, and there will be a roll of strips sitting in a room for four weeks.”

“I will not do that.”

“You have said a thing you have not done yet,” Rao said. “I have heard it. Go down now and take it down, and come up at the sixth hour in the morning.”

## Nine Steps With A Turn In The Landing

It is eleven steps and a landing with a turn in it and then nine more, and the rail is on the inside of the turn, and a roll of strips about the size of a barrow wheel goes up it two hands or it does not go up it.

She went down it with her left arm under it and her right on the top of it, and she got round the turn with her right arm open the whole way, and she came up the nine steps at the sixth hour in the morning with it and put it on the middle of the table.

It took her four minutes. She had to put her shoulder against the wall on the turn and walk her feet round.

Rao did not say anything about it. She squared it up against the middle of the table and went back to her margin and read for two hours.

And then she said, with the pencil still moving: “You will come up at the sixth hour on the first of every month and take that down and bring it back.”

“There is no line on this floor for me.”

“No,” Rao said. “That is still true and it has not changed. Bring the bundle down.”

## The Tenth Of A Month

She did the gate on the tenth at three and a half feet. It came up easy. She put the clay in the barrow and carried it out and put it against the wall, and there are six barrow-loads against that wall now and a seventh standing on its handles.

She slept three hours on the ninth and two on the tenth, and both nights she got up at the fourth hour out of a habit that had stopped having anything behind it about three weeks before, and both times there was no fire, because there is not a living thing in that shed but her, and there has not been since the third of a month.

Pei came out at the eighth hour, looked at the drain, and looked at her.

“You are lighter than you were,” he said.

“Yes.”

“You are not eating,” he said, “and I am not going to say anything about it, because it is not my ground and a man at a gate is allowed one thing and I have had mine.”

He went back inside, and she stood in the gateway for a minute with the iron in her hand.

## What She Had And What She Had Not Bought

Eight coppers went west on the twenty-eighth of a month and there was bread for five days, and on the sixth of the next month she went west again with eight and there was bread for five days.

That is the whole of it: eight out, five days in, and two days in seven that she walks to a gate and comes back from a gate without. It had been seven out and eight in for seven months and it had been eating a stock, and there has not been a stock since the twenty-fourth of a month about a month back.

She did the month out on the boards on the ninth and it came out in one line and the line was that she would finish the month of which this is the ninth with two coppers in the fold in the inside of the door frame, and she would buy bread on the fourteenth of the next month, and she had never in her life finished a month with two coppers in her hand before.

## The Works Board, The Tenth

She read the board at the works on the tenth from the bottom up and the ditch line was still on it, second from the bottom, by the length, two weeks, and the man who kept the list read the whole board out loud at the ninth hour for four men off a cart from the west.

She stood nine feet off in the gateway and listened to him say the ditch line the way he says the other ten.

“There is one line on that board a person could take,” she said, to herself, in a gateway, at the ninth hour on the tenth of a month, and there was a man at the bench about eleven feet off her who did not look up.

## The Plain Part About One Person

She is eighteen. On the ninth of a month a woman forty-five years old with a thumb gone off at the first joint asked her for the first time in seven months to do a thing with her two arms, and she said yes out loud on a table with forty books open on it, before she had worked out whether she could do it.

She did it in four minutes. She has carried a roll of strips the size of a barrow wheel up a turn in a landing with one arm open, and the whole of what she is worth on that floor at this hour is a thing with a cord round it.

And a woman who has read forty thousand columns in sixteen years told her there is no line for her, and also told her to bring a bundle down, and both of those were said the same way, in the same voice, on the same afternoon, and she has not yet worked out which of the two she would rather have been the second.

There is going to be a roll of strips on that table once a month, and she is going to be the one who takes it up nine steps with a turn in the landing, and there is no line on that floor that says she has any right to be standing at the edge of it.

That is a want she can put a shape to, and it is the first one in five weeks.
