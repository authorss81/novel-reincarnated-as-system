# The Column With Her In It

She went up the mill road on the twenty-eighth of a month at the third hour and she had the whole of the thing worked out before she got to the door, and she had been sick about it twice on the road and went anyway.

“Rao.”

It is the first name she has said out loud to anybody on that ground in seven months, and she had never in her life used a person’s name in the street, and she had used her own twice.

The woman at the far end of the table put her pencil down.

## What She Said

“I want to ask you for something.”

“Then ask me,” Rao said. “You have been sitting at that end of my table for three weeks and there is no line here with your name on it and I have said so to you once. If you have come up nine steps to sit there, ask me for the thing.”

“I want you to be in this room,” Ning Yan said, “at the fourth hour, on a day I name. Once a month. Four times a month if you can stand it.”

She said all of it in about nine seconds and she did not stop in the middle of it, which she had decided on the road she would do and which took everything she had.

## Why

“My hands are cold,” she said. “They have been cold since the second of a month about six weeks back and they were not cold before that for four months. They are cold on every road between this building and the shed. They are not cold in this room when you are in it.”

She put the back of her left hand flat on the table.

“I do not know why and I am not going to find out why, and I have been up here eight times in three weeks and I have been warm every single one of the eight, and it comes back cold on the road down.”

Rao did not look at her hand. She looked at the books.

“Then ask me that,” she said, “and not the other thing, which is what you actually asked me.”

## What She Had Actually Asked For

“Yes,” Ning Yan said. “I asked you to be in this room. That is the other thing and it is the one I want.”

“All right.”

“All right,” she said, “and I am going to tell you the other half of it, and then you can say no, and I am not going to be able to take it back either way, and I would rather you had the whole of it.”

She put both hands on the edge of the table.

“There is a thing that comes up out of me when there is another living thing in the room,” she said, “and it does not come up when there is not. That is the whole of it. I do not know how to say it in a way that sounds better than that.”

## What Rao Said About It

Rao put both hands flat on the table too.

“I have kept columns on this floor for sixteen years,” she said, “and I have read forty thousand of them, and in about thirty thousand of them there is a person doing what they are able to do at whatever hour they are able to do it. That is not a strange thing.”

She turned the pencil over in her hand and did not use it.

“I have never once asked a person on this floor to describe it to me before, because there has never been a reason to, and a woman who has been sitting at my table for three weeks reading dates at me is not somebody I was expecting to have a reason with.”

“I am not going to be paid for this,” she said, “and I am not going to be thanked for it, and I am not going to be honoured by it, and I am telling you all three of those now so that none of them is sitting in the room afterwards.”

“All right.”

“You come up at the fourth hour on the sixth of a month and on the eighth and on the eleventh and on the fourteenth,” Rao said, “and I am here on all four of those days because I am here every day, and you will sit at that end of the table and you will not talk while I am reading, and if I ask you to read something out loud you will read it out loud in the ordinary voice.”

“And on the first of a month you carry a bundle down nine steps.”

“Yes.”

## The Stair Book

Rao got up and went to the north end of the table and came back with a book about the size of a school book, and put it on the middle of the table and opened it, and turned it round so the pages faced her and not Ning Yan.

“This is the stair book,” she said. “It is not a column. It is who went up the nine steps and on what day and at what hour, and it is the only thing in this room that is not about anybody’s work, and I have kept it for sixteen years and it goes up the mill road on the first with everything else in this room and it comes back down on the ninth.”

She put her thumb on the last written line.

“You are eight lines down it,” she said, “and eight times I have written a woman and the day and the hour, and I did not write your name because you did not give it to me and I did not ask you for it, because a person who does not give a name is not asked twice.”

## The Two Words

She stood at the edge of that table for about eleven seconds.

“Ning Yan,” she said. “Of Lowwater. That is the whole of it and it is the only name I have.”

Rao wrote it down.

She wrote it in the ordinary hand in about two seconds, with the pen she put down for the pencil, and she wrote the day at the hour and the two words at the left of the line where the other eight lines had one word at the left of them.

Then she closed the book and put her hand flat on the cover of it.

“That is in a book on this floor,” she said, “and it goes up the mill road on the first at the sixth hour with a cord round the tray, and it comes back down on the ninth and gets copied out into a book on the floor below mine by a man with a brush who cannot read my hand and will not try, and nobody inside nine days of road will be able to tell you what is in it.”

“I know.”

“You do not know,” Rao said. “You know what I have just said to you about it. There is a difference and you are about to find it out.”

She put the book back at the north end of the table and came back and sat down and picked up the pencil.

“It was a yes before the name,” she said, “and I would have taken the yes without it.”

## What She Felt Going Down The Road

She was ashamed for two hundred yards of the mill road and then she stopped being ashamed and started being frightened, and the fright lasted the eleven steps and the road down to the shed.

She had asked a woman she had known for three weeks for about eight hours a month of her own company, and she had paid for it with nothing, because there is no way on nine days of road of paying for it, and the woman had said yes in eleven words and had told her in advance that she would not be thanked and would not be honoured by it.

She is eighteen years old, and the first thing she has ever asked anybody for was not bread and was not work and was not a line in a book. It was eight hours a month of a woman of forty-five in a cold room, and she asked for it in the ordinary voice at the fourth hour on the twenty-eighth of a month, and she gave a name out loud in a room to get it.

And what she had got in exchange was the right to be in a warm room four times a month, and the reason she had asked was that something in her own body will not come up unless somebody else is standing near it, and she had said that out loud in a room with forty books open on it, out loud, in the ordinary voice, to a person whose whole trade is a piece of paper.

On the first of a month that stair book goes up the mill road with a cord round the tray, and there is no version of this in which it does not go.

## The Twenty-Ninth, At The Counter

Up the mill road at the third hour on the twenty-ninth, and there were two names in the stair book where there had been one, and the day was the twenty-eighth and the hour was the fourth.

Then she went down the eleven steps and stood at the end of the counter, and the man behind it was writing, and the seasonal book was shut on the end of the counter where it has been shut since the twenty-sixth of a month six months back.

“You have been up that road twice in two days,” he said, “and you have not asked me for anything, and I am going to say one thing to you and then I am going back to my own work.”

“All right.”

“There is a book on the floor above this one that goes up this road on the first of a month with everything else that has come into this building in thirty days,” he said. “I cord the tray. I do not know what is in any of it and I do not ask, because a man behind a counter who asks what is on a tray has found out that it matters to him.”

He put the pen down.

“And there are now two names in one of those books where there was one line saying a woman,” he said, “and on the first of a month at the sixth hour that goes up the mill road with a cord round it, and you gave a name to a woman on that floor on the twenty-eighth of a month and there is no hand in this building that can take it out.”

She stood at the end of that counter for four seconds.

“Thank you,” she said.

“It was not for that,” he said, and picked the pen up.
