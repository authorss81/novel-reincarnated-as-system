# The Rest Of The Run

The fifth of a month beginning 1,034 was a gate day and she was in the run from the seventh to the ninth hour with the bar in her right hand, and there were eleven men on the bank above the gate because the second run had gone eleven days past the twenty-fourth and they were cutting it a second length and four of them would do it and there were eleven.

On the sixth she was at the yard at the turn at the sixth hour and read twenty-nine lines out loud twice, and four men at the north end turned round and listened to it, which they had done the first time and which she had noticed both times and had still not decided to notice.

## The Man At The North End

The man at the north end was thirty and he had been at that yard for six weeks and on the sixth of a month, in the middle of the second course, he said the thing to the man with a rule in his hand and not to her, in the voice of a man asking about the weather about somebody else.

“Does she get paid for that.”

The man with a rule in his hand did not turn round.

“No.”

“Right.”

And that was the whole of it and the work went on, and four men did not stop and none of them looked at her, and she had spent two minutes and eleven seconds reading out loud at the sixth hour and had been answered in four words by a man who was not asked to answer.

She stood at the low end of the sill course with the page in her hand and worked out what she had just heard, standing up, in about eleven seconds.

That man had not asked for her. That man had asked whether she was paid, and he had asked it in the voice a man uses when he has heard something and wants to know whether it is a rule, and he had got his answer and he had gone back to work.

## What She Asked Him Afterwards

She asked at the seventh hour, at the low end, with the page still in her hand, and she asked it out loud, and there were nine men on that ground.

“If I read it every month, does that put me in a book.”

The man with a rule in his hand put the chalk in his pocket.

“No.”

“There is a page here with my four words on it in your hand.”

“There is.”

“That goes up the mill road.”

“It does, on the ninth.”

“And it is not a line.”

“It is a margin on a yard page and it is in a yard-book hand and the yard book is not mine and it does not stay in this yard,” he said, “and if you asked me that question in front of nine men I would tell you the same thing I have just told you, which is that it is not a line and it is not yours and it is not a person.”

## What He Said About Her Working For Nothing

She put the page down on the sill.

“Then I will do it for nothing.”

He picked the chalk out of his pocket and looked at it and put it back, which he does about four times a week.

“I am going to say three sentences and then the second course goes on and it is the only conversation we are going to have today.”

“All right.”

“A person who works for nothing is not a line and not a column and not a day against anything, and it is not in any book in this ground, and there is no way on nine days of road to make it one, and you have known that since the eighth of a month four months back when a woman of forty-five told you a line is four coppers.”

“I have not worked out the rest of it.”

“You have not worked out the rest of it and it is the rest of it that matters.” He put his hand flat on the low side of the sill course.

“You can read. That is a fact. And being able to read is not a thing this ground has ever paid anybody for, and it is going to keep not being a thing this ground pays anybody for.”

“And the day you stop being surprised by that is the day you are standing in a room with nine men in it who already know you are not getting anything.”

## The Fifth And The Sixth, On The Bank

The bank above that gate is thirty feet high and it has been cut back eleven feet in eleven years by a man who brings a bar up it every morning, and there is a bench on it and a water butt and about nine men who come and go on it.

She was in the drain on the fifth from the seventh hour to the ninth hour, and she was up that bank at the fourth hour on the sixth, sitting on the bench with nothing in her hands, because eleven men were on it and four of them would do.

They did not ask her anything.

That was the part she got wrong on the way up. She had worked out at the second hour that morning that she was going to be looked at, the way she is looked at in every yard she has stood in since the fourth of a month two months back, and she had put her right arm against her side and got her four fingers and a thumb ready for it.

Four men on that bank looked at her for about nine seconds in the whole of two hours. One of them moved over a foot so that somebody behind him could get down, and one of them asked her whether the gate would be open on the thirtieth, and she said that she did not know, and he said that it would be.

She worked that out on the way down the bank and she had to stop halfway and stand in the cut for a minute, and it was not the furnace and it was not the ring, and it came up the width of a finger and stopped while she was standing in it.

## The Sixth At The Boards

She did not go to the gate at the seventh hour on the sixth. She went up the mill road and sat at the near end of the table on the third floor with forty books open on it and a woman of forty-five at the far end of it who did not comment on it.

She read eleven columns out loud in four hours and got them all the same both times, and her hands were warm the whole of it, and at the eighth hour the woman turned round and said:

“You have not read that one.”

“No.”

“You read it the same as the other ten.”

“Yes.”

“That is the correct answer and it is the wrong thing to have said and I am not going to argue with you about it at the eighth hour.”

## The Seventh, At The Rail

There were forty-one lines on the board at the works rail at the sixth hour of the seventh and she read all forty-one down and got them the same twice.

There was a man at the rail she had not seen before. He was thirty-five and he had a coat on and no chalk and he stood at the near end and read the board down the way she reads it, which is fast, and he got to the end of it and came back up and did it again.

Then he looked at her right hand for about a second and a half and then at the board.

“You are not on that,” he said.

“No.”

He did not say anything else and he went up the road toward the mill and she stood at the rail and let him go.

## The Plain Part About One Person

She is eighteen years old and she has been on that ground for seven months and she has never once been entered anywhere on it.

She worked at a gate for seven months and got a copper a day in an ordinary hand with no stub and no booking. She dug four hundred yards of drain. She read twenty-nine lines out loud at a yard twice a month for nothing. She reads columns on the third floor four times a month for nothing. A woman of forty-five has a slip with a date on it beside a column and it is not a line and it is not hers.

There is a man behind a counter who has written her first day down twice and cannot write a figure, and a man with a rule in his hand who has four words of hers in chalk going up a road on the ninth, and none of those four things pays her, and there is not going to be a fifth.

The bare board at the works was a season. It was bare for about ten weeks and she found work at a gate and she was paid a copper a day and she was hungry for none of it.

This is the rest of the run.

## The Plain Part About The Work

There were nine men on that ground on the sixth of a month who had begun, without any arrangement, to rely on a thing she does that this ground has never paid anybody for.

That is worse than being left out and she worked it out standing at a rail on the seventh with forty-one lines on it and a man walking away up the road.

A gate pays a copper a day and a yard pays nothing and a floor pays nothing and a counter pays nothing, and she has worked out inside a week that the ground is going to keep asking her to stand in rooms and be useful in them, and none of those rooms has a figure against her in it, and every one of them is going to know that by the middle of the summer.

She put a lump of clay in at the low end of the ring at the second hour of the eighth and it came up the width of a finger and stopped, and she did not do it again, because it was going to stop coming up on its own in a few days and she had decided not to be standing in a shed with nothing in it when it did.
