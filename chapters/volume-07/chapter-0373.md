# Two Lines Shorter

She carried the clay out on the ninth of a month.

Half a box is about nine handfuls and she had put four of them out of the corner by the fourth hour of that morning, and she carried them to the yard four miles east in the barrow because a barrow is the only thing on that ground that can take nine handfuls and a woman who cannot lift a dressed sill.

Four miles up a made road with a wheel that was not worth the name, at the pace of a woman pushing a wet load, which is the pace of a gate and not the pace of a person with a bar.

It came back with the clay still in it and the handles worn warm and nothing on it at all that she could have sold.

He looked at what was in the barrow and then at her.

“Where did that come out.”

“A fifth of a reach at Pei’s gate.”

“That is clay,” he said.

“Yes.”

He put the tip of the rule into it and turned it over about half of it and then pushed the barrow back at her.

“I have got about four tons of clay in that yard,” he said, “coming off a bank on the east end every day of this season, and there is a man in this yard who is paid to be on it. I am not going to take a barrow-load off a woman at a gate and put it in my yard and call it anything.”

She took it back down the made road.

He was right about all of it, and she had known that he would be before she got there, and she had gone four miles east anyway because half a box of clay in the corner of a shed she cannot fire anything in is a thing that does not stay put in a person’s head.

## The Tenth, The Second Hour

Up the mill road at the second hour on the tenth, and the question she was carrying up it had been sitting in her since the third of a month, when the fire had not come up.

She had not got as far as the stair.

The man with the brush was coming down with sheets in his hands and he stopped on the third step from the bottom and looked at her and then at the sheets.

“You have not come up this month.”

“I have come up the road.”

He went back up four steps, put the sheets on the top one, and came down again.

“I have got something to say to you before you spend your question,” he said, “and I have been waiting since the sixth to say it, and I would rather said it before than after.”

## What He Had Seen

He went up and got a sheet and brought it down and put it on the step between them.

It had two columns on it, ruled, with a margin at the left, and the left-hand column was days with dates down it and hours against them and a season marked at each end and the word above the hours that said good.

That was hers. She could read it from four feet.

She had got as far as the word above the hours and the season at the end of it and had never once been able to go on past that, because past that was the hand, and the hand was the other column, and the other column had been there four months and she had looked at it four times without being able to tell a letter of it from a mark in the paper.

The right-hand column was a different thing and it was in the fine hand, and it had lines on it under the days, and she could not read one word of it and never had.

“Last month,” he said, “there were four lines at the foot of that column under the days. I set this one against the other book on the sixth and I copied your column out into it the way I do every month, and I read the two of them against each other because that is what the cross-reference is.”

He put his thumb on the margin.

“This month there are two.”

She looked at it.

Two lines. She could see two lines. She could not read either of them and she could not tell whether they were two of the four or two new ones, because a line of writing in a hand you cannot read is a line, and a line has one width and every line on that column was the same width to the width of a finger.

“Two instead of four,” she said.

“Two instead of four,” he said. “And I have had four months of it with four and four and four and four and I know what four lines looks like going down a column and I know what two looks like, and I do not know what it means and I am not going to guess at it, because I am not able to read the thing and a man who cannot read a thing does not get to have an opinion about it.”

## The Part That Was On The Page

That was the whole of what he said.

At the bottom of that stair with the stove at her back and nine men at their cups, she looked at two lines in a hand she had never once been able to read a letter of.

And she worked out, standing there, what the two of them were.

On the first of a month, at about a quarter past the sixth hour, a strip about as wide as a hand had gone out of the yard of the works east up a made road with a stone on its tail-board, and it had her name at the top of it, and it had four or five lines in the middle of it in the fine hand, and a word at the head of them she could not read.

She had watched it go. She had stood on the verge and watched it until it was a shape on the flat.

And the column in a room nine days of road away, in the hand that can read columns, had had four lines on it at the foot of it in the last four months and had two now.

Something had gone east.

Something had come back.

Not on the cart. The cart came back down the mill road on the ninth with four empty trays on it and a stone on the tail-board and nothing else, and she had stood nine feet off on the near side of that track and watched it go past the elms.

It came back the other way. The column is a column and the column is copied out every month by hand against another book by a man with a brush, and the column goes up the mill road on the first at the sixth hour and the copying is the ordinary traffic of that road and it goes both ways whether anybody decides anything or not.

## The Plain Part About One Person

She is eighteen years old and she cannot read the hand.

For six months the only thing anybody on that ground could tell her about what was written about her was the shape of it: how many lines, how wide, how many figures, whether there was a rule under the last of them.

She is a person two offices will vouch for, and she has been since the twenty-sixth of a month six months back, out loud, in daylight, at a bench in a gateway, and she has never once been able to read what she has been vouched for.

And on the tenth of a month a man with a brush told her that two of them had been taken off, in a hand she cannot read, about nine days of road east of a stair she has never gone up, and that they had been taken off because something about her had gone east on the first of a month at a quarter past the sixth hour.

## What She Did Not Do

She did not ask.

She had not spent the question, and the question she had brought up the mill road that morning was about a fire, and there was a man standing in front of her on a stair who had been eleven years on that floor and had told her something he had been waiting two days to say.

And she stood there and worked out that the question was the fire and the thing that had happened was the two lines, and that a man with a brush had offered her the thing that had happened for nothing at all, twice now, and had never once offered her the thing she came for.

She put her hand on the rail of the stair with her left one, because her right one has four fingers on it and it is the wrong hand for a rail on a day like that.

“Will you set my column against it again next month.”

“I set it every month,” he said. “That is the cross-reference and it is not done for you and it is not done for anybody.”

She went back down the mill road in the light with nothing new against her ribs and two lines in her head she could not read.

## What She Carried Down The Road

A furnace would not light itself and she could not light it alone, and there was not one person inside nine days of road she was entitled to stand in a shed with and try.

And a piece of paper she had watched go east on a cart on the first of a month had come back to a column nine days of road east of here in two lines that had been taken off it, in a hand she cannot read, by somebody she will never meet, out of a room she has never been in.

She had put one thing into a book in the first month she had been on that ground, in daylight, out loud, at a bench in a gateway, because a furnace eats three coppers a third of a sack and she had a third of a reach a day at a gate.

That had gone four hundred yards further than she had asked for it to. Then it went east. And now something had come back with two lines off it.

She was walking down a mill road with a bar over her shoulder and she had spent six months being entered into rooms she could not reach, and this was the first time in six months that something had come out of one of them toward her.

It had come back as less.

Less, and in the wrong hand, and nine days of road east of a stair she had never gone up, and she had stood in a gateway and been handed the number by a man who had waited two days to hand it over because that is the sort of careful he was.

She walked down the mill road with a bar over her shoulder and the two lines going round in her head, and going round is not the same thing as reading, and she knew the difference better than anybody on nine days of road.
