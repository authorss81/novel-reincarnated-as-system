# A Bench Long Enough For Nine

The eleventh of a month beginning 1,034 was the fourth hour on the top floor of a counting house with forty books open on it and the seventh hour at a gate, and on the twelfth she was sent two miles up the made road with a yard page in her left hand.

The man with a rule in his hand had a dressed stone on his list that came off a face two miles off, and he was not going to send one of his nine for a page of paper.

He said that out loud on the yard before she set off.

“I am not being kind. There is nine men here and four will do and I have got a stone on a sheet and the sheet says one hundred and forty pounds and the stone says one hundred and thirty-eight and I want one of them looked at.”

## The Road

Two miles up the made road, and the mill on the left of it the whole way, and its wheel has not turned since before she came.

It is a brick-and-timber building about a hundred feet long with a wide door at the near end standing open and the inside of it is one room.

She stood in that doorway for about a minute and did not go in, because he had said look at a page, and the words come in had not been said by anybody, and she has spent seven months learning the difference between the two.

## The Room

The bench goes the whole length of the right-hand wall and it is long enough for nine men sitting down, and there is a bar against the far wall about five feet long and a chain hanging from a hook, and eleven dressed stones standing on edge along the left wall in a row with their faces out.

Nine men were in it. Two of them were lifting a stone onto a low frame with a bar and a chain and one of them was taking the weight on the bar while the other one walked it in.

She could see where she went in the sequence from the doorway without being told anything about it, and she had been looked at and worked out and priced twice in seven months and both times it had been done in about two seconds by a man with a rule in his hand, and this time the room did not look at her at all for four minutes.

## Geng

He was at the left-hand end of the bench with a sheet of paper and a piece of chalk and he was fifty-one and he had been in that room for nineteen years and he ran it.

He looked at her when the stone was down.

“Page,” he said.

She gave it to him. He read the line with his finger a quarter of an inch under the writing and then he went down the room and put his hand on the stone and his thumb on the edge of it and came back.

“One hundred and thirty-eight,” he said. “You can write that on it and you can tell him it is a hundred and thirty-eight and short of a hundred and forty, and that the face is the face and not the bar.”

“That is four coppers’ worth of nothing,” she said.

He turned round.

That is the whole of the first thing. He turned round and looked at her right arm standing open at her side and her hand with four fingers and a thumb on it, and he did the arithmetic, and she watched him do it, and it took him about two seconds, and he did not say any of it.

## What He Said Instead

He put the page down on the bench.

“You can read,” he said.

“Yes.”

“You can count and you can read and you cannot lift the end of a thing.” He put his hand flat on the near edge of the bench, which is oak and about four inches thick. “I have got four men on this floor who can lift and none of them who can read, and it is the same in every room I have ever stood in, and I have never once been in a room where both were in the same person.”

“All right.”

“So here is the trade and it is not an offer and I am not asking you to do it now. When a stone goes on a frame I write it on a sheet and the sheet goes down the road and it is a day and a half before anybody sees it, and I have to write it twice because I cannot trust myself with a number I have written out once.”

He picked the chalk up and turned it over.

“If you can read me off a sheet of stone I will not have to write it twice and I will not have to send anybody down to check it, and that is the whole of what there is in it for anybody in this building.”

## Mouth Six

He put the chalk down in the groove and squared the sheet and said what he said next in four sentences, and he said them the way he said the number on the stone, which is without weight.

“I was put on a list in the second year I was in this building. It was a piece of paper and there was a name on it and it was not my hand that wrote it and it was not a room I have ever been in. I have never been taken off it. Nineteen years and I have not been taken off it, and the words have never come back to me again.”

He took his hand off the sheet.

“There is nothing in this building about that list. Not a book, not a copy, not a name. I went looking for it about four years in and I decided I would not go looking again.”

## The Part He Gave Up

He picked the sheet up off the bench and squared it again and did not hand it back.

“I am going to say the last of it and then I am going back to work, because I have got two hundred pounds of face going out on the sixteenth and nine men in here and four of them are mine.”

“All right.”

“I have been on a list for nineteen years and I have never found out who put me on it and I have stopped thinking about it. That is the honest way round and I want it written down that I stopped thinking about it, because the day I start again I will get it wrong, because I will start again on the assumption that somebody is waiting.”

He gave her the page back.

“Write one hundred and thirty-eight on that and tell him I said so, and if he asks you what that floor is like, tell him it is a bench long enough for nine and a bar and a chain and eleven stones standing on edge, and do not tell him anything else, because there is nothing else and he would only use it.”

## The Plain Part About One Person

She is eighteen years old and a man of fifty-one who runs the biggest room she has ever stood in looked at her arm for two seconds and worked out what she could not do and did not say it, and then asked her whether she could read.

Nobody on nine days of road has offered her a thing she can actually do since the eighth of a month four months back, when a woman of forty-five asked her to read a column out loud and it turned out to be the only instrument on that side of the line.

And he said it was not an offer and he was not asking her to do it now, and it is not an offer, and it is the first thing anybody has said to her in seven months that she is able to do and cannot be paid for.

## The Road Back, And The Second Time She Stood There

She went back down the made road at the eighth hour with a figure on a page in her left hand and walked the two miles and got to the yard at a turn at the ninth hour, and the man with a rule in his hand read the figure and wrote it against his own line in his own hand and put the chalk in his pocket.

And then he said, “That number has been wrong on that sheet since the second of a month.”

“It was wrong.”

“It was a hundred and forty, and the face is a hundred and thirty-eight, and I have had it as a hundred and forty because I have been writing down what the bar says instead of what the stone says.” He looked at the page. “That is two pounds on a line that closes on the sixteenth, and I am going to get about nine numbers right over the next four days, and I am not sending anybody two miles for any of them.”

“All right.”

“I am telling you because you will hear it from somebody else inside a week and I would rather you heard it from me.”

That was the whole of it. She walked four miles back to a shed at a mill whose wheel has not turned, and the next time she went up that made road she stood in the doorway again, and she stayed in the doorway.

## The Thirteenth And The Fourteenth

She wrote one hundred and thirty-eight on the page in her own hand and took it down the road and the man with a rule in his hand read it and wrote the figure against his own line in his own hand, and that is the first number of that stone that has been right.

On the eleventh she dug the run at the gate from the seventh hour to the ninth hour and got a copper, and there were seven coppers in the fold in the inside of the door frame and bread for five days, and she had not eaten since the sixth.

On the twelfth and the thirteenth she dug the run again at the same hours, and on the sixteenth she went west at the fifth hour with twelve coppers and came back at the eleventh with four loaves and nothing in the fold.

On the fourteenth she was at the rail at the sixth hour and there were forty-four lines on it.

And two miles up the made road, in a building a hundred feet long with one room in it and nine men in the room, a sheet of stone weights was going out on the sixteenth, and a man of fifty-one had a chalk in his hand and had not told anybody at the yard at the turn what he had said to a woman standing in his doorway.
