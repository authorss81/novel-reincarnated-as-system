# The Weight Or The Length

The eighteenth of a month beginning 1,004 was the day the board at the works rail filled, and she was at the rail at the sixth hour with the bar over her right shoulder, and it had eight lines on it on the fifteenth and twenty-nine on it that morning.

That is what filling means on that ground. It is not men arriving at a gate. It is the board.

She stood at the rail and read it down the way she reads everything, which is fast and by the shape before the words, and then she read it again at the same speed and got the same thing twice, which she has done at four places in seven months and never once been wrong at.

## Twenty-Nine Lines

Two hundred and six yards of dressed stone by the load, checked by a man with a rule in his hand and not by anybody else.

Forty feet of bed by the length at the north end of the yard at the turn, and a margin note against the fourteenth in a yard-book hand, and three men at that yard have read that note.

Nine hundred feet of drain by the length, second run, low side, at the works, and the twenty-fourth against it.

Two hundred yards of hedge by the length, east of the turn, and it is the second listing of the same ground, and the thirtieth against it.

And under all of those, at the bottom, in a different ink, a line that is not stone and not bed and not drain, and it says that it is a cart track east of the turn, nine hundred feet, by the load.

That one is new. It has been on the board two days.

## What Every Line Is Measured By

There is one rule on that board and it is eleven words long and a man with a chalk wrote it at the head of the rail in the spring before she came and it has not been taken down since.

Everything on this board is measured by the weight or measured by the length.

Nothing on it is measured by the voice. There has never been a line on that board measured by the voice. A man reads it out and the rail goes quiet and nine men look at the length of it and one of them puts his name at the end of it, and that has been the arrangement for longer than the mill has been standing still.

She read the twenty-nine lines twice and she could have taken none of them.

The two hundred and six yards of dressed stone is a weight. It goes on a bar, and the bar takes two hands, and the heavy end has been closed to her since before she came past the last working point and will not open again.

The forty feet of bed is a length. You dig it four feet deep with a bar and a shovel and you fill it and you pack it, and that is four hours of a right arm for every ten feet of it, and her right arm has been open half the time since a cut at the far end of a bad flat in a different year.

Nine hundred feet of drain is a length, and she has dug four hundred yards of drain in seven months for five coppers a reach.

Two hundred yards of hedge is a length, and she did the first listing of it herself five weeks back and it paid out of a line of work that has been shut for five months.

And the cart track at the bottom is by the load.

## The Listing Man

He came out of the hut at the near end of the rail at the seventh hour with his coat on over one arm, and he is forty-one and he has kept that list two years and he has not asked her a question in seven months and he was not going to start.

He looked at the board and then at her, in the way a man looks at a rail.

“Twenty-nine,” he said.

“Yes.”

“Full.”

“Yes.”

He put his arm through the sleeve.

“It was full in the spring before the wheel stopped,” he said. “It has not been full since. I have had that board at eight lines for a year and a bit and I could walk the length of it in six seconds without stopping, and I want you to know I know that is the worst thing that can happen to a list.”

“All right.”

“You want me to say that there is nothing on there for you.”

“No,” she said. “You do not have to say it. I read it twice.”

## What He Said Instead

He stood at the rail with his back to the hut door and looked down the twenty-nine lines, and he took his time about it, and he was not being kind and he was not being unkind.

“There is a thing you want me to say and I am not going to say it, because it is not mine to say. I keep the list. The man with the rule in his hand checks what is on it and takes what is off it. I have got a counter and a pen and a rail and nine names a week come off it.”

“Yes.”

“But I will tell you what the board is.”

He put two fingers on the rail and did not move them.

“Every line on it is priced off the ground. By the weight of what comes out of it or by the length of it, and every one of those twenty-nine is one of the two, and I have never once seen a third thing on that rail and I did not put the rule there and I could not take it away if I tried.”

He looked at her hands.

“You have got four fingers and a thumb on that one and an arm that stands open, and there is no line on this rail that a person with those goes on. That is not me. That is a board.”

## The Plain Part About One Person

She is eighteen years old and for seven months she has wanted a line on a board, and there has been one thing on nine days of road a person could have wanted instead, and today there were twenty-nine lines on a rail sixty feet from her hand and every one of them was priced off the ground.

She has been wrong about which one she wanted.

That is not a small thing and it did not arrive in the middle of the morning. It had been arriving for a week, at the yard at the turn, at the gate, at the third floor of a counting house with forty books open on a table, and she had been walking past it four times a day and putting a copper in the fold in the inside of the door frame and coming back to a shed with boards on it.

A full board is the thing she has wanted since before she came past the last working point.

There is one of them. She cannot get on it.

## Four Coppers And Bread For Three Days

She dug the run at the gate from the seventh hour to the ninth and Pei came out at the ninth and stood at the end of it the way he stands, and she got a copper, and there were five in the fold in the inside of the door frame.

Bread is two coppers a loaf and the store is five miles west and a trip to it is eight coppers for four loaves and that is bread for five days.

She had bread for three days and five coppers on the eighteenth. On the nineteenth she would have six and bread for two days. On the twentieth seven. On the twenty-first, eight, and she could walk west that morning at the fifth hour and come back at the eleventh with nothing in the fold and bread for five days in a cloth.

That is not a sum she has to work out. She worked it out standing at the end of a drain run with a bar in her right hand and four fingers and a thumb on it and it took her nine seconds.

The whole of it came down to the fact that the store was five miles west and the gate was where the copper was, and she could not be in both places in one morning, and a board that had been at eight lines for a year had gone to twenty-nine overnight.

## What A Line Looks Like

She had never watched one come off that rail.

On the eighteenth there were three names on it by the eighth hour and she had not been there when they went on, and on the nineteenth the man with a rule in his hand told her how it works, which is that the man who takes a line signs it at the end of it, and the man with the chalk checks it, and neither of them writes the name of the person who did the work.

That is the whole of it. There is no booking and no term and no way of walking up to a rail a year later and asking whether she had been there.

## What She Said At The Second Hour

She lay on the boards at the second hour with the bar under her right hand and the strip against her ribs and said the sentences out loud, because it was the second hour and there was not a living soul in that shed.

“There are twenty-nine lines on a board at a rail and every one of them is measured by the weight or by the length, and I can take neither, and I have been walking to that rail for seven months to be on it.”

“And there is a woman of forty-five one floor up from a man with a brush who has asked me twice for the price of a line of my own, and it is four coppers, and it is four for everybody, and I have had four coppers twice since I asked and I have not paid it either.”

She lay there for a while.

“And the thing I have wanted since the eighth of a month four months back is a line on a board, and I have just found out that I wanted it because it was the only shape anybody past the last working point had ever offered me, and not because I could hold it.”

It came up the width of a finger and stopped. She put a lump out of the box in at the low end of the ring and she did not do it a second time, because a rule is not worked out twice.

There was going to be a full board on that rail in the morning and there were going to be twenty-nine lines on it and she was going to be standing at it at the sixth hour, and she had five coppers and bread for three days and the store five miles west.

And there was nothing on it measured by the voice.
