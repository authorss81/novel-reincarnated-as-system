# What Two Men Saw From The Bank

She woke at the fourth hour on the third of a month because she had been waking at about the fourth hour every morning for four months for a fire that lit itself, and there was not a fire.

She lay on the boards in the dark and looked at the corner and the corner was black, and it had been black at the third hour the evening before when she had put a bar through her own chest a mile and a quarter east of that room with nine men standing nine feet off.

She got up and went and crouched at the low end of it with her hand nine inches off the brick, and nothing came up, and she did it for ten minutes.

A lump out of the box against the wall went in at the low end at the fourth hour and twenty minutes and the fire came up a finger’s width and sat there and did not go against the brick and stood nine feet high.

That is the whole of what it did.

She sat down on the boards with her back against the wall about four feet off it and looked at it for about as long as it takes to count nine, and it burned at a finger’s width and would not move.

## What She Tried

She tried it four times before the light came.

She put her hand flat on the brick. She put her hand flat on the front of the ring with her arm open. She put four fingers and a thumb on the low end where the lump goes in and held them there until her arm was open all the way and past the open into the other thing, which is the thing where it does not answer at all.

The third of them was one she had never done before and she did it for nine minutes.

She set the four fingers and the thumb against the brick at the low end where the heat comes first, and she held them there until the cold in her hand and the cold in the brick were the same thing, which is the thing she had done four thousand mornings to a flat stone by a door and had never once got an answer out of either side of it.

Nothing came up against her hand. What came up was the finger’s width it had reached on its own and not an inch past it, and it stayed at that while her hand was on it and it did not go back down after.

And she put the one thing she had left to try at the fifth hour, which was what she had done on the twenty-fifth of a month about three months back when three handfuls of ash had not brought it up and had not put it out, except that this time the clay went in under her own hand and she had forty-one days of furnace in her and nothing came up against the brick.

It had lit itself at the second hour every morning for four months.

It was not going to light itself. And she had been the only person in that shed for four months.

## The Flat Past The Last Hedge

She got to the flat at the sixth hour with the bar under her arm and the light coming up behind her and nothing in her hands, and there were men on the bank.

He had told her on the previous day to come back and stand on the high side, and she had said that she would, and she had come, and she was standing on the high side at about the sixth hour with a bar across her knees.

There were nine of them on a face sixty feet long, and at the sixth hour on the second of a month there had been nine of them on it, and the man at the west end and the man beside him were looking at her when she came up onto the high side and neither of them said one word.

She had been under a bar at the third hour yesterday and was on the high side at the sixth hour this morning, and not a man on that bank said a word about it.

## The Seventh Hour

It came at the seventh hour.

Not out of her. Not out of the bar across her knees. It came up out of the flat she was standing on, the way it had come up out of the grass a quarter of a mile off on the twenty-second of a month, and it went into the ground and it went into the air a quarter of a mile, and the dog on the farm a mile off did not bark.

It said its one sentence and stopped.

She stood on the high side of that bank with the bar across her knees and her hands cold and her right arm open and waited about two minutes for something in her to go that had gone every other time, and nothing went, and she stood there waiting for the bill the way she had stood on the grass on the twenty-second of a month about a month back.

It came in the shed about four hours later.

## The Two Men

That was not the part she had been carrying.

On the twenty-second of a month she had been on a bank on her own with a gang of four men two hundred yards off and a stone in her hand that went the length of her own arm and stopped, and the four of them did not look up, and a week later a man in a hand she could not read had written it down and sent it nine days of road on a cart to a gate post.

This time there were two men nine feet off her who were looking directly at her.

They had been looking at her since the sixth hour, when she came up the face with a bar and stood where the man with the rule in his hand had told her to stand, and they had been looking at her through the whole of the seventh hour while nothing at all appeared to happen on that bank.

Then something did happen on that bank, and both of them stopped with their bars in their hands.

That was all. A man at a bar who stops for a moment in the middle of a morning is a man who has heard something in another room and will go back to work. One of them said, to the other, about half of a sentence, in the ordinary voice, and she got the two or three words of it that came across nine feet of flat ground.

And there was no sound on that bank at all, which she heard standing still with a bar across her knees and a bar is a bad thing to hear nothing through, and which is how she knows it happened there and not in a room nine days west with a stove in it.

Then they went back to the face.

And she stood there with a bar across her knees and understood that she had been looked at.

## What Cannot Be Put Back

Not the bar. Not the bank. Not the face.

The nine days of road that this thing takes to cross, and the seven months she has just spent being unreadable, and the three pieces of paper with her name at the top of them in a hand she cannot read.

She had been a woman a room found out about. And she was now a woman a room can look at, in daylight, on a flat with a mill in it, at the seventh hour, in front of two men who work a bank and will be at the same bank on the eighth of this month and the fifteenth and the twenty-second.

Two men know a thing happened on that bank at the seventh hour on the third of a month. They will not write it down. They have no book and no column and no reason to say a word about it to anybody.

They will say it to the next man who stands on that high side.

And there are nine men on that bank, and a man with a rule in his hand four miles back at the yard, and a woman with a cart that comes twice a week, and a gate three miles west with a man at it.

Every stage she has ever taken was announced in a room to people who had to write it down before she finished saying it, and there was a bound around all of it and a county book inside the bound, and she had been standing outside the whole of that since the twenty-second of a month about two months back.

This one was taken in the open at the seventh hour on a flat with a mill in it, and two men saw it, and there is no book anywhere inside nine days of road that can be un-corded.

## The Bill

She was at the shed at the eleventh hour and put the bar down against the wall and went to the ring.

It was still sitting at a finger’s width where it had been sitting since the fourth hour that morning, and she put a lump out of the box in at the low end with her own hand and stood in front of it, and it came up a finger’s width and stopped.

She did it nine times over about three hours. She did it with ash and with clay and with her hands flat on the brick and with her hands held four inches off it, and it came up a finger’s width and stopped, and it was not a fire that was going out. It was a fire that was waiting for something.

Then she went and got the one thing she had that she had never needed, which was half a box of clay out of the corner of the gate at a fifth of a reach a day and eight days of bread and the flat of her second finger, and she sat down on the boards about four feet off it and understood the whole of it standing there.

A stage cannot be built by one pair of hands.

The furnace will not light itself and she cannot light it alone. There is no clay in this room and no bar and no hold and no count of breath that does it, and there is nobody else inside nine days of road she is entitled to stand in a shed with and try.

That was the bill and it was not a limb and it was not a hold and it was not a finger.

She had put her hand flat on that brick nine times in three hours and the stone and her hand had come out the same temperature every one of the nine, and she had understood it every one of the nine, and the understanding had not moved it a finger’s width.

She had owned a fire for four months that came up at the second hour every morning and had never asked her for anything, and on the third of a month she put it out with a bank about four feet of it, and what she had in its place was a room with a cold ring in it and a door standing open onto flat ground that had nine men on it.
