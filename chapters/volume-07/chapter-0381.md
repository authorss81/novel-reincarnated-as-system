# Forty Thousand Columns

She went up the mill road on the sixth of a month at the third hour with her strip in her left hand, folded in two, because she had told a man with a brush on the third that she would go up with something in her hands and there was one thing on nine days of road that was hers and about as wide as a hand.

The first flight was eleven steps and worn through in the middle. The second was nine.

The room at the top of nine steps is about twice the size of the room at the top of eleven and it is colder, and the cold is at the north end where there are two windows that have not been opened since before the man on the floor below started.

There was a table down the middle of it the length of nine men.

## The Books

That is the whole of it, and it took her a minute and a half to believe.

The books on that table are open. Not stacked, not squared up, not closed and corded on a tray in a low room — open, face down, page open, side open, about forty of them at once with the leaves standing up at every angle a book that has been left alone for an hour stands at.

There is nowhere else on nine days of road where more than one book is open at a time. In the counting house the book shuts. On the copying floor the sheets are squared. At the works it is a board. Here there is a table with forty books open on it and a chair at each side and nowhere for a person to stand that is not between two of them.

## The Smallest Person In The Biggest Room

The woman was standing at the far end of that table with a book open in front of her and a pencil in her hand, and she was the smallest person Ning Yan had ever stood in a room with.

Not the youngest and not the newest. She was about forty-five and she was four feet ten and she had a thumb gone off the right hand at the first joint and she was holding a pencil in what was left of the thumb and two fingers, the way a person holds a pencil who has never held one any other way.

She had been on that floor sixteen years.

She looked up. She did not startle and she did not get up.

“You are the woman from the gate,” she said.

“No. I am the woman at the gate. I have never been inside it.”

“There is no difference in this building,” she said. “Sit down.”

## What She Was Worth In That Room

She was not let in so much as not made to leave.

The woman asked her three questions in ten minutes and they were about columns, and they were the ordinary questions of a trade: which book a column had come out of, in which month, and how many days at the foot of it.

She answered all three.

“You read quickly,” the woman said. “You are quicker than the boy who does it and he is twenty-two and has been doing it two years.”

And then she said the rest of it, in the ordinary voice, the way a person gives a price.

“There is no line on this floor,” she said. “There is a line on this floor for a person who has been on it long enough that somebody else can do their work while they are at the water. There are three of those and one of them is me. The work is the columns and the columns come up the stair once a month with a cord round them and they do not stop.”

She put her pencil down flat on the open page.

“You walked up nine steps with a strip in your hand and there was not going to be anybody stopping you,” she said. “That is not the same as there being a place here. Nine steps are nine steps.”

## What Her Hands Did In There

Her hands went warm at the fourth hour of that afternoon.

She had them in her sleeves. She had had them in her sleeves in every room she had stood in for seven months, because her hands were cold and a hand that is cold in a warm room is a hand somebody asks about.

They were not cold.

She took her left hand out of her sleeve and put the back of it against the table, which was cold, and the table was colder than it should have been and the back of her hand was warm against it, and she put it back in her sleeve about as fast as she could get it out.

She had got one hand warm on the first of a month at the sixth hour in a room with nine men in it, with a man’s hand on the floor and his own skin coming up in blisters, and she had got nothing since, and now she was sitting at a table with forty books open on it and her hand was warm against cold wood.

She worked it out there and then, with her hand back in her sleeve and her eyes on an open page.

It is not the place. There is no place. A fire in a corner of a shed at a mill is the same thing as a fire in a floor at the top of nine steps, and the difference between the two is whether she is the only living thing in the room.

## Her Name

The woman got up at the fifth hour and squared four of the books up against the middle of the table and put the pencil in a groove cut in the edge of it.

“Rao,” she said.

“Rao.”

“That is mine,” she said. “It is not a word about anything. There is nobody else on this ground called Rao, and I have not gone by another thing since I was nineteen.”

She stood at the end of the table with her hand on the back of a chair.

“What is yours.”

And Ning Yan sat at a table with forty books open on it with her hand warm inside her sleeve and looked at a woman forty-five years old who had asked her three ordinary questions and told her the price of herself in eleven words, and did not give it.

She had said her name twice on that ground in seven months, both times in daylight, both times in front of people, and both times it had gone into a book and come back down a road in a hand she could not read.

“I have not got one to use here,” she said.

Rao looked at her for about two seconds and then she nodded and picked the pencil up off the groove.

“That is the first sensible thing anybody has said on that stair in about four years,” she said, “and it is still true that it does not put you a place at this table.”

## The Seventh And The Eighth

She went up on the seventh and on the eighth, at the third hour, with the strip in her left hand, and both times Rao gave her the edge of the table and nothing else, and both times she stood in a room the size of a hall with the two cold windows at the north end and forty books open on it and did not touch one of them.

On the eighth, at the fourth hour, Rao put a book round in front of her and turned it so the column faced her.

“Say the dates on that one out loud,” she said. “From the top.”

She said twenty-nine dates out loud in the ordinary voice, going down, the way you read a board from the bottom up and the way she had read a board in a gateway every second day for two months, and the room was very quiet, and the only other living thing in it was reading a page.

Rao listened to all twenty-nine of them and then put her thumb on the eighth.

“This one is wrong,” she said. “It is the eleventh here and it is the tenth in the book underneath it and somebody has copied a day across by the shape of it and not by the number.”

And she took a small knife out of the groove and lifted the corner of the leaf and looked at the margin, and then she looked at Ning Yan.

“Say that date again,” she said.

“The eleventh.”

“No, once more, out loud, the way you said the other twenty-eight,” she said. “That is how I know whether you are reading or whether you are getting it out of the shape.”

## The Plain Part About One Person

She is eighteen years old and somebody on the top floor of the only building inside nine days of road that has open books in it has told her that there is no place there for her, in eleven words, in the ordinary voice, and she was not angry for even a minute about it.

That is new. Every room she has walked into for seven months has priced her and she has been angry about it for two minutes each time and then walked the anger down on a made road.

Rao did not price her. Rao told her the terms of a trade she was outside of, the way a man behind a counter tells a person what a thing costs when they have not asked.

She wants to stand at that table. She has wanted to stand in front of an open book since she was twelve years old in a county that no longer exists, and there has never been a room on nine days of road with one open in it.

She is standing at the edge of one now, twice a week, with a strip in her left hand she cannot read, and the whole of what she has been asked to do in two days is say twenty-nine dates out loud in the ordinary voice.

On the eighth of a month a woman on the top floor asked her whether she was reading a column out loud or reading the shape of it, and made her do the eighth date over to find out, and the second time she said it she got it right because the first time she had been doing the first thing and not the second.

And she had four fingers and a thumb and a cold hand on every road between the shed and that table, and it was warm on the table, and the room was full of people in the way a room is full when there is one other living thing in it and it is forty-five years old.
