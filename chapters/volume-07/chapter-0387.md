# Spent On Nothing

The arm came back over two days and then it did not come back.

On the twenty-third she could get the four fingers and the thumb halfway to the palm. On the twenty-fourth, at the sixth hour, she got them shut. She shut them nine times, and the ninth time it took four seconds and the other eight were quicker.

On the twenty-fifth she could not get them shut at all.

That is what four fingers and a thumb is on a hand where the short bone in the forearm has been out of line since she was fifteen, and she had been getting away with it for four years because a closed fist is not in the eight of eleven holds she has and never was.

So she has lost the last of it and she lost it on the floor of a four-foot cut at the fifth hour on the twenty-first of a month, and she could not have told a living soul inside nine days of road.

## The Second Hour Of The Twenty-Fourth

She lay on the boards at the second hour and put her left hand flat on the boards, the way she has done every night for four years, and said it out loud because it was the second hour and there was not a living soul in the room.

“A ditch at the north end, four hundred yards, by the length, four feet, off a listed line between the twentieth and the twenty-second of a month.”

She said the whole of it in the ordinary voice, in the order a thing is done, the way she said it at the second hour on the first of a month a month ago for a hedge at the east end.

Three days and six hours.

She said it a second time, because he had said it to her twice on the last one and she had not understood until the second one that he was saying it twice for her and not for himself.

Three days and six hours, and it is out of the ground under her and it is not a thing she can put back in a book or on a board, and it is going out of the ground under her now.

And then she sat up and put her palm flat on the flat stone by the door, which was cold, and her hand was cold, and the two of them were the same temperature, and for the first time in five weeks she was not sure that was supposed to happen yet.

## What He Said About It, And When

You are going to spend that before you have had a night with it. That is not me telling you what to do, it is the shape of a person who has a number in the morning and spends it in the afternoon.

I have not said a number out loud in four years. I am saying it to you twice because you are going to spend it in about an hour and I would rather you had heard it than worked it out.

Three days and six hours.

Three days and six hours.

And then there was nothing for ten minutes, which is the part I do not have a use for, and then he said: put it in the floor of that shed and not in a man and not in a book, and it does not come back, and you will not feel it go.

## The Gate On The Twenty-Fourth

Eight coppers went west on the twenty-second of a month and there was bread for five days, and on the twenty-fourth she went out to the gate at the sixth hour and dug the run in two lengths, four feet for the first six and three and a half for the rest of it, and the bar went in where the ground was hard and it came up easy.

Pei came out at the eighth hour and stood at the end of the run looking at it.

“You have changed the shape of it,” he said.

“Yes.”

“You have cut it four where it was three and a half and three and a half where it was three and a half, and the six feet at four is not on the low side, and I have got one thing I am allowed to say and I said it on the sixteenth and I am not saying it again.”

“You do not have to say it.”

“I am aware that I do not have to say it,” Pei said, “and I am going to go and have my cup, because you have dug a run that will still be a run in the eleventh month and that is the work.”

He went inside and she stood in the gateway for two minutes with the iron in her hand and a copper in the fold, and it was the first thing in six weeks that had gone right.

## What It Cost

She spent it on nothing.

That is what she has done with the last one and it is not a rule and it is not a doctrine. It is that there is nothing on nine days of road she can buy with it and nothing on nine days of road that will put it back, and she did not want a hold and she did not want a count of breath and she did not want her hand back.

It went out of the ground under her into the boards of a shed at a mill whose wheel has not turned since before she came, and it did not come back.

## The Twenty-Fifth

She went up the mill road at the third hour on the twenty-fifth with her strip in her left hand, and she sat at the edge of the table with forty books open on it for about an hour and a quarter with a woman of forty-five four feet off the other end of it.

Her hands were cold for the whole of the hour and a quarter.

She put the back of the left one against the table twice, in between two columns she was reading out loud, and it was cold against cold wood, and it was the same temperature as the wood, and it told her nothing.

And at the fourth hour she said, out loud, in a room, to a woman:

“The room is cold.”

Rao put her pencil down.

“It is always cold at this end,” she said. “The north end has not been warm since before I came on it and I have a stove at that end that I light in the twelfth month and put out in the second.”

“No,” Ning Yan said. “It is colder.”

Rao looked at her for three seconds and then she said: “Then you should go down and warm your hands at the stove on the floor below me, and come back up, and that is not a thing I am going to have you sit here through.”

And she went down the eleven steps and put her hands on the stove in that low room until they hurt, and she came back up the nine and put her hands flat on the table, and they were cold again inside a minute.

She was not sure until the twenty-sixth whether that was going to be the price of a ditch or whether it was one afternoon.

## The Twenty-Sixth

On the twenty-sixth, at the third hour, she went up and sat at the edge of the table and her hands were warm inside her sleeves inside ten minutes and stayed warm for an hour.

The back of the left one went against the table and it was warm against cold wood, and she took it away again.

Rao did not ask her anything about that and did not look up.

## The Plain Part About One Person

She is eighteen and she cannot close her right hand and she cannot write a false line and the fire in her own shed has come up twice in fifty-two days and only ever with another living thing in the room.

Three days and six hours went out of the ground under her on the second hour of the twenty-fourth of a month and came back as a cold afternoon on the top floor of the only building inside nine days of road with open books in it, for about a day and a half.

That is the price. She cannot say whether it was a day and a half or whether it is the start of something, and there was nobody inside nine days of road she could ask and nobody she could tell.

What she wanted on the twenty-fifth, more than she wanted a hand, was to know whether it was a day and a half.

## What Happened At The Table On The Twenty-Seventh

She went up at the third hour on the twenty-seventh with her strip in her left hand and sat at the edge of the table for an hour and read six columns out loud, and her hands were warm the whole hour.

At the fourth hour she had nothing left to read and did not get up.

Rao went on down a column for four minutes and then she put the pencil down flat and turned round on her chair and said:

“What do you come up here for.”

It is the first question anybody has asked her in seven months that is not about a column.

She sat at the edge of that table for about nine seconds and worked out four answers, and three of them were true and did not go far enough, and the fourth one was the whole of it and she was not going to say it at the fourth hour on the twenty-seventh of a month in a room with forty books open on it.

“I come up here because my hands are warm,” she said.

“No, you do not,” Rao said. “That is what it is and that is not what it is. You have been on this floor eight times in a month and you have read eleven columns and you have carried a roll of strips up a turn in a landing once, and there is not one line here with your name on it, and I have not asked you before because you were not here long enough to ask.”

She picked the pencil up.

“You have got until the fifth hour to think about it and then I am going to read a book,” she said, “and you can sit there or you can go down.”

She went down the nine steps and the eleven and down the mill road and out to the gate and got a fifth of a reach, and it came up easy, and she put the clay in the barrow.

She had a day before she went up that stair again, and she had worked out on the walk that there was one thing she could say out loud in that room that she had not said in any room in seven months, and that it was two words, and that one of them was a woman’s name and she had not used it in the street.

She had never used anybody’s name in the street. She had used her own twice.
