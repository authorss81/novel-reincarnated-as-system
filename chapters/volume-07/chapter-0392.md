# Thirty-One Dressed Stones

The board at the works is three miles east of the shed and it is a board the size of a door with lines rubbed into it and a rail along the top, and she has read it from the bottom up every second day for two months.

On the twelfth of a month it had eight lines on it.

It had had eleven on the first of that month. Three had gone off in the four days before, and one of them had been a ditch line at the north end of the yard at the turn with a rubbed patch in the board where it used to be, and she could still see the patch from four feet off.

The eight were dressed stone by the ton, by the load, and by the hundredweight, and every one of them was a thing her right hand could not take.

## The One That Was New

It was second from the bottom and it had a date in the margin against it.

She read it eleven times with the flat of her second finger on the rail, which is how she reads everything.

“Sill course, south end, yard at the turn. By the weight. Thirty-one dressed stones, four feet of bed. Off the twenty-eighth.”

The margin had a day against the off and no day against the on, and the rule for that was plain: a line with no day against the start goes on when it is written, and a line with a day against the end comes off that day whether or not it is done.

That is twenty-six days from the twelfth.

## What A Sill Is

She did not know what a sill was and she worked it out on the walk east by looking at every building between the shed and the turn.

A sill is the lowest course of dressed stone in a wall and it takes the weight of everything above it, and everything above it has to sit square, and it sits on four feet of bed, and the bed is clay and broken stone and it is dug out by the length and it goes back in by the weight.

Thirty-one dressed stones is not thirty-one things to carry. It is four feet of bed going in along six hundred feet of line and thirty-one stones coming down on top of it square, and every one of them has to be where it is going before the one beside it, because stone does not slide.

She got to the yard at the turn at the fifth hour of the thirteenth and stood at the office end and read the board inside the door, and the sill line was second from the bottom there too with the twenty-eighth against it.

## The Thirteenth

The man with a rule in his hand was on the low side of the yard with a length of cord in his pocket and a piece of chalk in his hand.

She has had two conversations with him about work in seven months and both of them have been correct, and the second of them was in the middle of a month with nine men within hearing and he gave her a day early and said plainly that he was telling her then so that it was not a thing that happened to her tomorrow.

He did not look up.

“It comes off the twenty-eighth,” he said.

“I can read the margin.”

“You can read the margin and I am going to say it out loud anyway,” he said. “It is on the board and it comes off on the twenty-eighth and I have said that date out loud to every man on this yard and I have said it out loud to you, and you are the ninth of nine and I am not putting you on it and I am not going to.”

He put the chalk in his pocket.

“You will not be paid anything for any part of that line.”

“I have not asked you for anything.”

“No,” he said. “You have not, and that is why I am saying the date.”

## The South End

She went and looked at it because there is nothing else to do with a date.

The south end of that yard is forty yards long and nine wide between the office wall and a wall that has not been touched in years, and the ground is the lowest ground on that site and it is the ground that goes soft at the end of a month, which is the whole of the reason there is a day in the margin.

There were nine men on it.

Four will do, and has done, for as long as that yard has been a yard. There is a bar, a chain, a wedge, a plank, a bucket of water and a strip of plank for the line, and that is the whole of the equipment, and on the south end there is a stone bed already dug out for eleven feet of it with the clay stacked on a barrow at the near end.

And in the middle of the bed there was one dressed stone already laid and not squared, and a second one beside it that two men had got a bar under and had not moved.

She stood nine feet off and watched them for a quarter of an hour.

They did it four times. A bar under the near end, a chain round the far, one man on each end of the bar and a third with his boot on the bed keeping the stone from riding up. They got it up half an inch four times and every time it came back and the chalk line under it did not move.

“You are not going to get that today,” said a voice behind her.

The man with the rule in his hand had followed her down the yard and was standing at her shoulder.

“Twenty-eighth,” he said. “I am short two men since the last of a month and I have been short two men for three weeks and that is the whole of what is wrong with that stone and the whole of what is wrong with the date.”

He looked at the two men on the bar and then at the chalk.

“It is not the ground and it is not the stone,” he said. “It is that I have not got two men and there is no version of it where a stone that size comes up without two men.”

## The Fourteenth

She went up the mill road at the third hour on the fourteenth with the strip in her left hand and sat at the edge of the table with forty books open on it and read six columns out loud in the ordinary voice for two hours.

The fifth one she read had a yard at the turn in it.

It was the ordinary shape. Four days down the margin against a line at the top, the place, the nine men, the count of loads, and at the foot of it a number of hours that was not hers and was not going to be hers.

She read it out loud at the ordinary speed with a woman of forty-five four feet off the other end of the table listening to every word of it.

Rao did not say one thing about it for two days.

## The Plain Part About One Person

She wanted her hours on that page.

That was the want, and she worked it out on the walk up the mill road with her hand on the rail, and it was a small want and she was ashamed of it inside a minute, and she did it anyway.

She is eighteen years old. She has been four times up nine steps in a month for nothing and she has read eleven thousand dates out loud to a woman who cannot read a hand she has never seen. And on the fifth of a month that day she read a yard out loud that she had stood at, at nine feet off, for a quarter of an hour, and it was not hers.

A column is read out. That is all it does. She can do the one thing that building was built to do and she cannot be given a line of it.

## What She Said At The Fifth Hour

“The fifth column I read this morning.”

“Yes.”

“It has the south end of a yard in it.”

“It has eleven months of that yard in it,” Rao said. “They come up twice a month and I have read them for years and the man with the rule in his hand is in one in four of them and he has never once asked me anything about them either.”

She put the pencil down flat.

“He sets those lines himself and he comes in here twice a month and reads what has come back and he has never once asked me what the numbers are for. And you have not asked him for a line and he has not offered you one, and I am not going to tell either of you what to do about that.”

## What Was In Her Head On The Road Back

Four miles of made road and a hedge and a field with a gate that does not shut, and she walked all of it inside her own head the way she walks every road.

“You have moved one stone in your life and you did not touch it. It went the length of your arm and it stopped and four men nine feet off did not look up. If it happens a second time on purpose, a man with a rule in his hand will have to write down what happened at the south end of his yard, and he will be right to, and there will be nothing on nine days of road that can be entered against it.”

“And if you do not do it, two men short is two men short and that stone is going to be in the wrong place on the twenty-eighth.”

She worked it out at the field gate. Both of those were true and there was no third one, and she had never before in five months had two things that were both true and no room to put a third thing between them.

## What She Carried Down The Road

She was down the mill road at the fifth hour with seven coppers and a strip against her ribs and she stood a hundred yards short of the counting house with her hand on the rail for a minute.

There are two men short at a yard four miles east of here, and a stone in a bed that will not come up, and the ground goes soft at the end of a month.

And she is the ninth of nine at that yard, and the board has no line on it she can take, and there is nothing on nine days of road she can pay with and nothing anybody in it is entitled to hand her.

She had one thing she had never used for anything. She had carried it since the twenty-second of a month four months back, when a stone she never touched went the length of her own arm on a flat past the last hedge and stopped and was set down clean in front of four men nine feet off who did not look up.

It had happened once. It had never happened on purpose and she had no way of knowing whether it would happen a second time and she had not asked, because there is no version of asking that is not a question in a room.

The twenty-eighth was fourteen days off and there was a bar under a stone at the south end of a yard and two men short.
