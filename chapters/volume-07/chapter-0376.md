# Seven In And Eight Out

She went west on the twentieth of a month with eight coppers and came back with four loaves and nothing else, and the eight miles there and back is a day and a half of what she was doing before the season turned.

The woman at the store had not asked her a question in seven months and did not ask her one then. She counted the coppers into her palm and put the four loaves on the counter and said that the third loaf off the shelf went stale first and would not keep past the fourth day, and that was the whole of the conversation.

Her boy was sitting on the sill with his boots off and he asked her, with his feet swinging, whether she was going past the mill or up to the works, and she said she was going to the shed, and he said that was neither, and she said no, and he said that was all right, and went back to his boots.

She had not talked to anybody under fifteen for about a year. It took about nine seconds and she walked two miles afterwards thinking about a boy on a sill with his boots off, which was an absurd thing to spend two miles on and she did it anyway.

## The Eighteen, The Nineteenth

Two days at the gate and nothing coming up out of the drain but clay, which she put in a barrow and carried out and piled against the outside wall, and there were five barrow-loads against that wall now and a sixth standing on its handles.

The cut came up easy at three and a half. It came up so easy that on the nineteenth she got out of it in two hours where it had taken two and a half at four feet.

She stood in the gateway with the iron in her hand and worked out that she was getting a copper a day for two hours instead of two and a half, and that by the end of the season at that rate the ground would be soft enough on the low side that three and a half feet would be a hole.

That was a thing she would have to come back to. There was a version of the rest of the season where she earned a copper a day for an hour and three quarters, and there was a version where she was in a hole at three and a half on the low side with four fingers and a thumb, and a man at a gate forty minutes away.

She did not tell Pei. She had told him about the depth once, on the sixteenth, and he had said it once more and gone inside, and a man at a gate has one thing he is allowed to say and she was not going to spend it twice.

## The Board, Turned Round

On the twentieth, at the tenth hour, she stood in the gateway of the works and the board was turned round.

Not taken off. Turned round, so that the eleven lines faced out into the gateway instead of facing the wall, with the cover up over the top of it and the wet edge of the cover standing up like a hood.

The man who kept the list was standing at the bench with his back to the gateway and his nib down, and four men were standing in the yard with their hands empty, which is a thing that does not happen on a working morning.

For two months she had stood in that gateway every second day reading a board that could not pay her.

He turned round and looked at her and then went and took the cap off the nib and put it back on.

“That is not for you,” he said.

“I know it is not for me. I have read it every second day for two months and I have never once said I thought it was for me.”

“I have not had a person standing at that wall reading it since the twenty-third of a month,” he said, “and I have had nine men come off it in that time, and a man who reads a wall for two months is not standing at a wall.”

He put the cap on the nib again.

“It is turned because it is easier to read it off the wall than to write nine slips and count them, and it is turned whether you are standing there or not, and it was not turned for you, and I would rather you heard that from me than worked it out from the cover being up.”

That was all of it, and he went back to the bench.

And she stood nine feet off in the gateway with a full board turned out in front of her and eleven dressed-stone lines on it priced by the ton, and she was the reason it had been turned round in the sense that she was the only person on that ground who would have noticed either way, and he was right that it was not for her.

## The Arithmetic She Did Not Want

Seven days of gate is seven coppers. Five days of bread at two coppers a loaf, four loaves, is eight.

She was a copper short every week and had been since the day she came past this mill, and there had never been a week since that she had not been a copper short.

So she worked out the other end of it on the twenty-first, standing at the shed door with the flat of her second finger on the boards, and it came out in a shape she had not been looking at.

Seven coppers a week. Bread eight. And the only other thing anybody on that ground sold her was clay, at three coppers a third of a sack, and she had five barrow-loads of clay against her own wall that she had dug out of a drain at a fifth of a reach a day and had not been able to use for two weeks.

So: she could go without two days a week, and come out a copper and a half a week ahead, and the whole of what a copper and a half a week would buy on nine days of road was clay.

And she could not go without five days a week, because she does not have a body that goes without five days a week. That is not a thing she has found out. She went four days on the twelfth to the sixteenth with no bread at all in that ground and got a fifth of a reach out of a drain every one of them, and the drain came up easy, and she was not tired at the end of any of them.

She worked that out twice and got the same thing.

Seven in and eight out, and the missing copper coming off a stock that is now nothing at all, and the only thing on that ground she can buy with money is the one thing she cannot use.

## What She Had Not Bought In Seven Months

She stood in the doorway and looked at the barrows.

She had not bought anything since before she came past this mill. Nothing at all. Everything she had put inside that shed she had taken out of a drain, off a dead hedge, or out of a bucket in an office end at a yard that does not have her name on anything.

There had been cloth, because there had been a strip in her coat for six months, and the strip had come off a cart and not off a shop.

That was the whole of the money. It was not a hardship she had worked her way up to. It was a floor she was standing on, and she was eighteen, and the floor went out from under her about two coppers a week and had been going out for seven months.

## Standing In A Room To See What It Was Like

She went up the mill road on the twenty-first at the second hour with the bar over her shoulder and stood in the counting house for about twenty-five minutes and said nothing to anybody.

The counter was busy from about the second hour and there were nine men in the room and the stove going and the man behind it writing, and the stair at the back went up one flight with a man on the top step and sheets squared up beside him.

She stood at the end of the counter with her hands in her sleeves like a person waiting for something.

The man behind the counter finished what he was writing, and looked at her, and went back to it, and then looked at her again.

“You are standing at my counter,” he said, “and you have had nothing to say for twenty minutes, and I have four things to do before the hour turns, and if you want something you say it, and if you do not want something then this is a bad place for you to be standing.”

She said she knew that.

“Then go and stand somewhere else,” he said. “I am not saying that to be hard on you. There is about nine people in this room and four of them are waiting on me and one of them is waiting on the stair, and the fifth thing that happens in a room this size is somebody being in it.”

He put the pen down and picked it up again.

“Everything that comes into this room gets put in this room,” he said. “That is not a threat and it is not a policy. It is a low building with a counter and nine men in it.”

She took the bar off her shoulder and went out and down the road in the light.

## The Plain Part About One Person

She had one question a month in her and she had not spent it, and she had gone eleven days carrying it toward a fire that would not come up, and it had not once occurred to her that a person who is light in the head for three days out of seven does not go and buy bread with eight coppers on the twentieth of a month.

It occurred to her at the eleventh hour on the twenty-first, standing in the shed doorway with nothing in her hands.

She went without. She had been going without all along and she had been buying as well, and both of those had been going into the same hole.

She worked out that if she went without two days in seven she would be a copper and a half a week ahead, and in two months she would have thirteen coppers, and she looked at the barrows of clay against her wall and at the ring that would not take them.

Thirteen coppers of clay is about two thirds of a sack, and a third of a sack is three coppers at the yard, and there is no ring on nine days of road that takes clay, and there has not been one since the second of a month.

She had worked out on the seventeenth that she had to stand in a room on the first of a month with a man in it she had never spoken to.

On the twenty-first she worked out that she would arrive there hungry, which she had not thought about, and that she was going to go without for the three days in between on purpose.
