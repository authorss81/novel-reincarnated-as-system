# A Line In Her Own Hand

She went up the mill road on the eleventh of a month at the third hour with her strip in her left hand and a fifth of a sack of clay in a barrow at the shed, and there were eight coppers in the fold in the inside of the door frame and bread for five days.

The stage came at the seventh hour on a table with forty books open on it.

## The Sixth Hour

Rao put a leaf flat on the table and squared it and gave her the pencil.

“The eleventh on this one is wrong,” she said. “It is the eleventh in the copy and it is the tenth in the book underneath it. Put the tenth back in the copy, in the margin, in the ordinary hand, and put your mark under it.”

She said the last part without looking up.

“My mark is my mark,” she said. “Yours is yours. That is how a room keeps a thing straight and it is not a favour to anybody.”

“There is nobody else on this floor to sign it.”

“There is nobody else on this floor,” Rao agreed, “which is why I am asking you and not asking you.”

She went out at the sixth hour to the water, and took her cup, and shut the door behind her, which is a thing a person does on a floor where they have been for sixteen years and does not mean anything by it.

## The Seventh Hour

It came up out of the boards.

She was standing at the edge of the table with the pencil in her left hand and her right arm open, and it came up out of the floor of a room the way it had come up out of a flat past the last hedge with nine men on a bank above her, and it went under the table and under the twenty books that were open on it and up through the boards, and every page of every one of them stayed where it was.

The window at the north end had the light coming across it at a low angle and there was nothing in the room to throw a shadow.

[Ning Yan, past the line, servant-born, under no ceiling any county holds. Nascent Soul, stage three, is held.]

It said its one sentence and stopped.

There were eight feet of ceiling in that room and the whole of it happened in the floor, and it took four seconds, and the door was shut, and she stood at the edge of a table with a pencil in her hand and waited two minutes for something to go that went every other time.

Nothing went.

## What Was In The Room

The door opened at ten minutes past the seventh hour and a woman of forty-five came in with an empty cup in her hand.

She stopped in the doorway.

She did not come further in and she did not go out again. She stood on the boards with the cup in her hand and she looked at Ning Yan, and then at the table, and then at the twenty books open on it with every page exactly where it had been, and then at the floor between the table and the door.

Then she came in and shut the door behind her.

“Nothing fell,” she said.

“No.”

“Nothing moved and nothing fell,” Rao said, “and the books were open at the angles they were open at, and I have been in this room sixteen years and I have never once come into it and found the air like this.”

She put the cup down on the edge of the table.

“Say what it was,” she said, “and then do not say it twice, and I am not going to ask you a second time in my life.”

## What She Said Once

“There is a stage,” Ning Yan said. “It came on the third of a month about five weeks back, out on a bank a mile and a quarter east of the mill, with nine men on it and two of them nine feet off, and they saw that one happen.”

Rao did not look at her.

“I did not see that,” she said.

“No.”

“Then nobody saw this one,” Rao said, “because the room was empty, and I came in after it had gone. That is true and you did not have to tell me any of the rest of it.”

She picked the cup up off the table again.

“You told me a thing in a room with open books in it,” she said, “and you told it to me once, and I am the only person on nine days of road whose trade is a piece of paper and a pencil. That is an unlucky choice and you made it anyway, and I am not going to pretend to you that I do not know what it is.”

Then she went and sat down at the far end of the table and put her pencil down flat on the page in front of her.

“I am not going to write it down,” she said. “Not tonight and not at the end of the month and not in sixteen years. Do not thank me, because I have not done anything. A woman who has been at a table for sixteen years can look at a room and not write down what she saw in it. That is what the table is for.”

## The Pencil

The leaf was still lying on the table where she had left it, and the pencil was still in her left hand.

She went back to it.

The copy said the eleventh and the book underneath said the tenth, and she was to put the tenth back in the copy, in the margin, in the ordinary hand, with her own mark under it.

She did it. It took her a minute and a half, and the ten went in her own hand in the ordinary shape, and she put a mark under it that was a line and a notch, which is what she has used since she was twelve because she could not make a letter any smaller than the rest.

And then she went to the bottom of the leaf, where there is about two inches of margin, and she took the pencil and she did a thing.

## What Her Hand Would Not Do

She wanted to write a figure that was not in the column.

That is all. It is not a lie anybody wanted and it is not in anybody’s book. It is two inches of margin at the bottom of a leaf on a table in a cold room and a pencil in the hand of a woman who has been writing in the ordinary hand since she was six years old.

She set four fingers and a thumb on it and she wrote a seven where the column said a four.

Her hand wrote a four.

She took the leaf and turned it round and looked at it and there was a four in it, in her own hand, in her own writing, exactly the shape of the four in the column thirty lines up.

She did it five times over a quarter of an hour.

She set up to it with the number in her head and her hand went and made the number that was true, and there is no more to say about what it did than that, and she tried it fast and she tried it slow and she tried it with her left hand and she tried it upside down.

## The Plain Part About One Person

She is eighteen. She is sitting at the top of nine steps with a pencil in her hand, and she cannot write down a thing that is not true.

That is new, and it is not a finger and it is not a hold and it is not a count of breath and it does not touch her body at all.

A line she puts in ink in her own hand is a thing she means.

She can still say a false thing out loud, and she has done it to four people in seven months, and every one of them believed her and one of them put it in a book.

She can no longer enter a false line in any book on nine days of road, and there is no hand she can pay to enter one for her.

She had five false lines in her when she came up that stair at the third hour, and the first one was the date she was about to put in the margin. That one was true and she wrote it. The other four were things about herself that she had said out loud in daylight and would have liked to have written down somewhere.

She is the only person on nine days of road who knows what is in the fine hand about her, and she cannot write it, and she cannot pay anybody to.

You have got a thing you cannot do now. That is not the same as losing a thing you had.

## What She Did With The Pencil

She put the pencil down in the groove in the edge of the table, where it lives, because a pencil in the groove on that floor is the whole of the sum of what she is worth there.

She did not put the leaf in the stove at the end of that room, and Rao did not ask her to, and there is a stove at the north end of that floor and it is the only warm thing on it.

She carried it down nine steps and eleven and down the mill road and out to the gate with the leaf folded in four in her left hand, and she got a fifth of a reach at three and a half feet and the bar would not hold in the ground, and she went down to three feet and filled in the four feet and got the same copper for it.

There is less money in the ground at three feet than there is at four and she is going to have to work out a way to keep the depth and not put her bar in a hole, and she had not worked it out yet at the eighth hour on the eleventh of a month, with a leaf folded in four in her coat that has two inches of margin at the bottom of it with a five written on it five times over.

And a woman forty-five years old at the far end of that table had put her pencil down flat on her page and said I am not going to write it down, and she had not asked for anything when she said it, and Ning Yan had not said thank you, because thank you is a thing that goes in a room.
