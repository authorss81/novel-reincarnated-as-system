# The Gate On The Made Road

The second of a month, at about the seventh hour, which is the hour she has gone up that road at for seven months, and she went a mile and a half further than she had ever gone.

She had been to the counting house forty times in seven months and had never once gone past it, because there is nothing past it.

Past the counting house the made road goes on two miles between a field on one side and a hedge on the other, and stops at a post with a board on it. She has not needed anything at that post since the twenty-fifth of a month four months back, when she left a book of hers on a stone under a ladder and walked away from it.

She went past it on the second of a month at the eighth hour.

## The Man At The Hinge

The gate is a hundred yards short of the post, on the field side of the road, and it is two posts and a bar and a hinge, and there is a man at it.

He was sixty, more or less, and had a coat on at the eighth hour in a month that had not turned warm yet, and he had been at that hinge long enough to have worn a shape in the ground where he stands.

He watched her come up the road and stop and stand at his bar, and he said nothing for two minutes, and then he said:

“You are the woman at Pei’s gate.”

“I am.”

“You have been walking that road for seven months and you have never once gone past me.”

“No.”

He turned round and looked out over his field the way a man does when he is establishing which field he is talking about.

“That is forty acres and it is mine and it was my father’s and it was my grandfather’s before him,” he said. “It is the best ground for four miles in this direction. And there is a thing on a line about nine days of road west of here that no person on this ground has ever needed one thing out of.”

## What Is There And What Is Not

“You are looking at that road,” he said, “and I have watched four people look at that road in twenty years and two of them wanted something and two of them had lost something. You have got neither of those.”

“I am looking at the gate.”

“You are looking at the gate.”

He put both hands flat on the bar.

“There is a thing on a line about nine days of road west of here and it is walked and mended and copied and vouched for, and it is kept, and it is kept so that this ground stays worth farming.”

He said it the way a man says the price of a field.

“Forty acres here are worth what forty acres here are worth because of a thing I have never seen and could not get to if I wanted it,” he said. “My father never asked. I never have. I have a gate and a woman inside it and two boys on this ground and I shut this bar from the dark to the dark, and that is what I am for.”

## The Four Things He Said And Did Not Say

He looked at her for as long as it takes to count three.

“Nobody has come down that road in sixty years asking me what it is for,” he said. “Not my father. Not me.”

“I am not asking either.”

“You are not asking because you have worked it out already and I have not, because I have never had a reason to work it out.” He came off the hinge. “Somebody else keeps the line. I have watched them go past twice a day for forty years and I have never stopped one and asked him his name, and he would not have told me, and he would have been right not to.”

He looked at her arm then. Two seconds, the same two seconds a man with a paste pot gave her on the first, and she understood that every person on that ground who looks at her looks at that first.

“You are going to stand at that post about four times a month now,” he said. “And I am going to see you do it. And I am not going to ask you about it, and if you want to stand here in the morning there is a bench under the sycamore and it is not mine and no person has sat on it since the woman who lived here died.”

That was the whole of it. He shut the bar.

## What Was On The Post

She stood at his gate for ten minutes and then walked the hundred yards on to the post.

It is a post and a board and a ladder, and the board has a strip going up it in a hand she cannot read, and the strip is a month old and the last day on it is the twenty-eighth of a month about three weeks back.

Under the ladder, on a stone, where he put it because it is the driest thing he has, is a book of hers.

She did not touch it.

She stood four feet off it for the length of a minute and looked at the edge of it going into the shadow of the stone, and there was a rag over the corner of it, and the rag had been put there and taken away and put there again by somebody who cannot read a word in it and did not want the rain getting at the paper.

It had been moved. She had put it down flat on the stone in the shade of the step and it was flat on the stone in the shade of the step, but the edge nearest the wall was two inches out from where she had left it, and somebody had taken it up and looked at it and put it down again.

There is a way she has of reading a board by shape, and she has been doing it since she was twelve, and it is the only way she has ever had of reading anything written about her. Four days on a strip and one day on a strip are not the same shape. She stood four feet off that board and counted the days on it by the gaps between the marks, and got thirty-one, and did not know whether that was a month or two.

So she went and left it.

That is the whole of a thing four years old that is hers and that no person on nine days of road can read. The man who has had it four months has guarded it better than anybody who could read it ever would, and he cannot read one word of it, and she has not given him a reason and he has not asked her for one.

She walked back to the counting house, and the door was open and there were nine men in the room and the stove going, and the seasonal book was lying shut on the end of the counter where it had been lying shut since the twenty-sixth of a month six months back.

She did not go in. She stood in the gateway for a minute and looked at the shut book through the door, and then she went down the mill road to the shed, and it was the first road she had walked in seven months that had nothing to do with a copper or a loaf or a board or a cup of something hot.

## Two Nights

She had not slept on the first or the second, and the reason was not that she was thinking about him.

The reason was the fourth hour. She had been getting up at the fourth hour every morning for four months because a fire lit itself at the second hour and was not there when she got up, and then for a month there was nothing to get up for at all, and she had been getting up at the fourth hour anyway out of a habit that had stopped having anything behind it.

So on the night of the first she got up at the fourth hour and went and put a lump in the ring and it came up a finger’s width, and she stood there in the dark and could not work out a single thing about the day.

And on the night of the second she got up at the fourth hour and did not go to the ring. She lay on the boards instead with her hands on her chest and worked out that the ring had come up a finger’s width and stopped every morning since the third of a month, and that twice in all of that the fire had come up, once in her own shed at the third hour on the second with a bar coming down on her and nine men on a bank above her, and once at the sixth hour in a low building with a man standing in it and a bundle of strips on the sill.

She had not done anything to bring that about.

She had stood four feet off a counter and said nothing for ten minutes and had said the word yes when she was asked, and a floor in a room had come up about a second and a half before it was under a man’s hand, and a man had got up and looked at his own palm and asked her a question in the ordinary voice.

There is no arranging of that.

She got up at the fourth hour on the third of a month out of pure habit, and there was no fire, and she had eight miles of walking in front of her and nothing to do with any of it.

He will be at that gate on the first of every month. He is not going to ask you why you are walking and he has told you so.

## The Plain Part About One Person

She is eighteen years old, and she burned a man’s hand on the first of a month, and he went up a ladder with a roll of strips under his one arm, and there is nothing at all she can do about it.

She has not slept on either of the two nights since.

What she wanted on the second of a month was somebody to be angry with her.

She had been angry twice in two months, two minutes each time, at two men who were right, and she had walked both of them down to nothing on a made road. She walked three miles up that road and back looking for it, and she found a gate with a man at it and a bench under a sycamore, and neither of those was it.

Sitting on a bench at a gate that is not his is a thing four people could agree about later. She has one question a month in her and had not spent this month’s, and she was not going to spend it on a bench under a sycamore.
