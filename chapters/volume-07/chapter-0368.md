# The Ninth Man

She had never been east of the turn. She went four miles up the made road on the fourteenth of a month at the sixth hour to find out whether there was anything at the turn, and there was a yard.

It was brick and timber and an acre and a half of it, with a wall round three sides and a made road going in at one corner and going on east out of the other, and a covered bay along the north side with a bench in it and a bar standing against the end post and a chain on the wall.

There were nine men on the boards inside the bay.

## The Yard

She had never seen a yard from the outside. She had been out of a bound seven months and she had worked a gate and a board and four lines off a wall and she had walked past nine men on a bank two hundred yards off, and that was the whole of what she knew about yards, and standing at the mouth of this one at the sixth hour with her hands in her sleeves took her a minute and a half.

The nine of them were not all working. Two were loading a cart and three were standing at the bench and the other four were out of sight behind the stack at the south end, and every one of them was a man who could lift a dressed sill two hundred and something of it.

She stood at the mouth of the bay and looked at nine men and she was the smallest thing in that bay. Not the quietest and not the newest and not the one who had come furthest.

She had a bar’s worth of reach in a day and eight holds in her left hand and four fingers and a thumb on her right and an arm that was open about half of the time.

And there were nine men in that bay who would have picked up the sill she was looking at with one hand and set it down without a count.

A stack of dressed sill stone stood against the south wall nine feet off. She was looking straight at it.

## The Man With The Rule In His Hand

He came out of the office end with a rule in his hand and eight inches of it out, and he was fifty or so and he had been doing it a long time because of how he stood in the doorway.

He looked at her from the head to the boots in two seconds, which is not long, and most of it was the right arm.

“You are the woman at Pei’s gate,” he said.

“Yes.”

“I have heard of you,” he said. “I hear things and I have never once gone and looked at a thing I have heard about.”

He put the rule back against his leg.

“Not in the sense that I do not care what a person can do,” he said. “In the sense that there are eleven men between here and the works and every one of them tells me something about the other ten by the afternoon. That is how a yard runs. You have got nine men on this board and I have got four days’ work in front of them and I have got a hedge line on there that is not going.”

“I can take a length.”

That came out of her before she had any idea she was going to say it, and it came out in a gateway ten feet from nine men, which is the second time in seven weeks she had said a thing in that voice in front of people.

## The Thing She Could Do

He looked at her long enough to count a thing out twice, and then he went to the bench and took the board off the wall and turned it round so she could read it.

There were nine lines on it and eight of them were dressed stone and the ninth said hedge, at the east end, by the length, one day.

“That one is not yours,” he said. “I am not putting it on your name and you are not going on this board and there is no name of yours going anywhere in this yard. I have been running this yard for nineteen years and I have never had a woman on this board and I am not starting at fifty.”

He put the board back.

“I want that hedge down by the length and I have got nine men and four days of other work and I do not want to stop a man off a sill to do a hedge. If you take that hedge down and put it on the barrow I will give you a cup of something and that is the end of it.”

“That is not money.”

“That is not money,” he agreed. “There is no money in it for you because it is on my board and my board is against this yard and my yard does not have a line for a pair of hands that came in on their own.”

## The Hedge

She took it down in five hours.

It was four hundred yards of old hedge at the east end and two thirds of it was dead wood and a barrow was standing on the track behind it with the boards up. She had a bar off the fence and she was on her own with four fingers and a thumb, and a bar goes under the dead wood and then you carry it and then you throw it, and it is measured by the length and it is not heavy until it is wrong.

She got it down in five hours and got it on the barrow in nine trips and the barrow took it to the south end, and about the ninth hour the two men who were loading the cart came and looked at what she had done and one of them said she had got the foot of it up wrong.

He was right. She had got the foot of it up wrong on the fourth trip and the whole of the last hundred yards came up in pieces instead of in length, and she did the last hundred yards again at the ninth hour in the dark and it took an hour and a half and she did it with her right arm open the whole of it.

That was the whole of what she could do and she had done it in a day and a half and there was a cup of something hot in her hands at the end of it from a woman in the office end who had not asked her name.

## The Thing She Could Not

He was waiting for her at the bench in the morning with the rule in his hand again, and he did not say good morning, and he put the rule against the south wall and pointed it at the stack.

“Take the sill line.”

The stack was against that wall. Two hundred and something of dressed sill stone, cut and squared and stacked, and eleven pieces to the tonne, and it was going east at the fifth hour to a yard four miles on, and there were nine men in that bay and every one of them could put two of those pieces up without counting.

“I cannot.”

“I have been watching you since yesterday morning,” he said. “I watched you take that hedge down at about the pace of a woman doing a length, and I watched you get the foot of it wrong and go back and do the last hundred yards again.”

He turned the rule over in his hand.

“I have watched you carry that bar in your left hand with your right arm open the whole way, and I worked out at about the second hour yesterday which of my nine you are, and it is the one that is not on the board.”

He did not say which one that was. He did not have to.

“That is the sill line,” he said. “That is four days for two men and it pays by the ton and it goes east on the twenty-second. And you are not going to touch it, and I was never going to ask you, and I have not asked you a second time and I am not going to.”

## Nine Men, None Of Them Wrong

He had been right about both of them and he had said neither of them to hurt her, and that was the whole of it.

He had asked her for a length because a length is a length and she had said she could take one and she had, and he had given her a cup of something hot out of the office end from a woman who had not asked her name.

And he had asked her for the sill because he had eight lines of dressed stone standing on a board that pays by the ton and he had nine men and four days.

Asking her was not an insult. It was the way a yard works. You look at what is standing in front of you and you find the place it goes, and she was the smallest thing in that bay and he had found her place in it in two seconds with a rule in his hand and he had been right.

Nothing in that yard was wrong with anything.

Nine men on the books where four would do. Eleven pieces to the tonne. A hedge that is not going. A sill line that pays by the ton and goes east on the twenty-second.

And a woman at the gate end of it with four fingers and a thumb and an arm open half of the time, being looked at, for the first time since she came out of the bound, as a body and not as a line.

## What She Wanted There

She wanted him to have made her ask twice.

That was the want, standing in that bay at the seventh hour on the second day with her hands in her sleeves and nine men eleven feet off, and it was the first time in six months she had wanted a wrong thing badly enough to be angry that it had not happened.

He had not made her ask twice. He had looked at her and worked out where she went and he had asked her the length and he had given her the hedge and he had asked her the sill once and he had taken her no before she had finished saying it.

Six months ago she had asked a man at a gate in daylight to put her in a book, and two offices had written her down, and one of them had copied it upward into a hand she cannot read, and the copy had gone east on a cart at a quarter past the sixth hour on the first of a month, and this man in a yard with a rule in his hand had worked out where she goes in the order by looking at her arm.

That was what a yard is. It is where a thing goes in an order, and the order is not announced, and there is no word in it for what she is.

## What She Took Away

She took the bar back to the fence at the eleventh hour and stood at the mouth of the yard on the made road and looked east for ten minutes.

The made road went on out of the corner of the yard with the cart ruts in it, and past the turn it was five more miles to the long road, and the strip that had gone out of the works yard on the first of a month was somewhere past that, eleven miles off, with a stone on it, in a hand she could not read.

She had come four miles. She was going back four miles. And there had been nine men in that bay and she had been the ninth and the smallest and the only one of the nine who was not on a board, and the man who ran it had found her place in it in two seconds with a rule in his hand.

On the way back she worked out the whole of what a yard was, standing on the made road, at about the twelfth hour, with nothing in her hands.

A yard prices a body. It does not price a column. And every office she had been entered in since the twenty-sixth of a month six months back had her column in it, and there was not one line of her anywhere on nine days of road that had her arm on it.

That was the first room she had walked into where she was the smallest thing in it, and it had taken ten minutes, and nothing in it had been unkind.
