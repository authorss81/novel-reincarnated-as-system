# The Line With One Name On It

The strip came down the mill road at the eighth hour of the eighteenth of a month and it did not come down on a first and it did not come down on a ninth, and the man behind the counter read it at the end of that counter and then stood there with it in his hand for a minute before he did anything else with it.

She was outside. There is a stone bench against the outside wall of that building, two feet off the ground and five feet long, and she was sitting on it with her strip in her left hand when she saw a carter put a folded thing down on the step and go, and she did not get up to look at it, because the counter man was on the step inside the door at four seconds.

She heard him read it. She heard him put it down flat on the counter and pick it up and put it down flat again.

She went in at the eighth hour and ten minutes past and the man behind the counter had it in his hand.

## What Was On It

“It is about the line I have had in that book for five months,” he said.

She knew what line he meant before he said a word about it, and she had not thought about that line once in six months.

“You have the strip,” he said. “That is yours and I have never read it and I am not going to.”

He turned it round so the writing was the right way up and put it flat on the counter and did not let go of it.

“There is one name under a rule on it. I have never seen the name. It came down in the middle of a month five months back with words at the bottom of it and I put it in with the book because the strip and that book are the same thing, and I have never been able to do one single thing with it since.”

“Yes.”

“And it has now been sent for.”

## The Two Lines

“There are two lines on it and they are in the ordinary hand and the ordinary hand is the hand that writes in that building, so it has come back down from above its own floor.”

He put his thumb under the second line.

“The first one says that the line at the bottom of this page has not been carried to the room it was made out for on the twenty-eighth of a month five months back, and that the room has stood empty on that date every month since, and that it is the fifth one it has stood empty on.”

“The second one says that the line is to be carried or it is to be closed, and that a line is not closed with a name in it, and that a holder is required against it.”

He took his thumb away.

“That is the whole of it and it has come down the mill road at the eighth hour of a day that is not the first and not the ninth, and this building has no idea what to do with it.”

## What He Did About It

He folded it once and put it inside his coat and looked up the stair.

“A holding is kept on the floor above me,” he said. “I have been behind this counter eleven years and I know where everything in it is kept and that is the one thing in it I have ever had to go and ask a person about.”

He came round the end of the counter.

“I am going up nine steps with this and I am going to have a woman read it to me, and you are going to come up if you want to, and you do not have to, and I am not going to ask you why.”

## The Nine Steps

She went up.

Rao was at the far end of the table with forty books open on it and she read the strip standing up, at the end, without sitting down, in eleven seconds, and she read it twice.

Then she came round the table to the near end and stopped four feet off it and did not sit down either.

“I have been on this floor for sixteen years,” she said. “I keep every set that comes into this building and I have had a holding twice, and a holding is a line with one name under a rule, and both of those times it was a place and not a person.”

She put the strip down on the table, face up, in the middle of it.

“And there is not a room on nine days of road I have ever been in that has been shut on me for want of a holder being carried into it, because I have never sent one and I have never been asked to send one, and I have never once in sixteen years had a page come down off the road with a word at the bottom of it from a floor above this one.”

She looked at Ning Yan.

“So I cannot tell you what that room is and I cannot tell you when it shut, and I have told two people in my life that I could not find out and both of them took it as me being difficult.”

## The Question

“Is there a person on this ground who is that holder.”

The woman of forty-five asked it in the ordinary voice at four feet, at ten minutes past the eighth hour, and she did not put any weight on it.

“No,” Ning Yan said. “There is one name on it and it is not mine and he is not on this ground. He has not been on this ground since before I came to it and he is not going to be on this ground.”

Rao did not look up from the strip.

“Then the line has nothing at the other end of the rule,” she said, “and it has had nothing at the other end of the rule since the seventeenth of a month five months back, and this strip does not change that and does not close it.”

“I know.”

“And I am going to write on it, in my own hand, at the top of it, that I have read it and that it has been answered in the ordinary voice at the end of this table, and I am going to write what you said and nothing else, because a man at a counter who writes down what a person says about a thing he cannot see is writing down somebody’s opinion of it.”

She picked the pencil up.

“I am not going to be thanked for that and you knew that when you asked me for four hours a month.”

## What Was Written On It

“Read on the eighteenth. The holder is not on this ground. There is one name on the line and it is not hers.”

That is nine words and a woman of forty-five wrote them at the top of a strip in her own hand and turned it round and gave it back to a man behind a counter, who folded it and put it inside his coat.

And the whole of what had happened at the top of that building in six minutes was that a question which had come down a road from above its own floor had been answered by two people who were both right, in the ordinary voice, and had gone back down the same road with two sentences on it, and neither sentence was a refusal.

## The Part She Did Not Say On The Stair

She went down the nine steps and eleven and out of the door and along the mill road for two hundred yards before she stopped, and then she stood at the rail on the wrong side of the building so that anyone coming down that road would not see her face.

She had not said one word in that room about the room.

A room where a thing is put so that it can be looked at afterwards. She had said it to herself at a gate and at a counter and at the top of a stair and it was true every time and it had never once done her one thing of good, because every room she has put anything in has been a room that somebody else reads.

And in that room at four feet, at ten minutes past the eighth hour on the eighteenth of a month, a woman of forty-five had asked her one plain question and had taken the answer and written nine words on a strip and had not asked her for a single thing back.

That is the difference between a counter and a floor, and she worked it out at the rail in about forty seconds, and it is the only good thing that happened to her in eleven days, and she could not put it in a book.

## What She Did With The Rest Of The Day

She dug the run at the gate at about the ninth hour because the nineteenth was a gate day and the copper does not care what is in a strip.

The strip went back up the mill road on the nineteenth with the ordinary post down to the yard at the turn, and out to the yard with the next bundle.

The yard book came back down inside the tray on the ninth of the next month. The man with the rule in his hand would read it at his office end and it would be his problem and he would be right about it and he would not have a term for it.

That was a week away and it was Friday.

She got a copper and filled in the four feet at the near end and did three and a half the rest of it and came out at the eleventh hour with five coppers in the fold and bread for four days.

## The Second Hour Of The Nineteenth

The ring came up the width of a finger in the shed and stopped, and she lay on the boards at the second hour with her arm out and said it out loud, because there was not a living soul in the room.

“A holder’s answer is paid out of the foundation of the person it is about.”

She said it twice, because it is a thing she wanted to hear twice.

“That is the price. That has been the price since the twenty-second of a month four months back when a fire came up in a shed that had no man in it, and it is not out of my hands and it is not out of my column and it is out of the ground I am standing on.”

“And there is one name on a strip in a coat two miles down this road and it is not on this ground and it cannot be produced on this ground, and the only thing that can be done about that is for him to say something.”

She lay there.

“He will not do it. I know that the way I know my own hands. He has been asked once already, in a room, in the ordinary voice, and he said no, and the reason he gave was better than any reason I could give.”

“And if he does it, I will not know. That is the price and I am not going to be able to ask anybody about it, and there is no answer to that from anywhere inside nine days of road.”

She put her hand flat on the boards.

“And I am going to have to go on getting up the mill road four times a month and reading columns out loud in a cold room either way.”
