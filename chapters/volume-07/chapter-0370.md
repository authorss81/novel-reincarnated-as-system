# The Last Day It Lit Itself

The hedge was closed off the board on the twentieth of a month and she had walked four miles to find that out and it had taken about nine words, and she did not think about it again for a week.

Then, on the first of a month at about the second hour, with the ring burning nine feet away against the brick in a shed at a mill whose wheel had not turned since before she came, something settled.

## The Second Hour, The First

She was lying on the boards with her hands behind her head when it came, and it was not a sound and it was not a thing she had to be told about, and it took about a minute and a half and it felt like a debt coming in out of the weather.

A piece of listed work on a board four miles east of here, past the mill road, closed and checked and off.

Not a death. She went back along it once and there was no death anywhere in it, and there has not been one in her accounting since the crossing.

“A hedge,” she said. “At the east end. Four hundred yards, by the length, one day, closed and checked on the twentieth. Done with my hands and not on a book and not for money.”

He took his time.

“Four hundred yards is a day and a half,” he said. “Done by you and not on a book is worth more than four hundred yards, because a book is a thing that gets read and yours did not get read. That is a better figure than the length and it is the reason the length does not settle anything most of the time.”

“What is it.”

He did not pretend not to know.

“Six days and four hours,” he said. “That is what a listed thing you did yourself is worth past the mill, and I have not said that number out loud in four years and I am saying it to you because you are going to spend it before you have had a night with it, and I would rather you spent it having heard it.”

She lay there.

“Say it again.”

“Six days and four hours,” he said. “And it goes out of the ground under you and not out of your hands, and it does not come back, and there is no name for what it takes off you and there is no instrument on nine days of road that could tell me what it took, and I have watched it go out of about nine people and I have never once been able to say what it was.”

She said it out loud again to make sure she had it in the right shape.

“Six days and four hours.”

“That is the whole of what you get told before it is spent,” he said. “I am not going to tell you it is a good price. I am telling you what it is, and I am telling you before you have decided, because that is the arrangement and the arrangement is the only thing either of us has.”

## What She Spent It On

Nothing.

That was the whole of the plan and she had made it on the boards at about the third hour and it had taken about four minutes to make. She was not going to spend it on a hold and she was not going to spend it on a bar and she was not going to spend it on her hours and she was not going to spend it on the reach.

She was going to let six days and four hours go out from under her into the floor of a shed at a mill, the way it has always gone, and let the floor have it, and no office on nine days of road was ever going to see a line about it.

She put her palm flat on the flat stone by the door and said it out loud at about the fourth hour with the light still in, and then she put her hand on the ring.

She put her hand on the ring to say goodnight to it, because it had been coming up by itself at about the second hour every morning for four months and she had never once had to do anything about it, and she wanted to touch it one last time on the morning it came up on its own.

It came up warm against her palm.

## The Second Of A Month

The ring had come up at the second hour that morning on its own, the way it had come up at the second hour every morning for four months, and she had put her hand flat on it at about the fourth hour and said goodnight to it, and it had been warm under her palm.

The yard had a bank at about a mile and a quarter east of the mill, out on the flat past the last hedge, and it was a job of taking a face off and putting it back, and it was measured by the length because everything a bank is measured by is length.

Nine of them were on it at about the sixth hour on the second of a month, because a bank is a job that wants as many hands on the face as can stand on it, and the man with the rule in his hand had put nine men on a bank that four would do and had said out loud that four would do, and put nine on it anyway, because a bank that goes wrong costs more than a day’s wages.

She was there at about the eighth hour because she had cut a length of ditch at the west end and got a fifth of a reach and one copper and the ditch was finished at about the eleventh hour on the first and there was a bank in front of her that wanted hands.

The face was about four feet and the material was clay under stone and the bar came out of the face flat and heavy and about the size of two flat loaves, and she was on the high side of it working the loose at the top down to where the others were working, with nine men about eleven feet off along a face about sixty feet long.

## The Load

It was not a dressed sill. That was the whole of what she understood about it afterward.

It was four pieces of the heavy stone at the top of that bank that had come off together with the clay under them because the face above a gully goes first and the face above a gully had been going for about nine years, and a bar had gone under the top of them, and the bar was loaded, and it was on the low side of a bank with nine men on it and a woman eleven feet along the face.

The bar went.

It did not slip and it did not roll. It came out of the face and came down the bank on its side with four pieces on it and about a ton of it, and it came down the low side of a four-foot bank, and she was on the low side of that bank because she had come round the end of the face to fill a barrow with the loose.

## What Was Under It

She went down on her back and the bar came down across her and the four pieces of heavy stone came off it as it went, and the first one went past her shoulder and the second one stopped.

She had a bar across her chest and a stone across her legs and about four inches of air under her face and the side of her head going into the bank.

And her right arm was open, because it was open half the time, and it had been for four years.

Nine men came off the face. The nearest one was eleven feet away at the top and he was coming down the bank at a dead run, which is the correct thing and is what a bank is for, and a bank is about four feet and he would be at her in about two seconds and he was too far off.

## The Furnace

She did not think about it and there was no time in it and there was nothing in her that put it there.

She lifted.

That is the whole of it. There is no other word for it in four years and she had learned it at a bank nine days west from a woman with a scar across the back of both hands, and it was three words long.

What it is, is that the thing comes up out of the ground she is standing on and it goes under what is on top of her, and it is not her arms and it is not her hands and it is not anything that can be looked at.

She felt it go out of her in about half of a second. She felt it go out of the place under her ribs where it had been sitting since the twenty-second of a month about four months back, and she felt it come up under the bar and the stone on her legs the way a hand comes up under a thing, and the bar stopped.

It stopped about a finger’s width.

The man was four feet off and coming. He got his hands on the end of the bar and put his shoulder under it and it came up the rest of the way in about two seconds, which is what two strong men and a bar do in two seconds, and the stone came off it and went down the far side of the bank, and the bar went after it.

She lay on her back on the grass with the bar off her and about nine inches of air under her shoulders and the bank four feet above her and nine men standing in a line looking down at her, and she was whole.

## What Went Out Of Her

The ring was nine feet away in the corner of a shed at a mill that was a mile and a quarter west.

She found that out about half an hour later, in the yard at the east end of the bank, with nine men standing about nine feet off and one of them holding a chain.

She found it out the way she finds out everything, which was by putting her hands out flat in front of her and looking at them.

They were cold.

They had been warm for four months — warm the way a furnace is warm, the way her hands are warm the way nothing else on nine days of road is warm — and at about the third hour on the second of a month she had put a bar’s worth of four pieces of heavy stone up off her own chest with what was under her ribs, and her hands had gone cold almost the moment it stopped.

She stood in a yard with nine men in it and looked at four fingers and a thumb and the palm of the other one and they were the hands of an eighteen-year-old woman standing on flat ground in the middle of the morning.

The man with the rule in his hand came down the bank and stood nine feet off her and did not say one word about what had happened, because he had been running a yard for nineteen years and he had seen a woman go down under a bar and get up, and the thing a yard does about that is to check the ground and write the day down.

“You were on the loose side,” he said. “That is where a person is not supposed to be at the third hour.”

“I know.”

He looked at her arm, which was open.

“Come back tomorrow,” he said, “and stand on the high side, and do not stand on the low side of that face again.”

She said that she would.

And then she walked a mile and a quarter west with the sun out and no bar, and she went into a shed at a mill whose wheel had not turned since before she came, and the ring in the corner was black.
