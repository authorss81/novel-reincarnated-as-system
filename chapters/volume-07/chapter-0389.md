# Fifteen Coppers And No Bread

Ten coppers in the fold in the inside of the door frame at the end of the month before this one, and eleven on its first.

Thirty-six days at the gate after that, at a copper a day, which is thirty-six coppers, and four trips west to the store at eight, which is thirty-two.

Fifteen coppers on the seventh of a month, and no bread in the shed since the fifth, and a store five miles west she will walk to in the morning.

She did that sum twice on the boards with the flat of her second finger and got the same fifteen both times. It is the first month since she came past this mill that she has finished with more than she started with, and the whole of the difference is five coppers, and she knows exactly where the five coppers came from.

## What A Week Was

A copper a day at the gate, at three and a half feet at the low end and four feet for six feet at the near end, with a bar that goes in where the ground is hard, and about two and a quarter hours of standing for it.

Three miles east to the works every second day, to a board that is still full and still eleven lines and now has a ditch line gone off it with a rubbed patch in the board where it was.

A length at the yard at the turn three days in seven, and a cup of something hot out of an office end, and the man with the rule in his hand has not said a word to her since the twenty-second of a month.

Five miles west every eighth day.

And four times in that month, at the fourth hour, nine steps up a stair with a turn in the landing behind her.

## The Sixth Of A Month

The boy of about sixteen brought the bundle up the nine steps on the sixth at the fifth hour and put it on the middle of the table and said good morning to her on the way down.

He had not said good morning to her on any of the six times he had brought that bundle up before.

He was sixteen and he had been doing it for about a year and he had never asked her anything, and he did not ask her anything that evening, and going down nine steps he said good morning to a woman sitting at the end of a table he had never seen before.

That is a thing a boy does when a woman has been in a room four times.

She sat at the edge of the table from the fourth hour until about the sixth and read eleven columns out loud in the ordinary voice because a woman of forty-five asked her to, and her hands were warm the whole of the time.

At about half past the fifth the woman put her pencil down flat on the page and did not pick it up again for about twenty seconds.

“The wood is warm,” she said.

“No.”

“It is warm under your hand,” Rao said, “and it has been cold at this end of this table every day since before I came on it. I am forty-five and I have stood at this end of this table every day of sixteen years, and there has never been a woman at it before.”

She picked the pencil up.

“I am not going to talk about it,” she said, “and you are not going to talk about it, and there is a stove at the north end and I am going to put it in a quarter of an hour.”

She did not talk about it.

## The Seventh Of A Month

She walked five miles west at the sixth hour on the sixth of a month and stood in a shop with fifteen coppers in the fold and looked at four loaves at two coppers and then walked out of it and five miles back, and she was in the shed at the ninth hour with a lump out of the box going in at the low end of the ring.

She has fifteen coppers in the fold in the inside of the door frame and no bread in the shed. She had ten of them on the thirtieth of the month before and stood in that shop and bought nothing at all, which she has done twice in seven months. She is going back in the morning because on the eighth of a month there is a bundle to go up nine steps and round a turn in a landing, and there is a woman at the far end of a table who reads all day and does not eat at the hour she reads.

It came up the width of a finger and stopped there.

She did it six times in about an hour and it came up a finger’s width and stopped every one of the six, and the shed was cold at the back and cold at the door, and her hands were cold, and she put her palm flat on the flat stone by the door and it was the same temperature as the stone.

Forty days.

## The Plain Part About One Person

She is eighteen years old and her hands are warm in one room on nine days of road and cold in every other room she has stood in, and she worked that out in about ten days and she has not worked it out again since.

She is at the third floor about four times a month. On the sixth she was warm from the fourth hour to the sixth. On the road down, at two hundred yards past the counting house, they went cold, and she stood there for a minute working out that they went cold at the same place every time, which is a hundred yards short of the gate at a post she walks past four times a month and has not once spoken to.

And she has had two fires come up since the second of a month: one in her own shed at the third hour, and she used it to hold a bar off herself with four pieces of heavy stone on it and nine men on a bank above her, and one on the floor of a low building at the sixth hour on the first of a month with a man’s hand flat on the floor of it. She has not had one since the first.

What she wanted, for eleven days in the middle of that month, was to know whether a day and a half of cold hands on the twenty-fifth of a month was the price of a ditch or the beginning of something, and she could not have asked anybody on nine days of road, and it was neither. It was one afternoon.

## What Is Coming Down The Mill Road

She worked it out at the boards at the second hour on the seventh of a month, which is where she has worked every sum out since she was eleven years old.

On the first of a month at the sixth hour, everything that has come into that building in thirty days goes up the mill road with a cord round the tray. It comes down again on the ninth and gets copied out by a man with a brush into a book on the floor below, and what he writes goes down a cart at the end of the month and gets read against what came down the same month.

And in one of those books there is now a line that was not there on the last first of a month.

She wrote it out on the boards with the flat of her second finger and got the same answer both times.

Her name at the left of it, in the ordinary hand, in a hand a man with a brush can read.

He has never been able to read the fine hand. He has been eleven years on that floor and he has looked, and he has told her so himself, and nobody in that building can read it.

He can read this one. That is the difference between the two, and it is the whole of what she has done in about ten days, and she has not put a single word in the fine hand about herself and she is not going to.

On the ninth of a month, two days from now, a man with a brush is going to copy a stair book into another book on nine days of road, and there is going to be a name in it that was not in any book inside nine days of road about ten days ago, and it is going in a hand she cannot read and she cannot pay anybody to read it back to her.

You put it there yourself. There is nothing on nine days of road that can take it out and there was never going to be anything on nine days of road that could take it out.

## What Is Different And What Is Not

The board at the works still has eleven lines on it and still has no line on it she can take, and every one of the eleven is dressed stone by the ton, and she reads it from the bottom up every second day and she is not going to stop.

The gate still pays a copper a day and Pei still has one thing he is allowed to say and has said it. The drain is still four feet at the near end and three and a half on the low side and it still comes up easy and it is not going to be four feet all the way, because he told her twice.

The yard at the turn still has nine men on a board where four will do and still has no name of hers on anything in it, and there is a rubbed patch in the works board where a ditch line used to be, and nobody on nine days of road knows she was in that cut for two days.

The strip she has carried in her coat since the twenty-fifth of a month four months back is still folded in two and she still cannot read the word at the head of it and she still takes it up nine steps four times a month and puts it on the table.

And the woman in the office end at the yard has not asked her name once in two months and has put a cup of something hot into her hands six times in the last three weeks.

That is the whole of what is different. One woman knows her name.

One stair book on the top floor of one building has her name in it at the left of the twenty-eighth of a month, and a man with a brush is going to copy it out on the ninth. And she is going to stand in a gateway at a post a hundred yards past the counting house one morning in that week, and watch the cart go up the mill road past the elms.

She cannot un-ask it. She worked that out on the seventh at the second hour and she wrote it on the boards and got the same answer twice, and it is the first thing she has ever put into a room that she cannot get out of again, and she got it by asking for it out loud in the ordinary voice, and it is exactly what she wanted and it is the worst thing that has happened to her in seven months.

## The Last Of It

She lay on the boards and put her hands behind her head, and they were cold, and the ring was cold, and she said out loud at the second hour because there was not a living soul in the room.

“I have got four hours a month of a woman of forty-five in a cold room and I cannot pay for it and I did not have to have her name read to me to get it.”

“And she has got a book on a table with a name in it that goes up a road on the first of a month, and she did not ask me for anything, and she put her pencil down and said the wood was warm and then she did not talk about it.”

That was the whole of what she had.

And the thing she was carrying down the mill road with her, that she could not put down and did not want to, was that the first thing she had ever asked anybody for was eight hours a month of another person standing near her, and she had got it, and she was eighteen, and there was nothing on nine days of road that she could give back for it.
