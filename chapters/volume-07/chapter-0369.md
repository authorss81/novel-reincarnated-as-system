# Four People Agree Later

She went east four miles on the twentieth of a month at the sixth hour to find out whether a hedge line got closed on a board when it was done, because a board on that ground was the only thing she had ever been entered in and she wanted to know whether it keeps the thing you have done or only the thing you have not done yet.

It is a short question. It took four miles of made road and a whole morning to ask, and she had been carrying it since the second of a month when nine words had come back to her in place of an answer, and she had not told a person where she was going and had not been asked.

The board was on the wall of the bay with the bench against it and it had eight lines on it.

Nine had been on it on the fourteenth.

## Nine And Eight

He had the rule in his hand and he was standing in front of it with his back to her when she came in at the end of the track, and he did not turn round.

“It is closed,” he said.

“What is closed.”

“The hedge.” He put the rule against the bench and came about. “It is done and it is checked and it is off. That is what off means. It does not mean the money is paid, because there is no money in it for anybody on this board. It means there is not a thing left on it for anybody to do.”

“That is all I wanted to know.”

“You walked four miles to find that out.”

“Yes,” she said.

He looked at her for a second, and then he said, “It is a fair question and there is not a good answer for it and I am not going to make one up,” and went back to the board.

## What The Yard Was Doing

The sill line was half down and four men were on it, and the dressed stone was stacked against the south wall nine feet off with about four pieces out of it, and it was going east on the twenty-second.

Four pieces out of it, counted twice from the end of the bay, out of the nine that had been standing in that stack on the first morning.

That is how a yard measures a month as well.

She stood at the end of the bay and looked at it for ten minutes and nine men in that yard took no notice of her at all.

That was different from the last visit and she had to work out why before she left.

The first time she had come in at the sixth hour off a made road with her hands in her sleeves, and nine men had looked up. This time she came in at the sixth hour and got to the end of the bay and nine men did not look up, because nine men who have seen a person once do not look up the second time.

She was not being ignored. She was being put where she went.

## The Second Time She Stood There

She stayed until about the ninth hour and then walked the four miles back, and about halfway she worked out the thing that had happened to her on that ground and it was about nine words long.

She had been priced.

Not by the man with the rule. He had asked her for a length and given her a hedge and asked her for a sill once. That was a yard doing what a yard does. What had happened was that in two seconds on the first morning, with a rule in his hand, he had put her in an order, and every day since then nine men had been behaving as though the order were settled, and none of them had been told anything by anybody.

The counter had her column. The copying floor had her column. The board at the works had a ruled space at the left of it with her name in it and nothing running into it.

The yard had her arm. And a yard is where a body goes in an order, and that ground had no word in it at all for what her arm was worth, and it had not needed one.

## Nine Feet Off The Cart Track

She was on the cart track two hundred yards west of the works at the seventh hour on the twenty-second with the strip in her coat and nothing else in her hands, and he was at nine feet off it with the notebook shut under his arm and no other paper of any kind, and he had been in the same place every day for six months.

The grass either side of him was worn down in two places the length of a boot, which meant he stood in those two spots and had for six months, and there was nothing else in that grass worn at all.

She had worked that out in the first month she had known him was there and had never once walked over and checked it.

She had not asked him a question since the thirteenth of a month about three months back on a track in wet grass. She had asked one that time and it had been about a room with a date on it, and he had told her there were four rooms on the ground he had been in and two of them shut on the last day of a month.

She stopped at eleven feet.

## The Question

“What is a room on this ground for.”

She said it in the ordinary voice, in daylight, on a made cart track with the works three hundred yards behind her and nothing in the world in front of her but nine feet of dry grass and a man who had been standing in it for six months.

It was a question with no office in it and no date on it and no book fastened to it, which was why she had walked two hundred yards up a made road to put it to a man standing in grass rather than take it to a counter.

He did not take the notebook out from under his arm.

“A room is where a thing is put so that four people can agree about it later,” he said.

That was the whole of it.

She waited for the rest of it, because a man who gives a person four sentences and stops has given her four sentences and nothing else, and she had learned that about him four months back.

He did not add anything. He shifted the notebook under his arm and looked at the road and then back at her, and he did not ask her a question, and he did not tell her anything about the strip against her ribs or the book on a stone under a ladder nine miles west or the piece of cloth that had gone east on a cart at a quarter past the sixth hour on the first of a month.

## What She Had Asked It For

She had asked it because she had been in a building on the mill road at the bottom of a stair four times in six months and she had never once been upstairs, and she had said no in a room with a stove in it on the twenty-sixth of a month six months back in front of about nine men, and the room had written it into the only book on that road that shuts.

She had thought the room was where a person was brought to.

A room is where a thing is put so that four people can agree about it later.

Her name was the thing. It had been the thing since the twenty-sixth of a month six months back when a man at a bench in a gateway wrote it into a book with a ruled line and a cut across the bottom of it. And four people had agreed about it, out loud, in a room, and she had been standing in the room while they did it.

She had been the room. That was what she had been refusing and she had not known it was a thing she could be.

## What He Said After

He turned and started walking the nine feet back to his own side of the track and then stopped, because she had not moved and he was not going to be the one to come inside nine feet.

“I am still here,” he said, “and I am still buying nothing, and I am not going to tell you why, and you are not going to ask me.”

She had not been going to.

She stood there in the dry grass and let that be the end of it, and then she said thank you, which was a thing she had said to him once in six months, and she walked the two hundred yards back to the gate and opened a fifth of a reach and got one copper out of the fold in the inside of the door frame.

## What She Took East

That night she sat on the boards of the shed with the ring burning nine feet off and worked out the whole of what six months on that ground had actually been, standing up, the way she worked everything out.

The counter had read her. The copying floor had copied her out. The board had carried her name in a ruled space with nothing running into it. The mill road had taken a book up four times and sent it down once. The post had it on a stone under a ladder. The made road east had taken a strip off her at a quarter past the sixth hour and gone nine miles with it.

And every one of those was a room where a thing got put so that four people could agree about it later.

She had spent six months refusing to be brought to one.

She lay down on the boards with her hands behind her head and looked at nine feet of fire and understood, for the first time in six months, that she had been the thing in every room she had walked into and had been getting it wrong.

## At The Second Hour

“You have been working it out since the twenty-second.”

“Since the fourteenth,” she said.

He did not say anything for a while. The ring was about nine feet high against the brick and it had come up on its own at the second hour and it had not needed her at any hour of that day or any day since the twenty-second of a month four months back.

“A thing that gets put in a room is a thing four people agree about later,” he said. “That is what he said. He was not talking about you and he was talking about you and both of those are what he said.”

“It does not say anything about where I go.”

“No,” he said. “It does not.”

She lay there in the dark and that was the whole of the arrangement, and it was the arrangement it had been for four years, and neither of them had broken it, and the fire did not need her.
