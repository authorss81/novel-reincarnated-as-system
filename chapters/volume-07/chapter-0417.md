# A Denial Is An Entry

On the eighteenth of a month beginning 1,034, at the ninth hour of the night, a sheet came down the mill road in a hand that comes about nine times a year, and it had a woman’s name at the head of it and a date on it inside a week, and it went up nine steps and past the third floor and down one flight, and stopped in a room at the end of the floor above the counter.

A man of forty-three read it twice and put it in the groove with the other two and did not sleep.

She did not know any of that. She was four miles east at the ninth hour of the night at a yard where nine men work on four men’s work and she had got two dates wrong in front of all of them and had told them so, and she lay on the boards at the second hour and said the sentences out loud and put a lump of clay in at the low end of the ring and it did not come up at all.

## The Nineteenth

The man behind the counter came out of his door at the eighth hour and she was at the gate of that building with the bar over her right shoulder because she was on her way to a drain run.

He said five words.

“There is a thing on the second floor.”

“All right.”

“It is in the hand.”

She stood in the gateway a hundred yards short of that building with the bar over her right shoulder and four fingers and a thumb on it and she worked out four things at once, standing up, in nine seconds, and the fourth of them was the only one that mattered.

There is exactly one hand on nine days of road that a person cannot read, and she had the rate of it inside nine seconds: eight of the nine that come down that road in a year are lists of places and dates.

And one of them had her name at the head of it.

## The Room With The Lamp

He did not look up.

“Sit down,” he said. “I have got it out of the groove and I have read it four times and I am going to read it once more with you in the room and then I am going to say something to you, and I would rather say it with you here than have you come back up those stairs tomorrow.”

She did not sit down.

He turned the sheet round.

“I am going to say four things and then you are going to say something, and I am going to let you say it whatever it is.”

“All right.”

“First. It is the fine hand. There is no part of it in my hand and there is no part of it in anybody’s hand in this building.”

“Second. It came down on the mill road last night and it is dated the eighteenth, which is yesterday, and a woman at a gate is named at the head of it.”

“Third. It is one line and it is short and it has been in my room for nine hours and I have not said one word of it out loud.”

## The Offer

He put both hands flat on the slope.

“I am going to read it to you,” he said. “Once. In this room. And then you will tell me whether it is right or whether it is wrong, and that is how a reading is finished.”

The lamp was on and there was no window in that room and it was the seventh hour of the nineteenth of a month.

“Once,” she said.

“Once. I have never read one of these to anybody in twenty-six years and I am not doing it because I have gone soft. I am doing it because you are standing in this room and a reading about a person who is not standing in this room is the one thing my trade does not allow, and I have had two in twenty-six years and both of them were about a man who had gone nine days of road before I got to him.”

“What happens if I say no.”

## What He Answered

He did not move his hands.

“Then I write that you will not hear it,” he said, “and it goes up, and it is finished.”

“That is not what I asked.”

“I know what you asked.” He looked at her properly for the first time since she came into the room. “You asked whether a thing can sit in this building and never be answered at all. It cannot. A reading does not sit here. It goes up and down eleven hundred years of roads and it comes down and it has to be answered by the person it is about, and there are two answers and one of them is no.”

“You are telling me that if I say no, I have answered it.”

“I am telling you that a person who will not hear a thing they have been offered in a room is the only person in eleven hundred years who has ever answered one without hearing it,” he said, “and that it is a real answer and it goes up with the others and it comes back down if anybody ever asks for it again.”

He put his hand flat on the sheet.

“And nobody on nine days of road will ever be able to tell you what you said no to.”

## What She Said Then

She stood at the end of that table with her left hand on her coat where the strip was and she asked four questions and they were the four she had been carrying since the eighth of a month four months back, and she asked them in the ordinary voice.

“If I hear it and I confirm it, where does the confirmation go.”

“Into the column, in the ordinary hand, in the ordinary voice, at this table, the way every answer to every reading has gone since before this building was a building.”

“If I hear it and I deny it.”

“Same place, and it says denied, and that is a stronger thing than a yes because a denial is a person standing in front of a thing and saying it is wrong.”

“And if I say no to you now.”

“Nothing is written in this room today,” he said, “and that is the part I want to be exact about, because I have known nineteen years that I will say it. Nothing is written. No book in this building will not shut. Nothing fails.”

He picked the straight edge up and put it in the groove.

“Your no goes up because it is an answer, and it goes up the same road every answer has gone up since before the wheel on that mill was cast, and that is all that happens.”

## The No

She said it in four seconds and she did not sit down first and she did not put her hands anywhere.

“No,” she said. “I will not hear it.”

Dai wrote nothing.

He put the sheet in the groove with the other two, and squared it, and put his hands flat on the slope on either side of it, and turned round and looked at the lamp for about two seconds.

“All right,” he said.

And then he said, in the ordinary voice, and he was not being kind and he was not being unkind:

“I have been at this for twenty-six years and I have had that offered to me eleven hundred times and I have never heard of a person take it, and I am not going to stand here and tell you that you are the first, because you are not the first either, you are just the first one I have had in this room.”

He picked up his pen and went back to the top of a sheet that was not that one.

“You can go down the stair.”

## The Plain Part About One Person

She is eighteen years old and she has carried a word she cannot read against her ribs since the seventeenth of a month five months back, and she has wanted to have it read for every day since, and there was a man two miles up a made road with a lamp in his room whose trade is that hand and who would have read it in four seconds.

And she has given it up. Not for a season, not for a price, and not for anything that anybody could have taken off her.

Forever. On purpose. In a room, in daylight, to the only person on nine days of road who could ever have done it, with no force and nothing owed and a condition that was the ordinary condition.

## Why

She went down the stair and out of the door and a hundred yards down the made road with the mill on the left of it and she stood in the middle of it and worked it out four times and got the same answer four times.

Confirming is entering.

A man reads a thing out loud in a room and a person says yes or no to it, and that is a person making a statement on the record about a thing they cannot check, in a hand they cannot read, from a room they cannot get into, and it goes up a road.

She has been entered three times. As good, in a book nine days of road west she has never opened. For materials, in Volume 6. And on the twenty-second of a month two months back, on a step, in daylight, in one word, to a question about whether she was at a gate.

And she was asked whether she would do it again and the answer she gave every time was no, and she had been carrying that no for seven months as the cleanest thing she had.

She was eighteen years old in a made road with a mill on the left of it and she found out on the nineteenth of a month that she had now made herself a person who can be answered about a thing she has never read, and that she had done it to keep the other three from being four.

## What It Cost In The Room

Nothing.

That is the whole of what it cost in the room. Nothing was written, the lamp was on, and a man of forty-three went back to the top of a sheet that was not that one, and she had had no reach at all since the eleventh of a month, and eleven men were downstairs in a room with a stove in it, and not a thing she had done in that room had happened anywhere in that building.

The bill is hers.

## The Second Hour

She lay on the boards at the second hour of the twentieth with the strip against her ribs and the bar under her right hand, and she put a lump out of the box in at the low end of the ring.

It did not come up.

It had come up the width of a finger every night since the eighth of a month four months back, and it had not come up at all since the eleventh, and she had not tried it once in nine days because there was nothing in that shed she could put a hand on and have answer her, and she was not going to buy a bar to find out.

So she lay there with her hands behind her head and said the six words out loud once, into a shed with boards on the floor and no fire in it, and then she said them a second time because she wanted to hear whether they sounded any different the second time.

They did not.

And a hundred yards down a made road there was a sheet in a groove in a room with a lamp in it, and it would go up the mill road on the first of a month in a bundle with a cord round the tray, and a name was at the head of it, and a man with a brush on the floor below would copy it into a book that shuts.

She had paid six words for it, and the six words had cost her everything she had wanted and nothing anybody could take off her, and she had said so out loud in a room at the seventh hour with the door shut and no force anywhere in the world in it.
