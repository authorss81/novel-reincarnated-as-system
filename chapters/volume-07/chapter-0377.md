# Loaded And Going East

The sill line went east on the twenty-second of a month at the sixth hour, and she was standing in the gateway of the yard at the turn when it did.

Eleven pieces to the tonne and two hundred and something of them, and they went on a flat cart with four horses and a chain over the bed, and it came out of the south end of that yard and went east up the made road and did not stop.

She had never been east of the turn.

She had stood at that yard three times, and had eaten a cup of something hot out of an office end, and had had a barrow-load of clay refused, and had watched a cart with a chain on it go east out of the works yard on the first of a month.

She had never once been on this side of the turn in her life, and she found out standing in that gateway that she had been imagining a wall.

## The Yard Before The Cart

The man with the rule in his hand was standing at the bench with his back to the loading and eight inches of the rule out of his pocket, and he was not looking at the cart and he was not looking at the horse.

“Two hundred and something of dressed sill going four miles east,” he said. “That is the sill line and it has gone every twenty-second for eleven years and it goes on a flat cart with four horses because dressed stone is not a thing a man carries a quarter of a mile.”

He put the rule back against his leg.

“There is a ditch line come up off the works board for the end of the month,” he said. “By the length, at the north end, and it is two weeks of work and I have four men on it and it has got to be off before the ground goes. I have not put you on it and I am not going to put you on it.”

“I did not say anything.”

“You have been standing in that gateway for two months reading a board,” he said. “And then a man looks at your arm. And then the man finds he has two weeks of ditch and four men and a woman who cuts by the length and does not want a cup of anything.”

He went to the cart and put two fingers on one of the pieces at the near end and did not lift it.

“The cart goes four miles,” he said, “and it comes back, and there is nothing past four miles but road.”

That was the last of it, and he was at the cart end and the horse was turning before she had decided whether to walk out there.

## Following It

She went with it.

Not alongside. Thirty feet back and off to the side, on the verge, the way a person walks who is not going anywhere in particular and has decided not to look like it.

The made road was good. Two ruts and a grass strip, going out flat with a slow rise on the north side, and the cart was four hundred yards ahead and getting slower, and she walked it for about a mile before she stopped and undid it.

There was a hedge on each side for the first half mile and then a field with a drain in it and a stack of dressed stone at the far end under a lean-to with a tarpaulin over half of it, and that was a yard.

That was four miles. The cart came in at the seventh hour and a man came out of an office end and started putting pieces off it with two others, and she stood about three hundred yards off with a bar over her shoulder and watched for ten minutes.

He will deliver it there. That is what goes east from that yard.

## What Was Past That

She went on.

There was a mile and a half past that yard where the made road goes on, and there was nothing on either side of it but a low bank and grass and two gates, and she walked it at the pace she walks everything, which is the pace of a gate and not the pace of a person with a bar.

The bar was on her shoulder because she had picked it up off the fence at the yard on the way out. A woman walking a road with nothing in her hands looks like a woman who has come apart, and she had worked that out at fourteen.

At about a mile and a half the road came up over a rise and she could see it going on for another mile, and then a long low line about four miles further on that was not trees. It was a bank with something set along the top of it, the length of about a mile, and it did not have a gate in it that she could see from where she was.

She stood on the rise and looked at it for ten minutes.

Nothing came. Not a sound off it at all, which she noticed, because a road with nine men on it four miles behind her is not silent, and this was not silent either. It was a road with a bar and a cart and no men on it.

Then she sat down on the rise with her knees up, because she had walked a good way with no bread in her since the twentieth and the rise was the first thing in nine miles she had wanted to sit on.

## The Plain Part About One Person

She got as far as the line on the bank four miles off. It is the same line she watched a cart go towards on the first of a month. She has never been nearer to it than that, and nothing about it is different at four miles than it was at nine.

What she learned in nine miles of walking out and five and a half of walking back is that a woman standing on a rise at seven in the morning with a bar over her shoulder is a shape on a flat. Somebody on this ground could have looked up. Nothing did, and she was the only thing on nine miles of road that morning that was walking toward anything.

You went out past the turn and nothing came of it and you are standing at the same gate with the same drain in it.

## Nine And A Half Miles Back

She counted the breath the whole way and it was four the whole way, which it always is unless there is something in her arms that weighs.

There was a bar on her shoulder, and a bar weighs what a bar weighs.

The bar comes off the shoulder and goes into the left hand for the last three miles, because a shoulder takes it for an hour and a half and then it starts to be a thing she has to think about, and she had been doing that since she was thirteen.

At four miles she came back into the yard with the bar in her left hand and her right arm open and a cut on the outside of her forearm about four inches long where a thorn had got her on the way out, and she had not felt it at the time and found it at the fourth mile.

She filled the cut in the horse trough. There is a trough at every yard on that road, and no person has ever said a word about a woman washing a cut in one.

The woman in the office end came out with a cup of something hot in it and she did not give it to her, and Ning Yan watched her carry it about nine feet and put it down on the bench at the mouth of the bay where nine men could get at it, and then go back inside.

She took one, later, when the bay was empty.

Then four miles west, and the gate was shut behind her and the shed was dark when she got to it, and she put one lump in the ring out of the box and it came up a finger’s width and stopped, and she sat down on the boards about four feet off it and found that she had been away from that corner for about eleven hours and had come back to exactly the same fire.

## The Twenty-Fourth, The Twenty-Sixth

The board was turned round on the twenty-fourth and she read all eleven lines from the bottom up and did not read one of them twice.

The man who kept the list read it out loud at the ninth hour for four men off a cart from the west, standing at the bench with his finger going down the lines, and she was nine feet off in the gateway and one of the four was a boy of about seventeen who repeated the weights back at him from memory.

Two days earlier that boy would have had four slips in his hand. Now he had nothing at all in his hands and a set of figures in his head, and a man with a rule in his hand four miles back had taken two hundred and something of dressed sill off the front of his board because a man in a gateway read it faster than four pieces of paper travel.

She had watched a reading make a crowd twice in two months and had not understood either time what it was for.

She took a length at the yard on the twenty-sixth, four hundred yards of dead hedge at the east end, and got the foot of it up right the first time. The boy with the stick carried barrows with her and did not talk, and there were nine men in that bay on the second morning and every one of them was looking at something else.

And she was in the shed at the eleventh hour on the twenty-sixth with no bread in it and six coppers in the fold, because eight coppers went west on the twentieth and she was not going to walk five miles twice until she had the eight again, which was the twenty-eighth, and she was not hungry and had not been for seven months.

She put her palm flat on the flat stone by the door. It was cold and her hand was cold, and the two of them were the same temperature, and it told her nothing, and she had stopped expecting it to tell her anything about eleven days ago.

Then she took the flat of her second finger and put it on the boards and wrote out the month in front of her, the way she had been writing months out on those boards since she was eleven.

Seven coppers in a week. Eight out. And on the first of a month there is a cart at the sixth hour with a cord round a tray on it, and a man who keeps a board at a post a mile and a half up that road will be standing at the counter with his paste pot and his rag, waiting for a bundle.

Five days.
