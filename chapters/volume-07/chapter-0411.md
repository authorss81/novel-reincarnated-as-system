# What A Column Needs

The third of a month beginning 1,034 at the seventh hour, in the room with the stove in it, and the seasonal book was out on the counter and open and there were eleven men in the room and about nine of them had come in from the cold.

The man behind the counter had the pen in his hand and a sheet of paper under it and he was setting the month up at the head of the page, and he did not stop when she came in because he has never once stopped for anybody.

She stood at the counter for about two minutes and then she said it.

“I want a line.”

He put the pen down flat.

“Not today,” he said.

## The Price Of It

“It is ten coppers,” he said. “It has been ten coppers since before I came and it is a day at this counter and it is ten days at Pei’s gate and it is not a deposit, it is the price of the line. You have asked me this before and I gave you the number then and you went away without paying it, and I did not put anything down either time, so you know as much about that as you did before you walked in.”

“I have seven,” she said.

“Yes. You said that on the fourteenth of a month two months back too and you had eleven then.”

He picked the pen up and turned it once and put it down again.

“That is the first part of it and it is the easy part. Ten coppers. It has been ten for forty years and it is ten whether you have it or not, and I have never moved it and I have never refused a person for not having it, because that is not what this counter is for.”

## The Three Things

He turned the book round on the counter so that the head of the page was facing her.

“A line is three things,” he said. “A name at the head of it, in the ordinary hand. A first day. And a last day.”

He put the flat of his hand on the open page.

“A name, so that the page belongs to a person and not to a place. A first day, so that the hours know where to start. A last day, so that the page can be shut.”

He looked at her.

“That is the whole of it. It is not a rule I made and it is not a rule this building made, and I could not enter a line with two of the three if I wanted to and I have wanted to twice.”

## The Two Inches Of Counter

He said the three things and then he put his hand flat on the counter and looked at her along about two inches of wood, which is the length of a man’s hand laid flat.

“There is a fourth thing and I am not going to say it yet, because you have not paid for the line and the fourth thing is the price.”

“All right.”

“I am going to say it anyway. You have been to this counter three times in two months and asked me a question every time and I have given you a number every time, and a man who is asked the same question three times is a man with something going on, and I would rather be told by you than work it out myself.”

He turned the book a quarter of a hundred degrees.

“The fourth thing is that a line has a place in it as well as a name and a first day and a last day, and the place is where the work was done, in the ordinary hand, and it is read by four people before anybody reads the hours. It is the part of the line that gets you.”

He put the pencil down.

“Put that with the four coppers and the ten days at that gate and tell me you still want one.”

She did not answer him and he did not wait for it.

“All right,” he said. “Go on, then.”

## What She Had And What She Did Not Have

She had the name. She had had it said out loud at this counter on the twenty-second of a month two months back, in the ordinary voice, on a step, in daylight, and it was in the book with the twenty-second against it and the book shuts.

She had the first day. There was not any question about the first day. She had been digging a drain at a gate three miles east of a mill whose wheel had not turned since before she came for seven months, and the gate paid five coppers a reach and a fifth of a reach was a copper a day, and if a man behind a counter wanted the first day of it, it was the first day she had ever dug at that gate.

And the third one.

She stood at a counter in a room with a stove in it with eleven men in it and she did not have the third one.

That was not a thing about her arm or her hand or a hold or a count of breath. It was a thing about whether she would be at that gate in the spring, and she had no idea, and inside nine days of road there was not a person she could ask, and no clerk in that building could give it to her.

## What He Asked

He asked her anyway, and she has been asked a great many things in seven months and this is the only one she has not been able to answer with a sentence.

“Last day,” he said.

“I do not know.”

“All right.”

“You have to write it down.”

“No,” he said. “I do not have to write it down because you have not paid for the line and I have not opened one, and there is nothing on this counter to write you down against.”

He shut the book.

It took him a second and a half and it shut the way it has always shut.

## What He Said With His Hand On It

He kept his hand flat on the closed book for four seconds, which is longer than a man behind a counter usually keeps a hand on a book.

“I will tell you what I do not know,” he said, “and then I am going back to my own work, because the season opens on the first and I have got nine men wanting pages.”

“All right.”

“I do not know whether you will be at that gate in the spring. I have watched you go out of that door eleven times in seven months and I have never once seen anybody come in. That is not a thing I am complaining about.”

“It is a thing I have been keeping in my head since the twenty-second of a month, and I could not say it out loud in this building, because the day I say it out loud it is a page.”

He picked the pen up.

“And I am not going to open a line on this counter that I would have to close, and I am not going to leave a line open on this counter that I cannot close, and I would rather have told you that at the fourth hour than at the ninth.”

## The Plain Part About One Person

She is eighteen years old and she asked a man behind a counter for a line in a room with eleven men in it, out loud, for the first time in seven months, and she gave him two of the three things a line needs and she did not have the third one and there was no way on nine days of road to get it.

He was right about all three and he did not write a word of it down.

There is a season’s book on that counter and it opens on the first of every month and it shuts every night and there is a name in it against the twenty-second of a month, and there is no name in it against the third.

And she had seven coppers of the ten, which is the part she is going to carry, because the reason she did not get a line was not the price and it is going to stop being about the price on the fourth of a month.

## The Walk Up The Mill Road

She went up the mill road from the counting house to the works at the eighth hour with seven coppers in the fold in the inside of the door frame, and she was still working it out when she got to the top of it.

Ten coppers. She could have had three more inside three days at a copper a day, and she had been telling herself for two months that the money was the reason.

The money was not the reason. She worked that out at the third milestone from the counting house and it took her about ninety seconds, and then she stood at a milestone on a made road for eleven minutes with seven coppers in the fold and the strip against her ribs, going over the two months again with the eight and the ten in front of her.

It had been the money because it was the only wall she had that she could see the top of.

A line needs a name and a first day and a last day and a place, and she had a name and a first day and a place and no last day, and the part she had not looked at for two months because she did not want it was the part that decides whether she will be standing at that gate in the spring.

## The Second Hour

She lay on the boards at the second hour of the fourth of a month with the bar under her right hand and she said it out loud, because it was the second hour and there was not a living soul in that shed.

“A line is a name, a first day and a last day, and I have got two of them and the third one is whether I am still standing at that gate in the spring, and I do not know, and inside nine days of road the only person who knows it is the man who keeps the list, and he is not going to tell me for nothing.”

She lay there.

“And it is ten coppers and I have got eight, and that is not why.”

She put a lump of clay in at the low end of the ring and it came up the width of a finger and stopped, and she left it there, because the fourth of a month was the next day and a rule is not worked out twice.
