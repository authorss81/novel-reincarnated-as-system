# A Question About Her

The twenty-second of the month began at the gate at the sixth hour with a bar in her right hand and four fingers and a thumb on it, and the run was nearly filled in, and Pei was inside.

At the eighth hour she was walking up the made road from the gate with the iron over her shoulder when the man behind the counter came out of the door of the counting house and stood on the step and looked up the mill road and then at her, and then went back in.

He came out again four seconds later with his coat on over his shirt.

## What He Said On The Step

“I want to say two things to you and I am going to say them out loud here because I would rather you heard them in daylight on a road than in my room with a book open.”

“All right.”

He did not come down off the step.

“There is a thing on my counter that did not come up a road,” he said. “It came into this building this morning in a way I have not had anything come into this building in eleven years, and I have been in buildings for forty and I have never had one arrive like that, and I am telling you that I have noticed before I tell you anything else about it.”

“Yes.”

“And I am going to read it to you here on the step because it has your name at the head of it and I would not read it to you over a counter with the book open.”

## What Was On It

He had it in his hand, folded once, and he did not open it until she was within four feet of him and then he opened it and held it out flat.

It was the strip. It was the same strip that had come down on the eighteenth and gone up on the nineteenth with the ordinary post, and it had the two lines at the bottom of it, and it had nine words written at the top of it in the ordinary hand of the woman at the top of that building.

And under the nine words, in a hand that had never been in that room and was not the fine hand of any office on nine days of road, there were two lines more.

“The holder has answered.”

“The holder has been read to and the reading has been carried up and this ground has not had that happen before. The answer is given.”

“There,” he said. “That is the whole of it, and it came into this building at the fourth hour this morning, and there is no cart on the mill road and there is no post and there is no boy.”

## The Question

“There is one more line and it is under those two,” he said, “and I am going to read that one too, and I am telling you beforehand that it is a question and that you are not obliged to answer it and that I am going to write down whatever you say, in the ordinary hand, in the seasonal book, against today.”

He looked at it.

“The answer names a woman refused at this counter on the twenty-sixth of a month six months back. Is that woman at the gate three miles east of the mill.”

The mill road was empty. There was a cart going up it four hundred yards off with nothing on it.

## What She Felt Standing There

She had nine days to think about this and she had used eight of them on other things.

It did not come as a shock. That was the thing, and she had worked it out on the walk and it was the whole of what she had on the twenty-second of a month at the eighth hour with an iron bar over her right shoulder.

She was not surprised. She had named the price out loud on the boards at the second hour on the nineteenth of a month and she had named it correctly and she had got a woman of forty-five to write nine words on a strip in a room with forty books open on it, and everything that had happened since then had been the price being paid and arriving at the place it was going to arrive at.

An answer cannot be refused. She had known that since the eighteenth.

## What She Said

He waited the way a man behind a counter waits, which is without moving.

“Yes,” she said.

In the ordinary voice, in daylight, on a step, at four feet, at the eighth hour of the twenty-second of a month, with an empty mill road behind her.

He wrote it down inside the door on the counter and the seasonal book was shut before she was inside the room, and he opened it at the page with her name on it, and he wrote, and he read it back to himself once without saying it, and he shut the book.

“Ning Yan, of Lowwater. At the gate three miles east of the mill. The twenty-second.”

That is eleven words, and it is in the only book on nine days of road that shuts, and it is in the ordinary hand.

## The Thing He Said Afterwards

He came back out onto the step with the pen in his hand and did not put it away.

“I am going to say the rest of it and then I am going back to my own work,” he said. “There is a refusal in that book from the twenty-sixth of a month six months back and it is still in it and nothing has come off it and nothing is going to, and I am not touching it and I have never touched it.”

“Yes.”

“And there is now a second line about you in that book, and I wrote it in nine seconds because you answered me in one word and a one-word answer does not need a room.”

He put the pen in his pocket.

“And I want you to understand what has just happened on that step, because I do not think you will be able to see it from inside it and I have been behind this counter eleven years and I have not had a morning like this one.”

## What Had Happened On The Step

“You have said no to this counter once, in this room, in front of six men, and I wrote it down and it went up the mill road on a first with a cord round it and it is still in that book.”

“And this morning this counter held an answer, from a room above my own, that it has not asked anybody for and did not expect to get, and it has already been read, and the reading went up, and I do not know where it has gone and neither do you.”

He looked at her.

“And you have now given this counter a second thing of your own, in the ordinary voice, at the end of a day, in daylight, and it is in that book, and it shuts.”

He came down one step.

“I am not going to tell you that you should not have answered it. I have been behind this counter eleven years and nothing I know would tell me that, and there is a room somewhere on nine days of road that has been shut five times on a date, and you have not seen it and neither have I.”

## The Plain Part About One Person

She is eighteen years old.

She can never again refuse a reading.

Not because there is a rule against it and not because anybody has decided it. Because an office on this ground now holds an answer that has already been given, and a second refusal would be a refusal of an answer already given, and an office that holds an answer already given does not accept a second one.

There was one option in that book and she had it on the twenty-sixth of a month six months back, and she spent it then, and she has walked for six months believing she had it still, and she had it still until the eighth hour this morning.

That refusal is still in the book with the twenty-sixth against it. Nothing has taken it out. What has gone is not the entry. It is the second time.

## The Part She Did Not Say On The Mill Road

She did not say thank you.

She stood on the made road four hundred yards below that building for two minutes with the bar over her right shoulder and she worked out, on the boards, whether she had been got.

She had not been got. That was the part she could not make any other way and she made it nine times while standing in the middle of a made road in the middle of the afternoon.

A man had come out of a building in daylight and told her a true thing about a piece of paper, and she had answered it, and the answer was one word and it was true, and a man behind a counter had written it in a book that shuts in a minute and a half.

And an office on nine days of road had just read a line upward for the first time in eleven hundred years of practice, and she had been the reason it could, and the price had come out of a shed at a mill three miles east at the second hour on the nineteenth of a month and had arrived at a gate this morning without anybody carrying it.

And what she had wanted, for four seconds at the eighth hour on a step, was to be able to put it back.

## What Was On The Board At The Works

She read it on the sixteenth of the month at the rail and she read it again on the twenty-second and it was the same both times.

Eight lines. Two hundred yards of dressed stone by the load. Forty feet of bed at the north end of the yard at the turn with the fourteenth against it in the margin.

And under it, in a strip from that yard that had come back down on the twenty-first and gone out to the yard with the bundle of the twenty-second, two lines in the ordinary hand of a building two miles west.

“Nothing on this page can be entered as it stands.”

“Two holders are now required against the seventeenth of a month and there is one holder on this ground.”

The man with the rule in his hand had written his margin note under both of them, in his own hand, on the twenty-first, exactly as he had told her on the thirteenth he would.

He was right. That was the part that did it.

He had said on the thirteenth that there was no column on that ground for what had happened at the south end of his yard, and no term for it, and that he would not invent one. Eleven days later there were two holders required against the seventeenth of a month.

One of them was nine days of road away and had never been on that ground. The other one was standing at a gate three miles east of a mill whose wheel had not turned.

And the page was going up the mill road on the first at the sixth hour with a cord round the tray, and it was going to come down on the ninth, and nothing in that building could stop it.
