# The Book Of What Happened

The yard book of a yard at the turn came back down the mill road on the ninth of a month inside the tray and it went out again to the yard on the tenth with the ordinary bundle, because a yard book is not the book of that building and a yard book does not stay in a building.

She went and looked at it on the thirteenth at the fourth hour.

The man with the rule in his hand had it open on the office end table with the cord still round it and he was standing the way a man stands when he has been waiting four days to say something.

“Sit down,” he said.

“I would rather stand.”

“Sit down anyway,” he said, “because this takes about four minutes and I would like to have said it sitting down once in twenty-two years.”

## What Was On The Page

“Twenty-two years of this yard,” he said, “and that book has a page for every week of it and it goes up the mill road on the first with everything else in that building and comes back down on the ninth, and every page that has ever gone up it has been a page of what men did here.”

He put his hand flat on it.

“Your sill line is on the eleventh of that page. Two hundred and ten feet of bed and twenty-nine stones, and the low side went on the twenty-eighth at the third hour and the course is holding six days later, and I have written that and two men have checked it and it is the correct page for that line.”

He turned the page over.

“And underneath it I have written, in the place where a yard book writes what went wrong, that on the seventeenth of a month a dressed stone was moved out of its bed at the south end and has not been returned and is not going to be, and that the bed at that end is four inches down with a lip on it.”

## The Two Lines That Came Back

“And here is what came down off it on the ninth.”

He turned the page to the foot and turned it round so the writing was the right way up for her, and there were two lines at the foot of it in the ordinary hand of the building two miles west, and she could read them from four feet off because it was an ordinary hand and she has been reading ordinary hands since she was six years old.

“Nothing on this page can be entered as it stands.”

“A holder is required against the seventeenth of a month and the holder is not at this end of it.”

He let her read them twice.

“I cannot do one thing with those two lines,” he said. “I have been keeping that book twenty-two years and I have never had a page come back with a line at the foot of it that I could not enter, and I have certainly never had one that wanted something off a yard.”

## What He Said He Would Do

He squared the page against the table.

“I am going to send it up again on the first of the next month with the two lines still at the foot of it, because that is what a yard book does and because there is no other correct thing for a yard book to do with a page it cannot enter.”

“All right.”

“And I am going to write in the margin of my own page, in my own hand, before I cord it, that the two lines at the foot of it came down off the ninth and were not in the page when it went up, and the day they came down.”

He looked at her.

“That is a margin note in a yard book and it goes up the same road and it will be read by whoever reads this, and there is no version of me not writing it, because a book that does not say what came back off it is not a book.”

## The Worst Thing That Happened To Her That Month

“One of my nine asked me on the tenth,” he said, “whether the woman from the gate was the one who did it.”

She did not say anything.

“I told him no,” he said. “That is the same three words I have said about four hundred things since you started coming to that yard and it is the correct answer, because there is no name at that yard and there never has been one and you are not on my board.”

He put his hand flat on the page again.

“That is also the answer that puts you in it,” he said. “Because a man asked me that question and I have been keeping that book twenty-two years and he does not know why he asked it. He knows a stone moved. He knows a woman has been standing nine feet off that yard for six weeks. He worked that out himself, on his own, in his head, with nothing to go on.”

He turned the page back the right way up and squared it.

“I have not told him anything. I have not told him a single thing about you, and I am not going to, and I want you to hear me say the other half of that as well, because I am right on both halves and you will work it out on the walk home and I would rather you had it from me at four o’clock than at eight.”

## The Plain Part About One Person

She wanted to say “tell him”.

That is all she wanted. One ordinary sentence, out loud, in an office end with one man in it, and it would have fixed it and it would have been over in nine seconds.

She did not say it. A name told in an office end goes on a page in a yard book, and that page went up the mill road on the first and came back down on the ninth, and she has spent seven weeks learning exactly what that does to a person.

What she said instead was nothing, and what the man with the rule in his hand did with nothing was put the page square and cord the corner of it and go back to the board, and neither of those is a thing either of them will remember in a month.

So the correct answer was said over her head and the wrong answer was said under her feet and both of them were right, and she walked four miles back up the made road with the strip against her ribs and four fingers and a thumb on her right hand and she did not say one word for the first two miles.

## The Fifteenth, At The South End

She was at that yard on the fifteenth at the sixth hour for nothing, because there was nothing to do there and she had started walking four miles east when there was nothing to do.

The second course was on. Two hundred yards of it, forty feet of bed at the north end going under the sill that had gone down on the fourteenth of the last month, and eight men on the north end and one man at the low side with a barrow.

The man who had asked the question was on the bed. He was about thirty and he had been on that yard four years and she knew that because she had watched him for six weeks, and he was working, and he worked well, and he did not look at her once in ninety minutes.

At the seventh hour he came off the bed to shift a plank and went past her at about four feet.

“Water’s in the bucket,” he said.

She said thank you. He went and got the bucket and came back and put it down where the planks were and went back to the bed.

That was the whole of it. Nine days after he had asked a man with a rule in his hand whether she was the one who had done it, and about two weeks after he had worked out on his own that she was, he had given her a bucket of water and said four words, and had not asked her anything at all.

She picked the bucket up and carried it about eleven feet and put it down where the planks were, which is where he had put it, and went and stood at the low side for the rest of the morning.

And that was the answer to the whole of it. There was no answer to the whole of it. There was a man of thirty who was right about a stone and right about a woman and had been given three words to say about her and had found a fourth thing to say instead, on his own, at four feet, on a Tuesday, at the seventh hour.

## The Fourteenth And The Sixteenth

The fourteenth was a gate day and she was out at the sixth hour and the sixteenth was a gate day and she was out at the sixth hour and the bar went in where the ground was hard and it came up easy both times.

On the sixteenth, at the fifth hour, she did the sum, and it was the same as it had been on the seventh of a month.

There is a page at the top of this mill road with her name in it in the ordinary hand. There is a page at the bottom of the same road with a hole in it and nothing in the margin. Neither of them has her name on it and both of them go up on the same cart on the same morning, and one of them has a name under a rule with nothing at the end of it, and that name is not hers.

That was the whole of what she had on the sixteenth, and she had four coppers in the fold and bread for two days and a question going up a road that she could not answer and had not been asked.

And on the seventeenth she would be at the yard at the turn, and on the eighteenth there was a gate day, and on the nineteenth there was a gate day.

## What She Carried Down The Mill Road

“There is a holding in a book two miles down this road with one name under it and the name is not mine and the man it belongs to is not on this ground, and I told a counter on the twenty-ninth of a month that I was not carrying him anywhere, and that is in a book in the ordinary hand that shuts.”

“And there is a yard at the turn whose page has come back asking for a holder against the seventeenth of a month, and the only holder anybody has ever entered anywhere on nine days of road is that same name, and I am the only person within nine days of road who could say so, and saying it would put it in a book in the building it was asked from.”

“And this ground is not going to tell her what happens to a person whose name is at the foot of a page.”

She lay on the boards that night with the bar under her hand and thought about the two lines and the margin the man with the rule in his hand was going to write in his own hand, and about a yard book that had twenty-two years in it and could not close a page.
