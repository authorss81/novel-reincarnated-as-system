# The Seventh Person

The fire did not come up on the fourteenth, the fifteenth or the sixteenth of a month, and that made thirteen days.

She counted them on the boards at the second hour on the sixteenth with the flat of her second finger against the boards, the way she had counted every date in four years, and she got thirteen both times.

Thirteen days of a ring that came up at a finger’s width and stopped.

## What She Did With Thirteen Days

She worked the gate thirteen days at a fifth of a reach and got thirteen coppers, and she went to the store once, on the seventh, with eight of them, and came back with bread for five days, and she walked thirty-nine miles west and thirty-nine miles east and about a mile and a quarter every second day back to the flat past the last hedge to look at a bank she was not on.

She took a length at the yard on six days and got a cup of something hot out of the office end on all six, and a man with a rule in his hand did not once ask her why she was coming, and a woman who worked in that office did not once ask her name.

She stood in a gateway at the works on six days and read a board with eleven lines on it, every one of them dressed stone, every one of them priced by the ton, and no line on it she could take.

She had walked six days of three miles east and six days of four miles east on the strength of a board she could read and could not work, and she had done both of those things because they were the only two places on that ground where a person who can read a column was ever standing still.

She carried four barrow-loads of clay out of the shed and put them against the outside wall of it, and she went up the mill road once, at the second hour on the tenth, and a man with a brush told her that a column had two lines at the foot of it where it had had four.

She worked it out twice on the boards and got the same five both times. Thirteen days, thirteen coppers, one trip west at eight, five in the fold in the inside of the door frame at the end of them.

That was the whole of the thirteen days: a barrow against a wall, a fifth of a sack of clay in it, and her hands cold.

## The Gate, The Sixteenth

Pei came out at the eighth hour with his own bar and his boy on the fence with a stick.

He did not say one word about the arm, because he had never once said one word about the arm. What he said, on the sixteenth of a month at about the eighth hour at a gate three miles east of a mill whose wheel had not turned, was this.

“You have been opening that drain shallower.”

“I have.”

“You were four feet at the start of the month and you have been at three and a half for about six days, and you are not doing it wrong, and the third day you went at four I said nothing and the sixth day you went at three and a half I said nothing, and I am saying it now because a man at a gate has one thing he is allowed to say and it is how deep a drain is.”

“Thank you.”

“I am not doing it for that,” he said. “I am doing it because the ground is soft on the low side after the rain and if you go down four feet on the low side in this you will get into a hole you cannot get out of with four fingers, and I would rather tell you than find out.”

He went back inside and she stood in the gateway for about a minute with the iron in her hand and the copper in the fold in the inside of the door frame and the third and a half feet of the drain she had done that morning standing about four feet off her.

The boy on the fence put the stick down through the rails and picked it up again, and that was the end of that, and she filled the cut in before she left, because a gate left open at the eighth hour is a gate somebody else has to find out about.

## The Plain Part About One Person

She is eighteen years old.

She has four fingers and a thumb on one hand and an arm that is open half of the time and eight holds in the other, and a furnace that has stood nine feet high against a brick in a corner of a shed since the twenty-second of a month four months back, and it did that on its own, at about the second hour, every morning, and it has never asked her for anything.

It stopped on the second of a month when she used it to hold up a bar with four pieces of heavy stone on it and nine men on the bank above her, and on the third it came up a finger’s width and stopped, and it has done that every day since.

And four months of a fire that came up on its own had been what taught her the difference between what she could carry and what it took to carry it, and it had taught her for nothing, because nothing had ever said it out loud to her.

There is no clay in that shed that does it and there is no hold that does it and there is no bar that does it, and the only thing that has ever brought that fire up is somebody being in the room, and she had been through that list twice already and it came out the same way both times.

## The List She Did Not Say Out Loud

She worked it out on the boards at about the second hour on the sixteenth, standing up, the way she worked everything out, and it took forty minutes and it came out in six.

The man who runs the yard. She had been in his yard three times. He had found her in an order in two seconds and he was right about it and she was not going to move him.

The man behind the counter. He keeps a book and he asked her one question in front of nine men and she gave him an answer, and the answer is in the only book on nine days of road that shuts. Asking him for a pair of hands would be the second thing she has said to him that gets written down.

The man who keeps the list. He reads a board out loud in a gateway every second day in the season and he told her in that gateway that the reading is not a trade and is not paid.

The man with the brush, and the gate man, and the carter with the chain.

Six people she could name, and every one of them somebody she would not ask.

Three of the six had families she had seen going up and down a made road, and one of the six was a man who had put a cup of something hot into her hands six times without asking her name, and she had not been able to make that an opening.

## The Seventh

And then she sat down on the boards about four feet off a ring that would not come up, and she thought about the one person inside nine days of road whose hands she could put in that corner without it being a thing four people would agree about later.

He was about fifty-five. He had a paste pot and a rag and nine years on a board at a post on the mill road. He could not open the book she had left standing on a stone under his ladder and could not read a word in it and would not shut it.

And she had never spoken to him in her life, and she had not gone near that post since she stood in his doorway, and she had not thought about him once in six weeks.

But he was on the mill road.

And the counting house was on the mill road.

And she had gone up that road on the tenth of a month at the second hour and down it again, and she would go up it again inside a month because the man with a brush copies her column out once a month and she had been going up it since she came past this mill.

That was the whole of what she had.

## The Thing She Was Going To Ask

She lay on the boards with her hands behind her head and her hands were cold and the ring was cold and the shed was cold, and she said it out loud because it was the second hour and there was not a living soul in the room.

“I have to put a person in this room and I have to be able to say it out loud and mean it.”

She lay there with that for about a minute.

“And I have to work out the walking. It is nine miles each way to that post and the counting house is about two miles before it, and I have to be walking both ways inside a day, and I have one copper a day and a gate that goes at three and a half feet now, and I have got about a fifth of a sack of clay in a barrow and no wages and a board I cannot take a line off.”

She laid her palm on the flat stone by the door. It was cold. Her hand was cold. The two of them were the same temperature and it told her nothing, because there was nothing in there to tell her anything.

What she was going to ask a man with a paste pot about two miles short of his own ladder, on a road she walked four or five times a month, was whether he would stand in a doorway for about as long as it takes to bring up a fire.

She had not said it to anybody. She was not going to say it to a man in a gateway about work and she was not going to say it to a man with a bench and she was not going to say it to the layer, which has never told her a thing and does not advise.

And there was a barrow-load of clay against the outside wall of a shed at a mill whose wheel had not turned since before she came, and a fire in the corner of it that had come up at a finger’s width every morning for thirteen days by her own hand with four fingers and a thumb, and would not come up the rest of a finger.
