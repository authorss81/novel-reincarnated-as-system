# The Beginning Of A Habit

The gate was four feet of drain on the low side of Pei’s field and she had it open by ten minutes past the seventh hour on the twenty-sixth of a month, with a strip folded in two inside her coat against the oilcloth and a short iron she had borrowed out of the gate house at dawn.

The bar was still against the fence of the shed, a mile behind her, where it had stood since the twenty-third of a month about two months back. She had got as far as picking it up twice since then and put it down both times, and the reason she put it down was the one she had said out loud on open ground with nothing within half a mile, and she was not going to say it again in a gateway.

The iron was the gate’s and it went back in the box before she left. That was the arrangement and Pei had not said one word about it, because an iron belongs to the gate house and a woman who opens four feet of drain four days in seven is not going to be the one who changes that.

She got a fifth of a reach out of it. That is the price at the gate now, five coppers a reach, and a fifth is one copper and the ground comes up to the lip of the bank about every four feet she goes down.

It had been a third of a reach for five months before Golden Core eight and a third was one and a half, and the half copper had gone out of her life on the twenty-third of a month in a gateway and had not come back since.

## What The Iron Is For

The iron came out of the box at dawn and it went back into it at the ninth hour, and in between it was the only thing she had to put a reach out with.

A spade is a lever for stone and clay. A bar is a lever for a dressed piece, and a bar has to be under the stone before the stone comes, and the last foot of that goes by hand, and that is two hands.

The iron goes in under the spade and turns the spade over, and a turn of the iron is one hand and it does not need a hand under anything.

So the reach went at a fifth instead of a third because a bar takes two hands, and Pei was right about the price of a fifth, and the difference between one and one and a half had come out of her life without anybody having to say a sentence about it.

## The Strip

She got it out at the eighth hour and stood in the gateway with it open on her forearm, four fingers and a thumb holding the bottom edge, and read the ordinary half of it again because she had been carrying it for a day and a night.

Her name. Of Lowwater. A column of days with the dates down the margin and the hours against them and a season marked at each end, and above the hours the word that said good, in the hand a man with a bench at a gate had put there in daylight five months back because she had asked him to.

And then about a third of the way down, in the fine hand, four or five lines and a rule under the last of them, and a word at the head of them she could not read a letter of.

She knew the word. She had known it three days before the strip arrived, standing on a bank with her hand on a stone she had not touched.

That was the whole of what a strip had ever been to her for four years. Somebody wrote a thing about her in a hand she could not read and carried it nine days of road and put it into her hand, and what she got out of it was the fact that it had happened, which she had been there for.

She folded it in two and put it back against the oilcloth, and the shape of it had not changed in a day and a night and she had checked it about four times anyway.

## The Board

She walked the three miles east at about the ninth hour with the iron in her hand and left it at the gate of the works on the way out, because it was not hers and she had not asked.

The board was inside the gateway on the wall to the left of the bench, and it had been bare since the fifteenth of a month about two months back, and the man who kept the list had a cover over it and two battens across it and a key in his pocket.

She had walked three miles to look at it.

That was the thing she noticed that day, standing in that gateway with the cover on the board and her hand on the iron she had just given back. She had walked three miles in the last seven days and she had walked three miles on the day before that and she had walked three miles on the day before that.

The gate she worked was three miles west of her and the shed was a mile and a quarter west of the gate, and she had not been to the board for a thing.

She had not been there for a line. There was nothing at all on it.

She stood in the gateway for about four minutes and the man who kept the list came out and asked her whether she wanted anything, and she said no, and he said the season turns, and she said she knew, and he went back inside.

## The Thing She Was Doing Instead

There was a thing she had been carrying since the twenty-fifth of a month and she had not put it down anywhere.

She had been refused at a counter four days before that, on the twenty-sixth of the month before, in a room with a stove in it, in front of about nine men, and she had said her name and then she had said no, and a man with a pen had written it into the only book on nine days of road that shuts.

From that hour there was not going to be a sending.

She had worked that out inside about a minute and a half of walking and she had not told anybody and she was not going to tell anybody, because what there was to tell was that she had asked a room to come and get somebody and the room had put a word in a book instead.

Nine days of road and she will not walk it. Nine days of road and nothing on it brings anybody east.

So the thing she had been carrying since the twenty-fifth was the shape of what a refusal does to a person who is expecting a knock.

The knock does not come.

That was all. It does not come on the twenty-eighth, or on the first, or on any day after that, and the only thing that comes is the next strip about nine or ten days later, and she is standing in the gateway of a works on a bare board three miles from a gate when it does.

## What She Wanted, Out Loud

She said it to the iron in her hand on the walk back west, at about the ninth hour, with nothing within a mile of her and the light going.

“I wanted somebody to come.”

And then, because she was eighteen and had been out of a bound six months and had said two sentences to herself out loud on that ground in seven months and both of them had been about a bar, she said the second one.

“I would have gone back if they had come. That is the part I want written down and I have never once managed to say it out loud to a person.”

## On The Road West

She got to the shed at about the eleventh hour and the ring was burning nine feet away in the corner, about nine feet high, and it had come up on its own at about the second hour that morning and had not needed her at any hour of the day.

She put her hand flat on the flat stone by the door out of the habit of four thousand mornings. It was cold. Her hand was warm. The two of them told her nothing.

She stood in the doorway a while and let the fire be a fire and thought about the strip against her ribs, and about the fact that the only people who have ever written anything about her write it in a hand she cannot read, and that she has three of those pieces of paper in the world and can read the ordinary half of all three.

Three days after it happened, a man she will never meet put the word at the head of it on a piece of cloth and sent it nine days of road to a gate post, because the reading of it is a thing that gets put on paper by somebody.

And she cannot read the hand.
