# What A Keeper Has To Fetch

The fire did not come up on the seventeenth of a month either, and that made fourteen days.

She lay on the boards in the dark until the light came up over the flat, and then she went and crouched at the low end of the ring with her hand four inches off the brick, and nothing came up, and she did it for a quarter of an hour before she went out.

There is a way of standing in a gateway with an iron in your hand that takes about two and a half hours, and she had got good at it in a fortnight, and the only thing she had got out of it was one copper.

## What She Had In The Fold

Five coppers, in the fold in the inside of the door frame, where it had been since the seventh of a month.

Eight had gone west on the seventh, and there was bread in the shed for five days, and the fifth day of it was the twelfth.

So she had eaten the last of it at the fourth hour on the twelfth of a month, and had gone to the gate on the twelfth, the thirteenth, the fourteenth, the fifteenth and the sixteenth, and had got five coppers out of a drain three and a half feet deep, and she was not hungry, and she had not been hungry on any day since before she came out of the bound, and she was not going to start now.

What she was, was light.

She noticed it at the gate on the fifteenth with the short iron in her right hand and the right arm open all of it. The cut was coming up easy at three and a half feet in a way it had not come up easy at four feet, and the spoil was not needing the bar the way spoil needs a bar at the bottom of four feet.

She noticed it and she worked out what it was and she kept on.

A week of gate is seven coppers. Five days of bread out of a store five miles west at two coppers a loaf is eight. She was short a copper a week, and she had been short a copper a week since the day she came past this mill, and the difference had come out of a stock she had brought with her and had never once entered anywhere.

The stock had been eight days of bread when she came past the mill. There was none of it now.

## The Gate, The Seventeenth

Pei came out at the eighth hour with his own bar and did not look at the drain, which was a thing he had stopped doing on the sixteenth.

“You are running out of days before you are running out of cut,” he said.

“I have got till the thirtieth.”

“You have got till the thirtieth,” he agreed, “and the low side is going soft again on this side of the year and the fourth foot is where it starts to slide, and I am telling you the same thing I told you on the sixteenth and I am telling it once more and then I am going back inside.”

She stood in the gateway and let him go back inside.

Three men had come to that gate since the season turned and two of them had asked her the same question in the same words, which was whether she wanted the depth done a certain way, and she had found that she could not explain to either of them why she was at three and a half feet when four feet was the same money to the man paying for it.

A fifth of a reach is a fifth of a reach. It had been a third of a reach and one and a half coppers until Golden Core eight, and that half copper had gone out of her life and had not come back, and the reason was a bar that takes two hands and a gate house with one short iron in it.

She filled the cut in before she left, because a gate left open at the eighth hour is a gate somebody else has to find out about.

## The Thing She Worked Out Standing Up

She did it at the gateway at the ninth hour with the iron back in the box and the hasp down, the way she stood for everything.

A man who keeps a board at a post has a board and a post, and a post sits in the ground. What a man like that has not got is paper.

Paper on nine days of road comes out of one building only. It comes out of the counting house in a bundle with a cord round it, once a month, on the first of a month, on the cart at the sixth hour, because everything that has come into that room in thirty days goes up that road at the sixth hour on the first whether it shuts or not.

She had watched that cart go twice and had never once been on it.

And a man who keeps a board at a post a mile and a half up the mill road beyond the counting house, nine years on that board, cannot send a boy, because a boy costs a day and a half and there is one of him. He cannot keep a board for a month out of a memory either, because a board is a strip with days on it and a strip that is a day late is a strip that is wrong.

So he fetches his strips.

Not up at his post. At the counting house, on the first, with the cart, because that is the only morning in the month that anything comes down that road.

And the counting house is about a mile and a half short of his own ladder, and she has walked up that road four or five times a month since she came past this mill, and she has never once spoken to him in her life.

The first of a month was in fourteen days.

## What She Was Not Going To Do

She was not going to the post.

That was settled inside a minute and it did not need working out. His ladder was six or nine miles west of that gate, depending which road a person counted, and she had not gone near it since she stood in his doorway a long time back and left a book of hers on a stone under it and walked away.

A book under a ladder is a thing you leave. A man at a post is a thing you visit. And if she had walked nine miles in the dark to stand at his post on a morning in the middle of a month, he would have known what she came for, because he has kept a board for nine years and a board is a thing that teaches you what people come for.

So: the counting house. The first of a month. The sixth hour.

And there is the other half of it, which she put down flat on the boards with the flat of her second finger when she got back, because it was the whole of the problem and everything in the last fourteen days had been the edge of it.

If she stood in that room on the morning of the first, on purpose, with a man in it she had never spoken to, then she had put a thing where four people could agree about it later.

And she could not read the hand.

## The Plain Part About One Person

She is eighteen years old, and she is light in the head for three days out of seven because the bread ran out on the twelfth and does not get bought again until the twentieth, and she has five coppers on the seventeenth and eight on the twentieth.

She has four fingers and a thumb on one hand and an arm that is open half of the time and eight holds in the other, and a furnace in a shed at a mill whose wheel has not turned since before she came, and the furnace has not come up since the second of a month.

She wanted somebody to be angry with her.

That was the want, standing in that gateway on the seventeenth at the ninth hour with nothing in her hands, and it was the first time in four years she had wanted a thing she could not get out of work.

She had been angry at a man with a rule in his hand for two minutes on a made road four miles east, and at a man who keeps a list for two minutes in a gateway three miles east. Both of them had been right, and she had walked the anger down to nothing both times.

There is no person on nine days of road she has a right to be angry with. Four people have written her down and every one of them did the job they had.

She walked the three miles east at the tenth hour and read the board from the bottom up in the gateway, standing the way she stands, and there were still eleven lines on it and every one of them was dressed stone and every one of them was priced by the ton.

She had been standing in that gateway every second day for two months reading a board that had been full for five weeks and could not pay her, and she went on doing it, and the reason she went on doing it is the same reason a person goes on reading a thing that has nothing in it for them.

Somebody reads that board out loud in that gateway every second day in the season, in front of anybody who happens to be standing there.

## Fourteen Days, And A Copper A Day

She lay down at the second hour with the boards under her back and worked out the whole of the month before she slept, because it was the only thing on nine days of road that came out the same both times.

Four barrow-loads of clay against the outside wall of the shed and a fifth of a sack standing in a barrow on its handles, and no ring to put it in.

Six days at a full board three miles east that she could read and could not work, and a length at the yard at the turn on four of them, and a cup of something hot out of an office end from a woman who had never once asked her name.

And on the fourteenth day of them, at the sixth hour, on a made road east, a gateway with a cart standing in it and a man in it with a paste pot and a rag, and nothing at all in her hands.

She put the clay in that night anyway, because there is nothing else to do with an evening.

It came up a finger’s width at the low end and sat there and did not go against the brick and stood nine feet high, and the shed was cold at the back and cold at the door and cold in her hands.

She watched it for about as long as it takes to fill a barrow, and then she stopped watching it, because there was no second living thing in that shed and there was not going to be one before the light came up.

She lay down in her coat with her boots on, because the store was five miles off and she had five coppers in the fold and three days to get eight.

On that morning there would be a cart standing in a gateway on a made road east with a man in it and a paste pot and a rag, and she would be in that gateway at the sixth hour with nothing at all in her hands, and she had never once spoken to him in her life.
