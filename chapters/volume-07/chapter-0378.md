# The Hand On The Ground

She worked the gate on every day from the twenty-first to the first and went to the counting house on the first of a month before the light came up, and she had been at the counter by the sixth hour with three coppers in the fold in the inside of the door frame and nothing in her hands.

That was the sum of the twenty-first to the first: eight coppers in eight days to the twenty-eighth, eight of them gone west at a store five miles off, and three more off the twenty-ninth, the thirtieth and the first, and that was all of it.

She had eaten the last of what she bought on the twentieth and had gone to the store on the twenty-eighth with eight coppers and had bread for five days, and she had a fifth of a sack of clay in a barrow at the shed and five barrow-loads against the wall and a ring in the corner of it that had come up a finger’s width and stopped every morning she put a lump in it since the third of a month.

She had not worked out one sentence to say.

## The First Of A Month

Everything that comes into that room in thirty days goes up the mill road at the sixth hour with a cord round it.

She stood at the low end of the counter about four feet off with the stove at her shoulder and nine men in the room, and the man behind the counter was cording a tray, and three bundles on the floor by the wall with string round them, and a man on the top step with sheets squared up beside him and a brush worn to a stub at the ferrule.

The cart was in the yard at the half hour with four horses and a chain over the bed, and the mill road went up past the elms and past a mile and a half of ground she had never walked, and everything that had ever gone up it had gone up it and stopped there.

She did not go up.

## The Man With The Paste Pot

He was at the counter with his back to her and a bundle on the floor at his feet, and he had a rag over his shoulder and a paste pot in his hand with the lid off it, and he was fifty-five or thereabouts.

She had never spoken to him. She had stood in his doorway once, a long time back, and left a book of hers on a stone under his ladder and walked away from it.

She knew the rest of him the way she knew the rest of the ground. He kept a board at a post two miles up the mill road beyond this house and had kept it for nine years. He fetched his strips here on the first because that is the only morning in the month anything comes down this road. He had a woman at the post and a boy of about twelve and he was not paid by anybody at this counter.

She stood four feet off him and said nothing.

The man behind the counter gave him the bundle and he put it on the sill and got down off the stool, and the stool was high and his right knee had not been right since the twentieth of a month a year ago, and he put his left hand flat on the floor of that room to get himself down and steady.

The floor of that room is packed earth and stone under an inch of beaten clay.

She felt it come up a second and a half before it was under his hand.

## What She Had Time To Do

She had time for about a second and a half.

There is a word a person can say. It has four words in it, and it would have put a man with a paste pot out of a doorway for the rest of his life, and it would have been true, and it would not have gone anywhere, because nothing said at the sixth hour in a low building with nine men in it goes anywhere a man who keeps a board for nine years cannot repeat.

And it would have been a thing that had been put where four people could agree about it later.

So she did not say it.

The ground under the clay came up the way the flat came up out from under her feet on the third of a month on a bank with nine men on it, and it went under the floor of that room and up through the inch of beaten clay over it, and it was under his hand before he had finished getting down.

## What He Said

He got his hand up and looked at the inside of it.

The heel of the palm was white and then red across two inches, and the inside of the forearm where it had been resting on the clay had a band on it a hand wide, and the skin on it was already coming up in blisters on the flat part of the forearm where the bone is straight.

He did not shout.

That is the thing she was not ready for. He looked at his hand for three seconds and then he said, in the ordinary voice:

“That ground is hot.”

The nine of them said nothing. The man behind the counter came round the end of it and stood four feet off him and did not touch him, and she understood that a man behind a counter who puts a hand on a burned arm in front of nine men has put a thing where four people can agree about it later.

The keeper put his hand down on the sill with the other one and stood up straight and looked at the floor where his hand had been.

Then he looked across the room at her.

There were nine men in it and six of them were looking at the floor, and he looked past all of them at a woman standing four feet off a counter with a bag in no hands at all.

“Did you do that,” he said.

And she stood four feet off a counter in a low building on the first of a month at the sixth hour and said the word.

“Yes.”

## What He Did About It

He looked at her arm.

Not her face. Her arm, the right one, and the four fingers and a thumb on it, and the way it was hanging, and he looked at it for two seconds the way a man looks at a thing he is working out where it goes in an order.

Then he said, “I have got to get that bundle two miles up the road and I have not got a hand that closes.”

That was the whole of what he said about it. There was no question after it and he did not say her name and she did not give it, and he had said yes back at her inside four seconds and had got on with it, which is what a man does when he has been hurt in front of strangers and has a road in front of him.

The bundle was a roll of strips about the size of a barrow wheel. She had watched men carry things on that counter for seven months. She took it off the sill with her left arm and her right and went out into the yard with it.

## The Mile And A Half Up The Road

She carried it two miles.

Two hands is the only way to carry a roll like that and she had four fingers and a thumb and an arm that was open for half of the walk, and she had it on her hip with the arm under it for half a mile and then she had it against her chest with the other hand on the top of it, and she did not put it down once, and it took her fifty minutes.

He walked beside her with his right arm held against his chest and his left in his coat.

They did not speak for fifty minutes.

At the top of the rise before his post she put it down on the flat of the wall and he got his fingers under it and got it up with one arm, and he stood there with it against his chest.

“You did not have to do that,” he said.

“No.”

“I am not going to tell you I am grateful,” he said. “I am going to tell you what it was, and it was the floor of a room that was hot, and I have been in buildings for forty years and that has not happened in one before, and I am going to go home and put water on it and I am not going to think about it again.”

That was the whole of it. He went up the ladder with the roll of his board under his one arm, and there was a woman standing in the door of the building at the foot of it who said one word about his hand and was told something short and came out and took the roll off him.

## What She Carried Back Down The Road

She came back down the mill road at the ninth hour with nothing in her hands and got to the shed at the eleventh and put a lump out of the box in at the low end of the ring with her own hand.

It came up a finger’s width and stopped.

She did it six times over an hour and a half, and it came up a finger’s width and stopped every one of the six, and it was the same fire it had been at the fourth hour that morning.

She lay down on the boards four feet off it and put her hands out in front of her and they were cold. The ring was cold at the back and cold at the mouth, and the shed was the same cold shed it had been for a month, on a day when a man had been standing in a room with her in it for a minute and a half.

Six months of this, and every true thing she had said had gone into a book, and the first true thing she had said since a strip went east in a cart on the first of a month had gone into no book at all, because the only person who had heard it was a man with a burn on his hand who had a mile and a half of road to walk.

That was the bill for a month, and it was on a man she had owed nothing to and had not asked, and she had watched his hand come up in blisters for three seconds and had said the word yes out loud in a room with nine men in it and had not said the four words before it.
