# The Floor Above The Counter

She spent her question on the third of a month, at the second hour, at the bottom of a stair, and she had been carrying it for eleven days and it came down to eleven words.

The stair goes up one flight from the counting house and the man with the brush is on the top step of it with a sheet against his knee, and there are two sheets squared up on the step behind him and the brush is worn to a stub at the ferrule.

He looked at her for about two seconds and then he put the sheet down on the step, which he had not done before.

“You have had that question since the tenth,” he said.

“Yes.”

“Then ask it.”

## The Question

“Is there anybody on the floor above yours.”

He did not answer that straight away. He turned the brush over in his hand and did not use it, the way he had not used it the two times before she had brought a strip up that road.

“There is a floor above this one,” he said. “It is one more flight and it is about nine steps shorter than this one and it is cold at the north end all year.”

He put the brush down on the step.

“There is a person on it. I have been on this floor eleven years and I have been up those nine steps twice, both times with a tray, and both times about eleven years ago, and I do not know what her name is and I would not tell you if I did.”

## What He Said About It

He got up off the step and came down three, the way he had come down to a strip three times in six months, and he stood on the landing with his back against the wall and his arms folded with the sheet under one arm.

“You have come up this road four times in six months and asked me to read a hand I cannot read,” he said. “You have not asked me that today. You have asked me a different thing, and I am going to answer it, and then I am going to go back up those stairs and work, because I have four things to do before the hour turns.”

“All right.”

“There is no way in,” he said. “There is a stair. That is the whole of what there is between this floor and that one. There is no man at the top of it with a list and there is no book you sign and there is no office you go into and tell. You go up nine steps and there is a room.”

He looked at her.

“You have been standing at the bottom of that stair for six months looking at the top of it,” he said. “That is what everybody who has ever come up this road does. You go up.”

## The Two Words He Said About Her

He went back up two steps and stopped.

“I am going to tell you one thing and then I am going to work,” he said. “On that floor there is more open book in one day than has come into this building in the four years I have been on it. Not more lines. More book. Pages open at once, on a table, all of them, so that a thing can be set against a thing while both of them are in front of you.”

He put the sheet down on the top step and squared it against the other two.

“You can read a column,” he said. “You have been able to read a column since you were twelve years old in a county that no longer exists, and there is nobody on this ground who can do it and get paid for it, and I told you on the twenty-eighth of a month that reading is not a trade.”

“I remember what you told me.”

“I know you do,” he said. “I am not saying it again. I am saying that there is a room up those nine steps where reading is the whole of what the room is for.”

## What She Did With That

She stood at the bottom of that stair with the stove at her back and nine men at their cups in the room behind her for four minutes.

Then she went down the mill road and out to the gate and got a fifth of a reach at three and a half feet and it came up easy again, and she put the clay in a barrow and carried it out and put it against the wall with the other five, and she had never in her life been so certain that she was going to do a thing.

The ground on the low side had gone soft since the twentieth of a month. It came up at three and a half feet in two hours and the spoil did not want the bar, and the bar stood against the fence where she had left it after the twenty-second.

You did not ask him anything he could give you. You asked him where the room is and he told you, and he did not tell you what is in it and he is not going to.

## The Board, The Fourth Of A Month

She read the works board from the bottom up on the fourth at the tenth hour and there were eleven lines on it and one of them had changed.

A ditch line, at the north end, by the length, two weeks.

It had not been on that board eleven days ago and it was on it now, in the same hand as the other ten, in the same column, at the bottom, in the same size of writing, and it was the first line she had seen come onto that board in seven months that had a person’s arm behind it instead of a bar and a chain and a cart.

She stood in that gateway and read it four times.

A ditch is measured by the length. A ditch at the north end by the length is two weeks for four men and it is the one piece of work on that board that does not need dressed stone to be put anywhere, because a man can stand in a ditch with a bar and take it out by the running foot and pile it on the bank.

That is a thing she could do. That is the whole of what she could do, standing on it.

And there were nine men in that yard four miles east and a man with a rule in his hand who had known that about her at the second hour on the second day she had ever stood in that bay, and who had said he was not going to put her on a board and was not going to ask her twice, and who had been right about that.

## The Plain Part About One Person

She is eighteen years old and she has found out in four minutes standing at the bottom of a stair that the only thing anybody on nine days of road is ever going to want from her, and that no office on nine days of road has ever offered it, is the thing she has done since she was twelve without being asked to do it.

There is one man at the works who reads a board out loud in a gateway every second day and gets nothing for it, and has told her the reading is not a trade, and he is right.

There is one man on a copying floor who copies down and has never in eleven years copied a thing upward out of the room he is in. There is a man with a rule in his hand four miles east who priced her arm in two seconds.

And there is a room nine steps above a stair with more open book in it in a day than the whole of that building has seen in four years, and a woman on that floor who has been there longer than the man on the stair below her.

What she wants is to stand in front of that book. She cannot read one word of it, and she has been able to read a column since she was twelve, and there has never once been a room on nine days of road with the book open in it.

She had spent eleven days walking about with a question in her and she had been carrying it to a man with a brush, and the man with a brush had taken it off her and read it back to her in four words and gone back up his stair to work.

## Why She Did Not Go Up That Morning

The low side of that gate had gone soft since the twentieth of a month and she had been getting the cut up easy at three and a half feet, and on the fourth she put the bar in it for the first time in about three weeks to see whether the ground would hold a bar at that depth.

It would not. The bar went in about two feet and then the ground closed on it and she had to work it back out with her left hand and her right arm open the whole time, and there was about nine inches of a hole left in the low side of a cut that is four feet long.

She filled it in with the flat of the iron and stood looking at it.

She had told no person at all that she had moved the depth. A man at a gate has one thing he is allowed to say and she was not going to spend his.

She went back up the mill road at the fourth hour in the afternoon and stood in the doorway of the counting house for ten minutes and looked at the stair.

There was a man on the top step with three sheets squared up beside him and the stove going behind her and nine men in the room, and there was one more flight above that one and about nine steps in it.

She went up the first flight and stood on the top step behind him and looked at the second flight, and he did not turn round, and she stood there for about three minutes.

Then she went down and out and down the mill road in the light, because she had worked out standing on that step that the second time she went up that second flight she would have to have a reason, and she had not got one, and a woman who walks up a second flight with nothing in her hands in a building with nine men in it gets read.

She would go up it with something in her hands.

She had not worked out what yet. She had until the sixth.
