# Five Days And One Hour

The ninth of a month beginning 1,034 was the day the tray came back down the mill road at the sixth hour, and she was a hundred yards short of the counting house when it came, the way she has been about four times in seven months, and it went past her at the speed a loaded tray goes past and there was a cord round it.

She went up the road to the gate of the counting house at the seventh hour and the man with a brush had the tray on the bench and was copying the sheet on the top of it down into the book under it.

He did not stop and he did not look up and he said, without stopping:

“There is a new line on it.”

“All right.”

“It is in a hand I have not seen before on this floor and it is in chalk and it is four words and it is not about the yard.”

She stood at the end of that table for a minute and a quarter while he copied it, and it went into the book under it in the ordinary way, and the book under it goes up on the ninth with the next one and it came back down on the eighteenth and she would be on that floor at the fourth hour on the eighteenth and could not do one thing about it.

## The Ninth, And The Four Words

The ninth is the day the tray comes down the mill road and the man with a brush turns it over on the floor below and copies what is on the top of it down into the book under it.

The yard page was on the top of it. It had four words in chalk in a yard-book hand on the margin of it, and a date against them in an ordinary hand on a slip of paper, and the man with a brush read both of them and copied both of them and did it without stopping and without asking anybody anything.

That is the whole of the trade of that floor. A thing goes up a road and comes back down and gets copied into a book, and the man who copies it is right about every single part of it.

He did not tell her. She found it out on the eighteenth, on the top floor, from a woman of forty-five who turned round at the eighth hour and said that a slip of paper with a date on it had been asked for at the top of that stair and she had said yes.

That is the second time in seven months that something of hers has been carried into a room by another person and put in front of a person with authority, and both times she was in a different room.

## The Tenth

The tenth of a month was the day the hedge came off the board.

Two hundred yards by the length, east of the turn, second listing of the same ground, put in on the twenty-ninth of a month with the thirtieth against it, and it had gone four days past and she had cut the last of it on the ninth.

He and two of his men walked it. It takes about fifty minutes and he did it with a stick and a line and he did not hurry any part of it and she stood at the end of the rail at the works while they did it, and then he came back up the road to the rail and rubbed it off with his own thumb.

It had been the first line off that board in eleven days.

He came and stood where he stands.

“Two hundred yards,” he said. “You have cut the first listing of that ground and the second and a cart track nine hundred feet that was never on a board because the cart track was put in on the second and I had the ground go on me before the cart came off it.”

“Yes.”

“That is eleven days of the ground going to that man who drove it, and I have never worked that out on paper in my life and I am not going to start now, because if I write the second one down I will have to write the first one too and there are a great many things on that board I would have to write down.”

## The Share

He did not go up the road.

“There is a thing you do not know about and you have been at that yard for two months and you have earned it twice without knowing there was any such thing,” he said. “Every line that comes off that rail pays a share to whoever was on the ground when it closed, and it is not money and it does not go into a purse. It is time. It is off the woman who was standing on the bed.”

“I do not know what I do with it.”

“You do not do anything with it,” he said. “You spend it. Out of yourself. And it does not come back, and I have watched two people on that ground find that out the hard way and I am not going to soften it for you.”

He put his hands behind his back.

“This one is five days and one hour.”

## Why He Said It Twice

He said it a second time at the rail about forty minutes later, with four men standing there, and he said it the same way and she asked him why.

“Because the last woman on this ground who had one of those was told about it once by a man who was in a hurry, and she tried to argue that it was four days and not five,” he said, “and she was wrong, and she went into the next season a day short, and I watched her do it.”

He put the chalk in his pocket.

“That is the whole of the reason. Nothing else. I am not doing you a kindness and I am not warning you. I have got a board and a rail and two men and I do not have a way of making sure a person has heard a number.”

## The Plain Part About One Person

She is eighteen years old and she has spent seven months being unable to be paid for anything and she has just been told that she has five days and one hour of something that is not money and is not wages and is not a line and is not a column, and that the only thing it can be used for is to be spent, and that it is paid to whoever was standing on the bed when the line closed and not to whoever cut it.

She cut it.

And she has been on that ground every day since the fifth of a month, and the man with a rule in his hand has known that, and he said it anyway, and he did not take it out of her hands and he did not leave anything in them.

## The Second Hour

She lay on the boards at the second hour of the tenth with the bar under her right hand.

“I have five days and one hour and I am going to spend it on nothing,” she said out loud, “and I am saying that twice because a man at a rail said it to me twice and I am not going to be the woman who got it wrong.”

She lay there.

“Five days and one hour out of me. And it does not come back.”

She lay there for about a minute.

“And I am going to say what it is going to cost before I spend it, because he told me the price before he told me the number and I have been watching men do that for seven months and I have learned it off all of them.”

## What She Said The Price Was

She did not know.

That is the honest part of it and she said it out loud in an empty shed at the second hour of the tenth of a month, and it is the first time in seven months she has said a sentence at the second hour and not been able to finish it.

“I do not know what it costs. Nothing in this ground has ever told me what anything costs except the things with a price on them and this one has not got a price on it.”

She lay there.

“So I am going to spend it, and it is going to take something, and I am going to find out what by having less, and that is the only way this works.”

She went west at the fifth hour on the tenth of a month and came back at the eleventh with four loaves and nothing in the fold.

## What It Took

It came out of her hands on the eleventh of a month at the seventh hour.

She was at the yard at the turn at the low end of the sill course with nothing in her hands and she put the reach there, on purpose, at a barrow wheel that had been standing four inches off true since the second of a month, because she wanted to know whether it was still there.

It did not go.

Nothing happened at all. She stood at the low side of that yard with her hand out and the barrow wheel four inches off true and nothing happened, and she did it nine times over two hours and got the same answer nine times, and four men on the north end worked within thirty feet of her and one of them asked her whether she wanted the bar.

And on the twelfth she tried it under a plank on the loading end and on the fourteenth she tried it against a post at the gate, and on the eighteenth she tried it on a dressed stone at the low side of that yard, which is the thing she did at the eighth hour of the seventeenth of a month two months back when a man’s leg was under a stone, and nothing happened there either.

Twelve days.

It came back on the twenty-third of a month at the fourth hour, on the third floor, with her left hand flat on the edge of a table with forty books open on it, and it went four feet and stopped, and a woman of forty-five turned round and said, without looking up:

“Wood is cold.”

And she put her hand on the next book and turned the page.

She had five days and one hour and it was the twenty-third of a month and it was gone.

It had bought her a barrow wheel four inches off true, a dressed stone with a man’s leg under it, forty books open on a table, and twelve days of standing in rooms with her hand out and nothing coming up out of the ground. It came back once in all that, and it came back because a woman of forty-five said a thing about wood.

She was going to be at that gate at the seventh hour in the morning.

She had no idea whether she would be at it in the spring.
