# Eleven Years Of Copying Down

She went up the mill road at the second hour on the twenty-eighth with the strip open on her forearm, because a month is thirty days and she had one question a month in her and she had been carrying it since the twenty-fifth.

The counting house was a low building with a counter and a stove and about nine men in it, and the stair at the back went up one flight, and the man with the brush was on the top step with a sheet against his knee and his sleeves turned up.

He looked down at the strip and then at her.

“That is not the one you had last month.”

“No,” she said. “This one is three days old.”

## The Stair

She stood at the bottom of it with the stove behind her and the strip held out flat on her forearm with four fingers and a thumb on the bottom edge, and she had held a piece of paper like that in front of that stair twice in six months and had got an answer both times that she had not wanted.

“Read me the four lines in the middle.”

He came down three steps.

“I cannot read that hand,” he said.

“You have had it in that building three times.”

“It has been in that building three times and I have not been able to read one word of it any of the three times, and I have never been able to read it, and I am not going to learn it in a month because you have come up a road with it in your hand.”

That was the whole of it and he was right, and she had known it was going to be that, and she had come up the road anyway.

## What He Could Do

He came down two more steps and put his hand flat on the sheet that was against his knee, which had two columns on it and a margin, and did not touch her strip at all.

“I can copy a column,” he said. “I have been copying books in this building eleven years and I take what comes up that stair and I write it out again in a hand anybody can read, and what I write goes down a cart at the end of the month and gets read against what came down the same month. That is the whole of this floor.”

He turned the brush over in his hand and did not use it.

“What is in the middle of that strip, I do not know. What I know is what is at the top of it, and the top of it I have set against another book three times since the first of this month’s book came down, and it has gone up and come down twice.”

“Then somebody read it.”

“Somebody read it on the way past,” he said. “That is not me and that is not the counter and that is not anybody in this building, because there is not a man in this building who can read that hand, and I have been on this floor eleven years and I have looked.”

## The Ordinary Course

She stood at the bottom of that stair and worked out the whole of what he had said and it came out the same shape as everything else anybody on nine days of road had told her since she came past the mill.

The hand could read what happened. The hand could not be read by anybody in any building she had stood in for six months. And a thing in a hand she cannot read gets written down by somebody, and somebody carries it, and it goes up a stair and it goes down a road and it gets set against another thing and the answer to that does not come back down the road.

That was the ordinary course of it.

She had said no at a counter a month ago in front of about nine men, and a man with a pen had written it into the only book on nine days of road that shuts, and she had understood at the time that from that hour the country past the line had to deal with her where she stood or not at all.

She had understood that and she had not understood what it was going to be.

It was going to be a thing that happened in a room she was not in.

## The Counter

He went back up the steps and she went and stood at the counter instead, because the man behind it was the one who had asked her the question in the first place and she had one question and she had brought it up a road for it.

He was doing the ordinary work of a man who keeps a book that shuts, and the book was shut, which it had not been for two months.

“You have not come up this month,” he said.

“I have. I have been up there for about ten minutes.”

He looked at the top step and then at her.

“Then you have asked your question up there and it did not do you any good, and you have come down here to ask me the same one, because I am the one who wrote it down.”

She put the strip on the counter with the fine half turned toward him.

“Read that.”

He did not touch it. He looked at the ordinary half for about as long as it takes to put a date on a page, and then he looked at the fine half, and then he took his eyes off it and looked at her.

“I cannot read it,” he said. “I did not write it and I cannot read it and it is not going to come back down that road in my hand either, because it goes up on the first at the sixth hour with whatever is on the stair and it comes down when it comes down.”

## What The End Of A Month Is

He put both hands flat on the counter on either side of the strip.

“I will tell you what the end of a month is in this building, because you have not been up here for one and you have a strip in your hand as though you had.”

He said it the way a man says the price of a thing he has been selling for a long time.

“Everything that has come into this room in thirty days goes up the stair and onto a tray and up the mill road at the sixth hour tomorrow morning. Every column, every account, every refusal, every line with a name at the left of it and a day against it, including the one you gave me here a fortnight ago. It goes up with a cord round it whether it shuts or not.”

“And then.”

“And then it is read,” he said. “That is the part that is not mine. I keep the book and I write down what people say in it and I tie the cord and I put it on the cart. What happens to it after the cart turns at the elms is not in this building and I do not ask, because a man behind a counter who asks what is done with a tray after it has gone has found out that it matters to him.”

She stood there with the counter between them and understood the whole of it standing up, in about a minute, the way she understood everything on that ground: by the shape of it.

The strip against her ribs was not a letter. It was not an order and it was not a request and it was not a person.

It was a line in a column, and the column was going up the mill road at the sixth hour tomorrow morning with a cord round it, and she could not read the part of it that had been written about her.

## What She Said

“Tomorrow is the thirtieth,” she said.

“No,” he said. “It is the twenty-eighth and it will be the twenty-ninth and it will be the thirtieth, and the cart goes on the first. I have told you the sixth hour because you will stand in that yard and watch it go and I would rather you knew the hour than watched it out of a guess.”

She picked the strip up off the counter and folded it in two and put it back against the oilcloth.

She had one question a month in her and she had spent it, and it had gone on the same thing it had gone on the two previous months before it, which was that she wanted a person in a room.

She went out and down the mill road in the light with nothing against her ribs that she could read, and behind her at the top of a stair a man with a brush was going to write a sheet out again in a hand anybody could read and put a cord round a tray and set it on a cart at the sixth hour in the morning.
