# A Light That Did Not Stop

The bundle went down the stair on the twentieth of the month beginning 2,024 at the sixth hour and the boy of sixteen carried it, four flights, with a strap across his chest, and brought it back up empty, because that is what the stair does and that is what he was taught by a man of sixty-seven who is not going to teach him anything else.

She stood at the third landing and let him go down past her and she did not offer to take it and he did not ask her to.

He came back up at the twenty-fifth minute with it gone and said, “That is a quarter’s bundle. There are four hundred volumes on those shelves and a working pile on the bench and four sheets on the rack and the bundle goes up on a clerk’s arm.”

She said, “I know what a bundle is.”

He said, “I have never carried one and I have been in this room two years.”

She said, “You carried it at the sixth hour.”

He said, “I carried it down. Coming up is different and he told me that either and I have worked it out from watching him.”

## Down Four Flights At The Fourth Hour

She went out of the tower at the fourth hour of the twenty-first of the month beginning 2,024 to walk to the square and buy bread, because there is nothing else to do in a town at the fourth hour and because the four flights had got into her legs again.

That is why she was not in the building. She has been asked in her own head, in four minutes, more than once, whether she would have been on the stair, or on the floor, or at the end of the bench.

The answer is no, and it is no by two hours, and it would have been no at any hour of that day because a man of sixty-seven had told her at the fourth hour of the eighteenth that he was going to be finished in a fortnight and had told her to go down four flights and come back up in an hour, and she had done it, and she was going to keep doing it for as long as there was a bundle going down that stair.

She was on the west side of the square at the fifth hour when the boy came.

## What The Boy Said On A Paving Stone

He said it in the ordinary voice, on a paving stone, with his hand on her arm because he had never touched her before and did not know that he was not supposed to.

He said, “He has stopped.”

She said, “Say all of it.”

He said, “He coughed and then he was on the floor by the working rack and I got the cloth and I could not lift him and I went down and got a carter and the carter has gone for a physician and I came up to find you because I did not know who else to find.”

She said, “How long have you been standing here.”

He said, “Eight minutes.”

She said, “You stood on a paving stone for eight minutes.”

He said, “I did not know what else to do with it.”

She said, “No. That is correct and you did it right.”

And she walked four miles up a mountain with a bar on her shoulder and four counts of breath coming in at the top of every one of them, and she did not run, because there is not one thing a girl of nineteen that a physician can do anything with, and she got to the tower at the sixth hour.

## The Hand On The Boards

He was on the floor by the working rack with the cloth under his head and his hand flat on the boards about nine inches from the bench.

She did not look at his face for a minute. She looked at the hand.

It was the hand that had put the brush down square with the edge of the bench nine hundred times in fourteen months and a thousand times in five years, and it was not shut and it was not open and the air did not move in it, and it was the only thing in that room that was not ordinary.

The physician came at the seventh hour and was gone by the eighth. She stood at the end of the bench through both of them and did not say one word and was not asked one question, because a physician at a death in a tower does not examine the girl at the end of the bench.

He was sixty-seven. He had a cough that had got worse every year for nineteen years and a hundredweight of coal for six of them and a quarter of a bundle every three months for five years and a sheet in a locked drawer four feet away with seven sentences in a fair hand in it.

That is all of it. There is no account of what it was like at the end and she is not going to put one on a leaf, and she has not looked back at that hour once in the days since, and she is not going to.

## The Burial At The Back Of The Tower

They buried Feng at the back of the tower at the fourth hour of the twenty-second of the month beginning 2,024, in ten minutes, with a woman from the square and a carter and the boy of sixteen and her, and there were no words because there was nothing four people would have agreed about.

They put him in the ground at the back of a tower with a lamp in a cage on the fourth landing, three feet from the tower wall, in a hole that had been dug before she got there.

She took a handle.

That is all she did at the burial and she would not have called it anything and no one asked her to. Two men and a boy and a woman lifted and she took the foot end with her left hand under the board and her right hand over the top of it and her right arm was shut on that day and it was the second day of five and it held.

And she put him in the ground with her own hands on the board and that is all she ever had of him.

There was not one word said to her about it at the time. The woman from the square said one thing about the weather. The carter said the ground was harder than it looked.

## The Bench Behind The Working Rack

There are four hundred volumes on those shelves and a working pile on the bench and four sheets on the rack, and there is one more thing in that room and it has been there for five years and no one has ever come for it.

It is the fifth item, and it is walled behind four county copies at the back of a volume on the second shelf, and it is his own column, and it is unindexed, and it has five lines in it, and it went up with the quarter and it came back with the quarter, and not one person above this county has ever sent for it.

She knew about it from fourteen months of sitting at the other end of that bench and she had worked out in her fourth year there that it was the only thing in the building that was his and that no one could take off him, and she had been wrong about what it was, and she worked out that she had been wrong on it on the sixteenth of this month at the seventh hour with a lamp turned away.

A thing in a county column can be asked for and answered.

A thing walled behind four county copies at the back of a volume on a second shelf is a thing no one has to ask for, because it cannot be known to be there. It is kept by the fact that it is in a building. That is all it has ever had. Six years and a quarter of a bundle every three months and no one has ever read it.

She stood in front of the second shelf at the tenth hour of the twenty-second of the month beginning 2,024 with her hand on the spine of the volume and she did not take it out, because it is not hers and there is not one thing in ten years that has made her a person who takes a county’s paper off a county’s shelf.

And she said it, at the end of the ninth hour of the twenty-second, to the shelves and to a boy of sixteen at the rack:

“He has got four hundred volumes and he has got one line in the register of this building and it is his own, and I have been in this room two days and one of us is going to have to do something about it and it is going to be me, and I have no standing whatever and I am going to do it anyway.”

## The Appointment And The Light

At the ninth hour of the twenty-second she went up the four flights with the key in the front of her bag and let herself into a copying room with a lamp burning in a cage on the fourth landing, and she put the key in the lock of a drawer and turned it, and there were four sheets in it with a fair hand on them.

A keeper’s lamp burns while the keeper is in the building. He had said that to her once in the winter of her fourteenth year and had not been asked and had told her because she was asking him for the oil.

She put the cloth over the lamp and she did not put it out. She left it burning on the fourth landing with the keeper gone, and she sat down on the floor with her back against the end of the bench where she had sat for six days, and she said it, in the ordinary voice, at the ninth hour of the twenty-second of the month beginning 2,024, to an empty room:

“The appointment was his for five years and it reports in full and it does not expire, and there is no one in this county to enter the next one, and that is going to be somebody’s problem and it is not going to be mine.”

And then she said, “I am not going to put a sheet of paper on a bench in a room with a lamp in it and read it at the fourth hour, because he told me not to and he told me not to for a reason and the reason was that I have four days of a thing arriving in daylight.”

She was lighter at the tenth hour of the twenty-second of the month beginning 2,024 than she was at the seventh hour of the day before, which is four hundred and eighty-five days of it, counting the eighteenth of the month beginning 1,544 as the first, and not one of them going back.

And she sat on the floor of an empty copying room with four hundred volumes on the shelves behind her and four sheets of a dead man’s handwriting in a drawer she had unlocked and a lamp burning in a cage on the fourth landing above her head, and she did not open the drawer, and it was the hardest thing she has done in this life and it will not be in anybody’s column.
