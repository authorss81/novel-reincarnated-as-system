# The Only Thing That Holds

She was awake before the light on the twenty-ninth of the month beginning 1,814, sitting on the stones by the trough with the bar laid across her knees, and the first thing she did was the thing her body has done every morning for ten years, which is to go through what she has.

A bar nine feet nine. A skin. A bag with five things in it. A heel of loaf and one copper she cannot count. Two things against her ribs and she has called neither of them anything.

And a turn she stands on for the last time today.

## The Arithmetic Of A Morning

She did the sum out loud at the second hour, in the ordinary voice, to the trough and the bench and the woman of sixty who was not on it yet.

“The twenty-ninth. I stand here the eighteenth to the twenty-ninth and this is the twenty-ninth, so there is no tomorrow of it.”

“Tomorrow you are on the road,” he said.

“Tomorrow I am on the road,” she said. “The face is four miles west. To be paid off it I have to be at the face on the first of the next month, and the first is tomorrow, and I am not going to be four miles west tomorrow, because I am going to be out on the made road east and further.”

She said the number and the two numbers after it, standing, with the cold coming off the stones.

“One board. That is what leaving the turn costs me. One copper does not buy a third of a loaf.”

He said, “That is the price and you have known it since the twenty-sixth.”

“I have known it since the twenty-sixth and I did not work it out until this morning, which is late, and I am going anyway, and I am saying that out loud so that there are two of us holding it.”

## The Sixth Hour

She carried the bar a hundred yards off the road into the broken ground on the north side and put it down flat, because that is where she has done everything that mattered since the eighteenth, and stood in it with her boots in the cold and waited for the hour the way a person waits for a bus.

It came at the sixth hour and forty minutes.

The little open place low behind her fourth rib, the size of a coin, went quiet the way it has gone quiet nine times before, and then it did not go quiet, and there was no sound in it, because there is no one inside to be told and the sound goes into the ground.

Ten seconds.

And after it she was exactly as tired as she had been before it and exactly as hungry as she had been before it, which she was not at all, and what it took came out of the ground under her feet and not out of her hands.

She said the number out loud standing over the bar.

“Two hundred and eighty-two.”

She said it again.

“Two hundred and eighty-two.”

Both times it came. It comes every day, it has come every day since the eighteenth of a month beginning 1,544, and not one of them going back, and she got it twice standing in cold ground with a bar at her feet.

And then she stood there and could not say what she had.

## What The Sixth Stage Took

She asked him at the seventh hour, in daylight, in the broken ground, out loud, one question, and she chose it because it was the smallest one left in the drawer.

“What was the last thing you counted.”

He said, “A course of the low wall on the third day of a span I do not name. Sixty-four, and there was a chipped one at the end I put to the same figure because it was half of one, and I have never checked it, and it is probably wrong by half a brick.”

She heard every word of it.

She understood every word of it, and it was true, and she could have said it back to him word for word, and it was the most ordinary thing anybody had ever handed her.

Then she put it somewhere.

That is what she does. A thing she wants, she says out loud so it is in the air, and then she says it again on a kerb or at a trough or in the dust at the foot of a post, because a number in the air and a number in the dust are two places and two places is more than one.

So she said it out loud to the broken ground.

“The low wall on the third day of a span he does not name. Sixty-four. Half a brick at the end.”

Then she waited, the way she waits, and nothing came up behind her teeth.

She said it again, slower.

“The low wall. Sixty-four. Half a brick at the end.”

And it was still a sentence. She could produce the words exactly. There was a place a number goes into and there was not a place for this, and she had gone looking for it three times, standing, in the cold, with a bar on the ground at her feet.

He said nothing at all for a minute and a half, which she counted.

## What It Took Instead

She said it out loud then, in the ordinary voice, because she says things out loud and because there were nine miles of road in four directions and she had checked.

“I can be told anything about you and none of it stays with me.”

“Yes.”

“Not my arm. Not my hand. This.”

“Yes.”

She put her hand flat on the bar.

“A voice counts things and a book writes things. Those are the two ways a thing has ever got into me and I have ten years of both and they have never once failed. And a sentence about a boy is not counted and it is not written, so it goes straight through me and out, and I can watch it go.”

He said, “That is the seventh and it is about knowing and not about a body, and you have just spent six days getting it.”

“I have spent six days and one night asking you what you were and this is what the asking bought.”

“No,” he said. “The asking bought the terms. This is what the stage cost. They are two different bills and you have been carrying them in one pocket for six days.”

## The Only Place Left

She got the barrel of it out standing up, and then she sat down on the bar, which is a thing she does not do, and she worked the rest of it out with her hands still on it.

“Then there is one way left and it is the oldest one there is and I have been refusing to look at it.”

He said, “Say it.”

“My hand.”

She said it out loud, to the ground.

“My hand writes and it keeps. That is what a hand is. Four fingers and a thumb and it writes what I mean, and whatever I write in a book of mine is in two places at once, mine and the book’s, and I have never in my life written one of those down and lost it.”

He said, “Yes.”

“And a told thing cannot be written, because it is not in me when the nib goes down. It is out on the air, and I cannot get it back off the air and down onto a leaf.”

He said, “That is correct, and you worked it out standing up, and it took you four minutes, and I have watched a great many people take a great deal longer.”

## Six Blank Leaves

She got the bag open and got two fingers in and felt the edge of the blanket book where it lies against her ribs on the side where the paper is, and took it out and held it in both hands and did not open it.

Eight leaves made out of a blanket. A date and a place on the first. Four sentences on the second she has read out loud once and will not read again. Six that are hers and blank and have been hers and blank for four months.

She said, “If I am going to write one, I am going to have to have it said and write it in the same motion, and it is going to be one hand and a stub of a nib and ink I have not opened in two months, and I do not get to hear it twice.”

He said, “You do not get to hear it twice.”

“I do not get to hear it twice,” she said, “because the first time is the only time it is anywhere.”

She put the book back against her ribs on the side where the paper is, and the two things lay side by side there and neither of them moved.

## The Ninth Hour

She picked the bar up with two hands, put it on her left shoulder, and walked the hundred yards back to the road, and she stopped at the post four hundred yards east because it is four hundred yards east and she was going that way and there is a habit in walking past a thing.

There was nothing on the board.

The chalk on the rail had gone soft at the edges and the board had been bare since a load of stone left at the fourth hour of a month beginning 1,754, and on the twenty-ninth of a month beginning 1,814 she looked at it for the last time as a person who lived on this side of it.

She did not say good to the woman of sixty, who was on the bench with her hands in her lap and had not looked up since the twenty-seventh of the month beginning 1,814.

She did not say thank you to the voice in her head, and she has not said it once in ten years, and there is not going to be a first time on a day she has just lost a board.

## What She Took East

She walked out past the trough and off the flat and onto the made road with the bar on her shoulder and the last of the light going, and on the first mile she worked out that she had been thinking about this the wrong way round for six days.

She had been thinking she was going east to be told something.

She is going east because there is a furnace out there that nobody has walked her to, and because a man with hands burned into the creases said six sentences about a boy who weighed lime and wanted nothing for them, and because she has asked one question out loud in the dark and it cannot be taken back.

And she worked out on the second mile, in the ordinary voice, to an empty road, that she now knows what she is walking into, and it is worse than not knowing.

Everything out there is going to be said to her.

And not one word of it will stay.

He said, “That is the first time in eight years you have known something about me in advance and been worse off for it.”

She said, “Say it again and see if I have it.”

He said, “That is the first time in eight years you have known something about me in advance and been worse off for it.”

She waited.

The words were there. She could have said them back word for word.

There was nowhere to put them, and there was a bar on her shoulder and one copper she cannot count and a stretch of made road going east with nothing on it, and she walked it, and she went on walking it, and she has a place to walk to and she cannot keep a single thing she will be told when she gets there.
