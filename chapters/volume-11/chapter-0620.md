# A Fire She Cannot Put Up

She came onto the furnace ground on the fourteenth of the month beginning 1,844 and she was on it for four days before she went and stood at the furnace, and that is the shape of it and she has not been able to shorten it since.

The ground is two acres inside a knee-high line of stone. A row of barrows nose up against the inside of it. A low building with one door and a long window along the front of it, and a furnace at the east end of the yard with a smoke coming off it that goes straight up and does not lean in any direction at all, on any day, because there has not been a wind on that ground since the winter before she came.

## Four Days Of Not Going In

She stood at the gate of the yard at the fifth hour on the fourteenth, the fifteenth, the sixteenth and the seventeenth, and she did not go in, and she got the same thing every morning.

Nothing happened.

That is what a ground that does not want you does when it is properly run. Not one of them came out. Not one of them turned her off. Not one of them sent a boy to ask her business. The gate stood open the whole of all four days and ten men and two women and a boy of fifteen went in and out of it around her, and they went around her, which is a thing a body does without deciding to.

She read them at eight feet each morning because she reads hands, and the hands said the same thing every morning of the four.

There are no burns in the creases of a man who has not been near a fire. All ten of them had them. Nine years old and deeper, in two of them. Fingers held a little apart.

She said, at the sixth hour of the sixteenth, sitting on the stone outside the gate with the bar across her knees:

“They do not want me here and they are not doing anything about it.”

He said, “That is a different thing from the one you have been carrying for four days.”

“It is. What have I been carrying.”

He said, “You have been carrying the idea that a place which does not want you has to be fought. You have fought nine places in ten years. Every one of them was a person.”

She said, “This is a place.”

“It is a place with thirteen people in it.”

## What The Ground Is

She worked it out standing at the gate across four days, and this is what a furnace ground is, and she got it from the outside and it took her four days and one afternoon.

Stone comes in at the west side and is broken on the floor and goes into the kiln. Lime comes in off a cart, twice a season. A boy calls the figure out loud as it goes on, so the men in the low can hear it, and the man with the board writes it, and the board goes down to a store when the month turns.

And she stood at the gate for four days and heard a boy of fifteen call out weight after weight after weight in a voice like somebody reading out a row of fence posts.

She said, “He says it the way I say it.”

“He does it the way you do it,” he said, “and the reason you cannot stand him saying it is not the way.”

She said, “Then what is the way.”

He said, “You are listening to a person do the only thing you have ever been able to do, in a place where you cannot do anything, and it has been going on for four days.”

## The Fifteenth Of A Month Beginning 1,844

At the seventh hour of the fifteenth she went and asked for a firebrick and a shovelful of charcoal.

The man who gave them to her was fifty-one and had stone dust in the creases of both hands and did not ask her a single question, and he gave them to her, and she did not thank him, and she has not thanked anybody else on that ground either.

She built it in the north corner of the yard outside the windbreak of the wall, nine feet off the furnace, which is as close as a person goes.

She got a scrap of paper out of the bag and twisted it and put it in and blew on it for four minutes with her left hand on the brick.

It went out. She got it again. It went out. She got a third scrap and she built it up out of the charcoal and blew for the better part of a quarter of an hour and got a blue place in the middle of it the size of a coin, and she held her face over it for a minute and a half and it went out under her hands.

Her breath was at four and then it was at seven and it stayed at seven, and she was leaning over a fire with a bar lying six feet off her.

The boy of fifteen came across the yard, looked down at it, walked to the furnace and came back with both arms full of a different charcoal, which is the coarse kind with bark still in it. He put it on top of hers without a word and went and stood six feet off and watched.

It went up.

She said, “That is the whole of it and I have known it since I was six years old in a lime bed and I have never once watched anybody else find out.”

He said, “It is not a secret.”

“No,” she said. “People do not hand you the bark.”

## What Nineteen Years Looks Like

The ground has been kept for nineteen years and she can put a date on most of it from outside.

The barrows stand nose up against the inside of the wall in a line with four inches between each pair, and the four inches are the same all the way along, and one of them has been moved and put back and the dust around it is a different colour.

The stack of broken stone has a corner worn down and the corner is the same shape it was the day before.

The low building has two places on the long window where the glass was broken and were mended with a piece of a different colour in it.

That is what nineteen years looks like when there is no one watching it. It looks like four inches between the barrows, and a worn corner on a stack of stone, and two panes of glass in a different colour in a long window, and ten men who have all got the same thing in the creases of their hands.

She said, at the ninth hour of the seventeenth, to an empty yard, on the stones outside the gate:

“Nineteen years of an ordinary day. That is what it looks like from a gate.”

He said, “Yes.”

“And none of them thinks anything remarkable happened on it.”

“No,” he said. “And if you had come here nineteen years ago you would have found it the same and you would not have seen anything either.”

## The Twenty-Third

She went to the furnace at the fifth hour of the twenty-third of the month beginning 1,844, which is the ninth day she has been on that ground, and she stood at the mouth of it.

The mouth is a square hole in the end of a low brick stack the height of her chest, and there is a course going in, and there are two men feeding it, and the heat coming out of it comes out at a person and does not go up first.

She got her breath to seven inside forty seconds and it stayed at seven.

Her right arm went at the second minute. It is open four days in five and this is not one of its days, and it went anyway, because the heat takes it and there is no negotiation available, and it hung at her side and she did not use it.

She was at the mouth about five minutes.

She has worked out since that she got to the furnace mouth on the tenth day of a ground that has not had a stranger on it in nineteen years, and that she had not asked for it, and that the man at the gate had not turned her off, and that a boy of fifteen had put bark on her fire and stood six feet off and watched her fail at it.

And she has not softened one word of it. The ground did not want her and let her stay and was correct on both counts.

## The Fire Door

At the fifth minute she went down.

Not far. She went down on her hands and knees in the ash at the mouth and the bar was on the ground six feet off her and she could not get the arm that works to it.

Her breath went to nine, which she had not had since a month beginning 1,784 and a fire in a low building she was not supposed to be in.

And she counted.

That is the only instrument she has ever had. She counted her own breaths out loud, in the ash, at a furnace, on open ground, in the daylight, with two men and the whole of that ground within nine feet of her: nine, eight, seven, and she got to four and held it for a count of twenty before she let herself be helped.

The boy of fifteen took the strap of the bar and pulled it two feet and stopped, and did not pull her, and waited.

She said, at the sixth hour, sitting in the ash with her face and her hands grey:

“I am not going to be thanked for that.”

He said, “You did not ask me to.”

She said, “I am going to say it so that there are two of us holding it.”

He said, “You say everything out loud.”

She said, “Yes.”

He said, “I have noticed.”

## The Door Of The Low House

She was on her feet with the ash off her and the bar back on her shoulder by the sixth hour and twenty minutes, and she had not meant to stay and was not going to, because one copper she cannot count buys a heel of loaf and she had a fortnight of walking behind her.

And then she looked at the low building and the woman of forty-five was standing in the door with her arms down at her sides.

She had been standing there for the length of time it took a girl of nineteen to go down on her hands in the ash and count her way out of it and get the ash off herself, and Ning Yan went over that afterwards, twice, and did not find one second of it that had not been watched.

The woman did not say anything.

Ning Yan did not say anything either, and the yard went on around them the way a yard goes on, and the boy of fifteen called out a figure at the far end of it in the ordinary voice so the men in the low could hear it.

And the woman in the door did not go back in, which was the first thing that was different about that ground, and it was not much, and it was the whole of what she was going to get.
