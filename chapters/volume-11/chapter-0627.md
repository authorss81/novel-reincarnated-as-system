# He Does Not Have The Day

The board went down on the second of the month beginning 1,904 and it came back up the track at the seventh hour on a cart with a carter who did not get down and did not look at the yard, and there were eight coppers in a cloth on the table in the low beside it.

She put them in the front of the bag and she did not count them and she did not look at them after the second one, and she bought bread on that ground the same afternoon from a woman in a doorway who put a rate of two a loaf on her and said the number out loud when she took them.

That is a trade. There was not one thing in it that was not a trade.

## What She Was Owed, And In What Sequence

She worked it out on the twenty-eighth of the month beginning 1,874, sitting on the stone wall with the book open on her palm, and it is a short sum.

A person who has been in your head for eight years owes you one ordinary day of his life.

She asked for that on the twenty-eighth of the month beginning 1,814 at the seventh hour in broken ground a hundred yards north of a turn, and she got three things where she asked for one, and she took no fourth.

The second thing he owes is the day before that one.

And she has a name on a leaf and a date on a leaf and she has not got a morning, and a morning is the only part of this a person cannot get out of a name.

## What He Owes, Asked For

She asked it at the seventh hour of the twenty-ninth of the month beginning 1,874, standing in the yard at the north end by the stone wall where the windbreak is, out loud, in daylight, on open ground, with a furnace working two hundred yards behind her.

“What was the day before you started counting.”

One at a time.

In daylight.

Standing.

And then she waited, which is what she has been doing for four months on every question she has put to him, and which is a different thing from waiting.

## What He Gave Her

He gave her three, the way he does.

“There was a senior at that fire who had been at it nine years.”

He said, “Nine years at what.”

He said, “At a work on himself. I am not going to tell you what it is tonight and I have not been asked.”

She said, “I asked what the day before was.”

“You asked what the day before was and there is a senior standing in it,” he said, “and that is not the same question and I am not going to be accused of dodging later, so here is the whole of the dodging: the day before I started counting there was a senior at that fire and I was nineteen and it was a morning.”

She said, “That is three sentences and not one of them is the morning.”

“No,” he said. “It is not, and you are not getting the fourth one, and you knew that when you asked it, and I would have said no to you in any case and I would rather do it now than at the end of the month.”

She said, “Then say it in the terms.”

He said, “One at a time. In daylight. Standing. Yes.”

## Nineteen

She said, out loud, to the yard, at the seventh hour and ten minutes of the twenty-ninth of a month beginning 1,874, with her hands on the stone of the wall.

“You said you were nineteen and on the twenty-eighth of the month beginning 1,814 you said you were nineteen when you stopped counting them, and you told me those were two different sentences and I had to work out which one I believed.”

He said, “Yes.”

She said, “Which one.”

He said, “That is the second question and you have just asked it.”

She stood there with her hands on the wall for about forty seconds.

Then she said, “No. I am not taking it. One at a time.”

He said, “Correct.”

She said, “Say that again and see if I have it.”

He said, “Correct.”

She said, “Correct.”

## The Date

And then she said, out loud, in the ordinary voice, to the yard and to the furnace and to the wall:

“I am going to read you something.”

He said, “I have been in your head for eight years and there has not been one single thing you could read to me.”

She said, “There is now.”

She got the book out of her coat with her right hand, which was open, and got it open on the flat top of the stone wall with her left, and she could read three lines of her own hand in a slant of light at any distance, because she has been reading boards since she was twelve.

She said, “There is a date on the third leaf above the place and above your name. It is nineteen years back and it is that morning, and it came off a page in a low house in a good hand and it is dry.”

He said, “Say that again and see if I have it.”

She said, “There is a date on the third leaf above the place and above your name.”

He said, “I have it. I have never once been able to get at a page.”

She said it out loud, slowly, in the ordinary voice, once, in daylight, to a man in her head.

And then she waited.

## The Reading Out

She waited for about a minute and she counted it, because she counts.

And there was nothing on the other side of it.

Not a silence. Not a refusal. Not carefulness. He had not gone careful, which is what he does when he is about to be careful, and she had watched him do it in the dark on a turn on the ninth hour of the twenty-fourth of a month beginning 1,814 and she knows the sound of it.

There was a person on that ground in a month nineteen years ago at that hour, and she had read it off a page in a good hand, and it had gone through him and out exactly the way every true thing she had been told had gone through her since the sixth hour of the twenty-ninth of the month beginning 1,814.

She said, “Say that again and see if I have it.”

He said, “That is the first time in ten years you have said that to me.”

She said, “Say it anyway.”

He said, “I do not have the day. I have had it in a book on a table in a low house for nineteen years and I have never once had the day.”

She said, “You did not have the date of it on the twenty-eighth of the month beginning 1,814 and you have told me you lost it.”

He said, “Yes.”

She said, “And the woman in the low house has had it in ink since the days it happened.”

He said, “Yes.”

She said, “And the people who wrote it down were right to write it down.”

He said, “They were told to and they wrote it true, and that has never once been anybody’s fault, and that is not a thing I have been able to say out loud until this second.”

## What That Cost

She put the book back against her ribs on the side where the paper is and she stood at the wall with her hand over it.

Here is the whole of it and she has not got past it and she does not expect to.

For nineteen years a woman of forty-five has had the day in her own handwriting on a page in a good hand, and she has not opened it, and she did not know that anything in the world wanted it.

And a man who was in that room on that morning cannot be told the day by anybody at all, including out of a page, read aloud, in daylight, by a girl of nineteen standing at a wall.

The page held for her. It did not hold for him.

She said, at the eighth hour of the twenty-ninth of the month beginning 1,874, out loud, with her hand on the book through her coat:

“I have spent nine days finding out that a told thing does not arrive. And I have spent nine days finding out that a read thing does not arrive either, and I did not know that until ninety seconds ago standing at a wall.”

He said, “No.”

She said, “So there is nothing on this ground that puts anything into you.”

He said, “There is nothing in this world that puts anything into me. That is not new and it is not the seventh and it is not the second.”

She said, “Then I am talking to a wall and I have known that for eight years.”

He said, “You have known it since you were eleven.”

## What She Wants After That

She wanted it out loud, standing, in the yard, on the thirtieth of a month beginning 1,874 and then on the first of the month beginning 1,904, and she said it both times and it was the same sentence.

“I want to give him the morning.”

And she worked out the rest of it standing at the stack with the last of the light on the wall, and it is a short sum and it came out the same way twice.

She cannot give him anything. Not one person in this world can put anything into him and that is not the seventh and it is not the second and it is not anything that came this year.

So if she cannot give him the morning, then the morning has to come to him by the only road there is, which is not a road at all, and which is that it gets said out loud on a page by somebody and a voice counts it and a book writes it.

And that is not something she can do.

So there is exactly one place in the world where that morning is written down true, and it is on a table in a low house that was opened twice in nineteen years, and it was shut on the first of the month beginning 1,874, and the woman who keeps it is not going to open it again.

She said, at the ninth hour of the first of the month beginning 1,904, standing outside a gate with a bag with eight coppers in the front of it she cannot count and a loaf she bought with a number said out loud:

“I have to go and ask her for it and she has already said no once and told me exactly why, and she was right, and I have no better reason than the one I had last time.”

He said, “You have one better reason.”

She said, “Say it.”

He said, “You have a page in your own hand with a day on it that you read to me at a wall, and you know now that it does not arrive, and that is a better reason and it is hers and she will know it is hers.”
