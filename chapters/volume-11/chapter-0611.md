# The Best Day She Has Had

She spent the rest of the twenty-fifth standing at a turn with two mornings in front of her and no intention of using either of them, and on the twenty-sixth of the month beginning 1,814 she worked the face from the sixth hour to the ninth.

It is the best day she has had since she was sixteen years old and she cannot tell anybody how much of it she did, and she has tried three times since.

The lane leaves the store road at the turn and runs four miles west to the face. At forty feet into it there is a low house with a bar across the door, a standing stone in the yard, a barrow with a wheel off it, and a woman of fifty standing in the yard who said good to her and did not say one word else, and who was not thanked, and who has not been thanked since the month beginning 1,694.

## What The Face Is

It is measured by weight and not by hands and that has been true on nine days of road for longer than she has been on it.

A man with a chalk on a string chalks a board. Every barrow that goes on comes up the face and goes into the low, and the figure is said out loud as he writes it, so that the men at the low can hear it, and the board goes down to the store on the second of a month, and the store gives out what the board says.

She has worked this face on seven sets of days in two years. She has never been paid a great deal. She has never been paid late.

She worked from the sixth hour to the ninth on the twenty-sixth of the month beginning 1,814 with a bar nine feet nine and her right arm open on one of the three hours.

## What She Put Up

Here is the whole of it and it is a sentence.

She put up more stone in three hours on the twenty-sixth of the month beginning 1,814 than she has put up in any one day in two years.

The man with the chalk said every figure out loud as he wrote it. He did not once look at her arm. She has worked out over seven sets of days that he is not being kind, he is being exact, and a chalk and a string do not care what a woman is doing with her shoulder.

Her count went to seven on a rise and stayed at seven through every barrow she put up and did not come back to four once.

She did not stop. That is the thing she has gone over since. A woman on that face with four fingers and a thumb stops at the ninth hour most days because her arm goes and it does not come back inside the hour.

She did not stop at the ninth hour. She put up one more barrow at the ninth hour and the man with the chalk said the figure out loud and she did not hear it.

## What Came Out Of The Ground

The little open place low behind her fourth rib, the size of a coin, went quiet somewhere in the eighth hour and then did not.

There was no sound in it. There has never been any sound in it, because there is no one inside to be told, and the sound goes into the ground, and she has been through this nine times and knows what it is before it arrives.

It took ten seconds.

And after it she was exactly as tired as she had been before it and exactly as hungry as she had been before it, which she was not at all.

And what it took came out of the ground under her feet and not out of her hands, which is how it has always been, and she was standing on a stone face four miles west of a turn with a bar on her shoulder and stone going into a low by the ton and not one piece of it was in her hand.

He did not say anything.

She went over that for an hour afterwards. In eight years, through a stage, a shut book, a wall, a barrow ground and a gate, there has not been one occasion on which he has said one word about what it costs her to stand somewhere. She has had nine of these and he has watched nine of them and the figure of what is left of him is not a thing he says, and she has never once asked him for it and is not going to.

## What She Cannot Keep

Here is the cost and it is the whole cost.

The man with the chalk said the last figure out loud at the ninth hour. It was out in the air. He said it in the ordinary voice so the low could hear.

And at the ninth hour and twenty minutes, standing in the low with her bar on her shoulder and one hand on the rim of the barrow, she went to say it back to herself to find out whether she still had it.

She had eleven.

She said it out loud and got eleven.

She waited for a minute and said it out loud again and got eleven.

And then she said it out loud a third time and got twelve, and the man with the chalk turned his head an inch, and she said it a fourth time and got eleven, and that was the last time she said it that day because her hands had gone cold.

That is the cost. It is the sixth stage and it is a stage and not a wall and not a rule, and it is the plainest sentence she has ever had: she can be paid, and she cannot keep what she has done.

She will be able to say she worked. She will not be able to say how much.

She stood in the low at the tenth hour and went through it one more time and got eleven twice and thirteen once, and the number of barrows is the number of barrows, and there is a man with a chalk on a string who has it written down, and she is the only person on nine days of road who is worse at it than the man who does not care.

## What The Man With The Chalk Said Afterwards

He put the chalk on its string and said, at about the ninth hour and forty minutes, in the ordinary voice, the way he says every figure of every day of his life:

“You are worth a third of a man of nineteen and you did a man of nineteen’s day.”

She said, “That is the first time you have said one of those out loud.”

He said, “I have said one of those out loud to six people in two years and four of them were not grateful and one of them was not listening and the sixth is a girl who cannot count what she has done this morning.”

She said, “Then why now.”

He said, “Because you did it on the third of your right arm and you did not stop, and a chalk does not care, and I am a man with a chalk and I am allowed to say what the board says.”

And then he said the figure again, exactly, at the top of his voice, so that the men at the low would have it, and it was the same figure twice, and she had it, and she kept it for four seconds.

And then she had to say it out loud to find out whether she still had it, and she said it out loud, and got eleven.

## Four Miles East With The Arm Gone

She walked the four miles east at the tenth hour with the bar on her left shoulder and the right arm hanging, and it stayed gone the whole way, and she got to the turn at about the eleventh hour.

She is not a person who is disappointed about an arm. It has been there since the month beginning 1,724 and it has been gone more days than it has been there since, and she has never once been angry at it.

What she is upset about is that she was better on that face for three hours than she has been for two years and she cannot produce the number.

She had it once more at the turn, standing in the dark on the stones with the bar on the ground. Eleven.

Then she put the bar on her shoulder and went and sat on the kerb by the trough and did not try again.

## What It Is Worth And When

Two coppers a loaf at the store and the board goes down on the second of the month.

The second of the next month is six days off. She has one copper. She has a bar and three-quarters of a loaf with a piece out of it and a bag and a book with six leaves in it that are hers and blank.

So the best day she has had in two years is worth a figure she cannot hold, six days, and the use of an arm.

He said, at the eleventh hour of the twenty-sixth, “You worked a day past the line.”

She said, “I do not know what that means.”

He said, “Neither do I, and I am the one who pays it. That is the arrangement and it has been the arrangement since the eighth of a month ten years back and you have never once asked me the price of it.”

She said, “I am asking what it means, not the price.”

He said, “The distinction is the whole of what I have got, and you have just made it, and you made it in nine words, and I have been trying to make it to you for eight years.”

She said, “Say it once more, plainly, and I will carry it.”

He said, “No. You will carry it for a day. You do not carry anything. You find it and then it is gone and then you find it again, and there is a woman of sixty thirty feet off you who would tell you that exactly the same way if you asked her, and she has not needed me to say a word about it in four months.”

## What She Is Left Holding

Two mornings.

She worked it out on the kerb at the trough at the eleventh hour of the twenty-sixth with the bar across her knees and the loaf out.

Three mornings of daylight on the twenty-fifth of a month. She said out loud on the stones that she was not going to spend one of them tomorrow. That leaves the twenty-seventh and the twenty-eighth.

One at a time. In daylight. And a question she has not asked yet, about the oldest thing in the world, out loud, on a turn, where a carter can stop and look.

She was lighter at the eleventh hour of the twenty-sixth than she was at the sixth of the same day, which is two hundred and seventy-nine days of it, counting the eighteenth of the month beginning 1,544 as the first.

And the bar on two posts four hundred yards east of this turn has nothing on it, and there is a store five miles west where a woman behind a counter counts things better than she does, and there are two mornings left, and she does not know what she wants asked.
