# What The Ninth Took Away

She was at the west end of the yard at the ninth hour and fifty minutes of the fourth of the month beginning 1,994 with the bar of fire gone back on the stack and her hands empty, and the whole ground was in it: men off the floor, the boy of fifteen on the roof of the low with a piece of chalk, the man of fifty-one at the plank table with the board flat in front of him.

The man of fifty came in off the track with ash to the elbow, the woman of forty-five in her doorway with the book under her arm.

And the man of seventy-one on his bench at the north end with his hands on his knees.

She said, out loud, standing, in daylight, to forty feet of open yard: “Now.”

And the little open place low behind her fourth rib, the size of a coin, went quiet somewhere in the eighth minute of the tenth hour and then it did not.

There was no sound in it. There has never been any sound in it, and she has been through this nine times, and the sound goes into the ground.

It took ten seconds.

And after it she was exactly as tired as she had been before it and exactly as hungry as she had been before it, which she was not at all, and what it took came out from under her feet and not out of her hands.

She stood in the yard at the tenth hour and ten minutes on a furnace ground with her arms at her sides and waited for a sound that a thing going into the ground sometimes makes for other people, and there was not one, and there was not going to be.

The furnace went on. The smoke went straight up.

No one on that ground said a word, and the man of seventy-one on his bench did not move, and the man of fifty went on up the track with his bar over his shoulder.

And a girl of nineteen stood in the middle of a working yard at the tenth hour of the fourth of the month beginning 1,994 and had the ninth stage, and had made the sound go into the ground for the last time there would be one.

## The Line In A Fair Hand

The man of fifty-one came round the end of the plank table at the tenth hour and fifteen minutes with the brush in his hand and the board under his arm and he did not ask her a single question about it, and he put the brush in the flat of the bar of fire that was lying on the stack and picked up the cloth.

He wrote one line at the top of the board with the day against it and nothing under it, and he wrote it the way he wrote the figures.

Then he squared the board with the back of his hand and hung it on the nail, and he said one thing to her and went back to the columns.

He said, “That is the last one I will put up in a hand that is not yours, and I do not know that, and I am not going to find out.”

She said, “You do not know it.”

He said, “I do not know that either. Put your figures up. There is a day of it left.”

## One Question, Standing

She went down to the gate at the seventh hour of the fifth and stood in the grit with the furnace going over behind her and asked him one question, in daylight, standing, one at a time, the way she has kept it since the ninth hour of the twenty-fourth of the month beginning 1,814.

She said, “What does it take.”

He said, “Say it slower.”

She said, “The seventh took a telling. The eighth took a way of holding on. What does this one take, and I am asking it standing in daylight so that it is a question and not a thing I have worked out on my own and do not want to hear.”

He said, “You are going to work most of it out on your own in four days and I am going to tell you the half you cannot.”

She said, “Say the half.”

He said, “It does not take anything away from you. You have got everything you had this morning. That is what the last one does and it is the only one of the three that takes nothing and gives nothing and costs you the thing you were going to try at the end.”

## The Word She Named Once

She said it out loud at the gate at the ninth hour and twenty minutes of the fifth, in the ordinary voice, standing, to the two miles of flat and the wall and the bar on two posts with nothing written on it.

She said, “I cannot go back to not knowing.”

She said, “Not the book and not the brother and not the name. Knowing used to be a thing I could open a door with and go out of, and it is not a door any more, it is the floor, and I am going to be able to learn anything else there is and there is not one hour in the rest of my life where I can be a person who does not know what I know now.”

She said, “And un-knowing it is nobody’s work. Not yours and not mine and not a clerk’s and not a county’s. It is the one thing in ten years that there is no instrument for at all.”

He said, “That is the ninth.”

She said, “That is the ninth, and I am not going to say it again.”

And she did not say it again that day or the next or on any day after that, and she worked out over four days what it costs, and the four days are the rest of this.

## Five Days On The Floor

She worked on the sixth and the seventh and the eighth and the ninth and the tenth, five days, and she put up barrows at a fifth of a man’s rate off the kiln floor and the boy called every figure and the man of fifty-one wrote them up and hung the board where anybody could see it.

And on the sixth day at the ninth hour the woman of forty-five came out of her doorway with the book under her arm and told her a thing she already knew about her husband, in the ordinary voice, and it did not arrive.

That is all of it and it took a second and a half and she did not stop her.

She said, “Say that again.”

The woman of forty-five said it again, word for word, the same thing, and it did not arrive the second time either, and the woman of forty-five said, “I have said it twice now.”

She said, “You have.”

The woman of forty-five said, “Then that is twice and it is still a Tuesday and my husband is still nine years at a work on himself and it has not taken you anywhere, and I have known that since the month beginning 1,874 and I did not think it was worth saying.”

And the woman of forty-five went in and shut the door, and that was the exchange, and she was right on every part of it.

## What The Ninth Did To The Work

On the fifth day she took the long hold at the pit because the boy had the stack behind him and the man of fifty had gone out with the ash, and she went down over hot stone for seven holds and came up on the seventh.

She was on the stones outside the gate at the ninth hour with her arm out in front of her where it had been since the first hour and her back against the low wall, and the four counts of breath were coming in at the top of each one.

She said, out loud, to the stones: “It is not different. That is all I have got to report.”

He said, “You expected it to be different.”

She said, “I have been lighter every day since I was a child and I thought the last one would take the work out of me as well, because it is the last one, and that was a stupid thing to think at the sixth hour of a day and I am saying it out loud so that it is a thing I did.”

He said, “It is not stupid. It is what a person expects and you are allowed to be wrong about it once in ten years.”

She said, “It will cost me four days of work.”

He said, “Then it will cost you four days of work.”

## What She Tried On The Sixth Day

She tried it at the seventh hour of the sixth, sitting on the stones outside the gate with her book of eight leaves open on her knees, and she tried it properly, because she counts and because a thing you cannot do is a thing you can argue about.

She got out the third leaf and put her thumb over the four capital strokes and said the two syllables out loud to the grit and waited.

They came back word for word. Two syllables, the first of them short and the second one falling, exactly as they had come back eight times on a made road on a night when she had nothing else to spend the hours on.

And that was the test and it came out the same as the road.

She said, out loud, at the stones outside the gate at the seventh hour and twenty minutes of the sixth of the month beginning 1,994: “I can say the two syllables and I cannot make them into nothing. I can say them a thousand times and they will come back a thousand times, and there is not one hour of my life left in which that name is two sounds and a man at a fire who did not want it.”

She put the leaf back and tied the string on and she was lighter at the tenth hour of the sixth than she was at the fifth hour of it, which is four hundred and thirty-nine days of it, and not one of them going back.

And the thing she was carrying east out of that gate at the fourth hour of the seventh, on a body with an arm open four days in five, was not a stage and not a name and not a number.

It was that she could not put anything down.
