# What A Furnace Uses

The man came west along the store road at the third hour of the twenty-fourth of the month beginning 1,814 on foot with a strap over his shoulder and nothing on the end of it, and he did not walk past the turn, and he stopped at it.

She read his hands at eight feet, the way she has read hands since she was six.

The burns are not a smith’s and they are not a cook’s. They go in the creases and they stay in the creases, and the fingers are held a little apart the whole time, the way a hand is held by somebody who has been feeling heat all day and has stopped noticing that it is heat.

He had been feeling heat all day and he was forty-five and there was stone dust on the shoulder of his coat.

## How She Started It

She said good and he said good, and then she said the thing she had been carrying since the twenty-first, out loud, in the ordinary voice, because she asks things out loud.

“About the lime bed four miles west.”

He said, “What about it.”

“It stopped nine years back. There is a cottage with a shut door at the mouth of it and I have been to it twice and I did not open the door.”

He said, “It shut when the kiln behind it shut. There is nothing else in a lime bed.”

“Where did it go.”

He looked at her for a second.

“I did. For a year, and then not.”

## Why A Lime Bed Has A Boy In It

She asked the second one because it was the only second one there was and she was not going to go away and come back to it.

“How was it worked.”

“We took it out in barrows.”

She said, “Who weighed them.”

And he stopped being a man on a road for two seconds and became somebody else for the rest of the day, in the way a person does when a question from a stranger lands on the exact place they have kept shut for twenty years.

“The boy weighed them,” he said. “Before they went in.”

She said, “Who paid him for it.”

He said, “Nobody pays him. His father fires.”

## The Sentences He Said After That

She did not ask the next one. It came anyway, the way a thing comes out of a man who has been carrying it and has met somebody who counts.

“A boy who counts what nobody is paying him to count is a boy the furnace will use.”

He said it in the ordinary voice, looking down the store road, and it is not a complaint and it is not a warning and it is not a thing anybody at a fire has ever thought of as remarkable.

She said, “Use for what.”

“For the thing a furnace does with a person. You have not been to a fire.”

She said, “I have.”

He looked at her hands then and not at her face.

“Then you know it is not the same thing as keeping, that not one man at a fire thinks it is the same thing, and that the only people who ever found the difference out were the ones it was done to.”

She said, “All of them.”

“All of them.”

## The Burning On Her Own Hands

She put her right hand out and turned it over without deciding to, four fingers and a thumb and the palm of a girl who is nineteen and was six years old in a place where heat is not a thing anybody has to be told about.

It is the same hand. That is what she has not got past in four minutes.

Not the same marks, because her hand has not been in it for nine years. The same hand. A hand that holds its fingers apart because it has been feeling something hot for six hours and has forgotten to close. A hand that knows what a course of bricks weighs without being told and can tell you in the dark.

She put it away.

He said, “You are a furnace child.”

She said, “I was.”

He said, “Then you will not need me to tell you what the rest of it is.”

## What He Was Not Saying

She stood there and waited for the other half of it, because a man who says a thing like that at a turn is generally on his way to saying the one where somebody is to blame.

It did not come.

She said, “Were they cruel at your fire.”

He said, “No, and it happened anyway, and they were not cruel, and both of those are how it happened.”

She said, “Then who.”

He said, “Then nobody can tell you, and I have been twenty years at that fire and I could not name one man who did it on purpose and I could not name one man it happened to who would have had a different day.”

That is all he said on it. She believes every word of it and it did not make her feel one inch better.

## What He Asked Her

It came at the seventh hour, out of nowhere, and it is the only question anybody has ever asked her in ten years that was not put by her and not put for her.

He said, “What do you count.”

She said, “I do not understand the question.”

He said, “You count everything. You counted the stones in a wall on the first morning and you counted the lime ruts on the twenty-first and you counted a man’s barrows on the twenty-third. What do you count. Not what. What.”

She stood in a cart track with lime in the ruts and found out that she could not answer it in one go.

She said, “I count the thing out loud so the men can hear me do it.”

And then she stopped, because she had said it out loud, and there was a man standing four feet off her who had been twenty years at a fire.

He did not say anything. That is the whole of his reaction. A man of forty-five with his hands burned into the creases stood in a cart track on nine days of road and did not say one word.

She said, “The boy at your fire was ten.”

He said, “Ten.”

She said, “I do not know that either. I have said it because it is my brother’s age and it came out of my mouth, and it is not the same boy.”

He said, “I know it is not the same boy. I did not say it was.”

And he asked her not one thing about the boy and not one thing about the brother, and he looked at the shut door four miles off and then at the bar on her shoulder, and that is all of it.

## What She Asked Last

She asked it at the eighth hour, standing four feet off him in a cart track with lime in the ruts, flatly, and she was not ready for what it did.

“What was he called.”

“He had a name and nobody at that fire used it.”

She said, “Why not.”

He said, “Ask anybody on that floor and they will tell you they do not remember it.”

And that is the whole of his answer. A thing the fire has hold of does not get its name said on the floor of it, and there is no rule and no rule-maker, and the men who would have known it are all the ones who are gone or who will not say.

And she did not ask him again.

## The Twenty Years And The Nine Days

He said, at the eighth hour, standing with the strap over his shoulder and not putting it down:

“I have been at that fire twenty years and I have told that to two people. The last one was my wife and she did not sleep that night and she has not brought it up since. You are the third.”

She said, “Why me.”

He said, “You counted my barrows on the road before I said a word. You asked who weighed them and you were not asking after a man. You were asking what happens to a boy who does it.”

And he said, “I would have said it to anybody who asked that. It is not rare. It is just that people do not ask it.”

He put the strap down on the stones at the roadside and straightened up, and the straightening took a while, and he did not ask her for anything when he went, and he did not say good, and he did not say anything at all after that.

She watched him go up the four miles to the cottage with the shut door and past it, and she did not follow him, and she did not call after him, and she stood in the cart track until he was a quarter of a mile off and then went back to the turn.

## Nineteen And Twenty

She did that one out loud on the stones at the ninth hour, standing four feet off a man who had said more to her in six hours than she had carried in six years, and who had put six sentences of it into her hands for nothing.

The harvest was nineteen years back. He has twenty years at that fire. So it happened in his working life and he was there and he was in the room, and he has carried it for nineteen years, and not one thing in it is wrong, and the only part of it that is any use to anybody is a boy weighing barrows.

Twenty years at a fire. One morning nineteen years back. And a girl on nine days of road with one copper and no rate on the ground and five days of standing left who counts out loud in front of everybody she has met for eleven years.

She said it out loud on the turn at the ninth hour of the twenty-fourth of the month beginning 1,814 and the road was empty in four directions.

## The Thing In Her Hand

This is the part she has gone over most.

For nine days everything she has had in her hands has been a question or the answer to one of her own. Nine coppers she cannot count, reduced to one. A shut door she did not open. A brother going west. A bar with a mark in her thigh. A loaf she bought with a number she said out loud.

And a man of forty-five came along a road at the third hour and gave her six sentences about a boy who weighed lime for a fire that nobody paid him, and he wanted nothing for them, and there is not one question in them, and there is nowhere they have to go.

She counted them on the stones before she went up to the trough, standing, in the dark, the way she counts a row of barrows.

A boy who counts what nobody is paying him to count is a boy the furnace will use. And it is not the same as keeping, and not one man at a fire thinks it is the same, and the only people who ever found the difference out were the ones it was done to.

And it happened without anybody meaning it, and not one man there was cruel, and not one of them can be named. And he had a name and nobody at that fire used it. And ask anybody on that floor and they will tell you they do not remember it.

Six. And twenty years of a man’s life went past her in between them, in figures, in rates, in the answers she asked for, and none of that is the six.

That is the thing she has to ask with.

She was lighter at the ninth hour than she was at the third, which is two hundred and seventy-seven days of it.

She said out loud, on the stones, with the road empty four hundred yards in every direction:

“I am going to ask him tonight, and I am going to ask it out loud, and there is no shed at this turn and no lamp and no wall, and I am going to stand in the dark and do it like a person asking a question of a road.”
