# What The Nine Came To

She counted the money on the flat of the bar at the second hour of the nineteenth of the month beginning 1,814, in daylight, sitting on the stones with the bar across her knees, because she had spent the eighteenth deciding that standing still is the only thing she owns, and a person does not stand still for a fortnight without knowing what is in the bag.

## How She Counts

She counts the way she has counted since she was eight years old, which is: without moving her hand off her head.

That is the trick and it took her two years to find out. You do not count out loud and then count again. You take the figures into your head and you go over them there, and your hand is free while it happens, and the number you arrive at is the number your head made and not the number your hand did.

She has counted accurately every day of her life. There is not one figure of hers in ten years that has come out wrong twice.

## Nine

She took them out of the front of the bag and put them out on the flat of the bar in a row, from her left to her right, and she named each one as she put it down.

That is the way the men with the chalk do it at the face. She has watched a man with a chalk on a string do it nine hundred times and she has done it herself six sets of days.

Nine.

She put them back in a heap and took them out again in a different order and did it the other way across, because she had nine coppers and a half of bread and the difference between those two facts is the difference between eating on the twentieth and not.

Nine.

## Eight

On the third count she got eight.

She did not stop. She had been eighteen years old once already in front of a woman behind a counter with the same nine pieces of metal in her hand, and she knows exactly what a face does, and she kept her face the same and she went on.

She put them back in a heap. She took them out again in the same order. She named each one.

Eight.

She put them back and took them out again and got nine, and she did not feel anything at all when the nine came, which is the part she wants written down.

## The Fourth Time And The Fifth

She did it four more times and she got nine twice and eight three times and she cannot say what order those were in, and she sat on the stones with the bar across her knees and nine feet of it under her forearms and looked at the pieces of metal for a long time.

They are nine. She has put them in the bag in front of a woman who gave out what a board said, and she has carried them since the second of a month beginning 1,754, and there are nine of them and they have not changed and nothing has happened to them.

The coppers are the one thing she has ever owned that is exactly what it was.

## What It Was Before It Went Wrong

Here is the finding and she has been three hours on it.

For eight years the count has missed on things. The line in the dew went out of her head in six days. The six people on a shut page stayed six. Her own entry is in a shut book and will not come out and she can say *lost* for it perfectly well.

Those are all things that were gone before she counted them.

Money is not that. Money is the first object she has ever owned that has a direction. Every other thing in the bag stays where she puts it. A copper does not stay where she puts it. It goes on the flat of a bar and then it goes on a counter and then it is not hers, and there is no version of her life in which nine coppers are nine coppers on the twentieth of a month.

And the one instrument she has had since she was eight years old will not hold still for a thing that moves.

She can count a day. She cannot count a coin.

## Nineteen Stones

Before she decided anything she went and checked whether the count had gone out of her entirely, because a woman who has lost the instrument and a woman who has lost one thing are two different women and she wanted to know which she was.

She went to the wall by the trough and counted the third row.

Nineteen.

She counted it again from the other end. Nineteen. She counted it with her hand on the stones one at a time and she counted it again with her hand behind her back, and it was nineteen both times, and she has counted that row on six mornings running.

Then she counted the bag. Five things in it, and the ring a finger’s width, and the lump of clay, and the dish, and the stub of a nib, and the cake of ink, and it was five both times.

Then she counted her own days out loud standing in the road, two hundred and seventy-two, and it was two hundred and seventy-two the second time.

The instrument is fine. The instrument has never once been the problem.

She went back and did the coppers a seventh time and it came out ten.

## What She Was At Eight

She learned it at Qinghe, on a face, from a man with a chalk on a string who wanted the men at the low to be able to hear the figure and did not want to come up and take it off him.

She was eight. She counted a load and said it out loud and the man said it again so that the low had it, and that was the whole of the arrangement and it went on for two years, and it is the reason her voice is the way it is when she is tired.

Nobody at Qinghe ever told her she was good at it. They told her the figure.

And every figure she has been given in ten years she has kept. She has kept six on a shut page and a bare stone under a ladder and a line in wet ground that went out of her head in six days and a brother who was not on it.

Nine coppers is the first figure she has been given that she is required to hold and spend it, and it will not go into her head twice.

## The Bread Is Two Coppers

She worked the rest of it out on the afternoon, sitting back against the kerb of the trough with the sun on her face and her arm out.

Bread at the store is two coppers a loaf and you cannot walk in and buy any, because the store gives out what a board says and a board says what a man with a chalk wrote on it.

She has never handed a woman a figure and taken bread for it. In this whole year she has had bread four times and three of them were given, and the fourth came out of a hand that counted nine in front of the woman and got ten.

And the store woman will not count them for her. That is not what a woman behind a counter does. A woman behind a counter takes the figure she is given and gives out what the figure says, and if the figure is wrong that is a matter between the figure and the person who said it.

So there is one way. There has only ever been one way.

Somebody has to say the number out loud.

## The Arm Came In At The Fifth Hour

She got the bar off the stones at the fifth hour with the right hand out of habit and it went before she had it up.

Not badly. It did not fold. It came up a foot off the stones and stayed there and she stood there holding it and looked at her own forearm as though it belonged to somebody else, and then she put her left under it and got it onto her shoulder with two hands.

Eight of eleven holds. She gets a thing up off the ground eleven times out of a set of eleven and the twelfth is not there, and it has not been there since the month beginning 1,724.

She has not cared about the twelfth for a year. This is the first time the twelfth has been the thing she wanted.

## What She Said Out Loud On The Stones

She got up and stood in the road and said it out loud, in the ordinary voice, the way she has said things to an empty turn for nine years.

“I have nine coppers and I cannot count them, and I am going to say a number at a counter and the number is going to be the one I say.”

And then she stood there and listened to what she had just done, because a sentence like that is a piece of a plan and she has had nine years of plans.

She is going to walk five miles west to a store behind a mill whose wheel has not turned in nine years and put a number of coppers she cannot count on a counter and walk away with bread.

It is the first arrangement she has made with anybody in nine years.

## What It Costs To Know A Thing Out Loud

He said, at the fourth hour, while she was doing the stones in the row for the sixth time:

“You are going to spend one of them to find out whether the other eight are the right number.”

She said, “Yes.”

He said, “And if the woman behind the counter counts them and gets a different figure, then there are two figures, and you will have one and she will have one, and yours is the one you will keep.”

She stood in the road and let that sit.

She had not thought of that. In nine years she has never once handed anybody a number and had to keep it while somebody else counted it themselves.

He said, “It is not the counting. It has never been the counting.”

She said, “You said that in a month beginning 1,754.”

He said, “I have been saying it for two years and you have been counting anyway.”

## The Nineteenth And The Last Of It

She put the nine back in the front of the bag at the eighth hour and tied it, and she said the number out loud one more time on the way, because from now on saying it out loud is the instrument and not the counting.

Nine.

And she does not know whether that is the case.

That is all of the nineteenth of the month beginning 1,814 and it is the first thing in ten years her count has been wrong about, and she was lighter at the eighth hour than she was at the first, which is two hundred and seventy-two days of it, and not one of them going back.
