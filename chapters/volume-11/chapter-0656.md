# Rice Paper And One Hand

She opened the drawer at the fourth hour of the twenty-third of the month beginning 2,024 and she had the key in her hand before she had decided to use it, which is not the way she does anything.

Four sheets. Not one of them a bundle and not one of them a return and not one of them in the hand of the room.

The lamp was in its cage on the fourth landing above her and she had put the cloth over it nine hours ago and left it burning, so the room had what light it had, which was not much and was enough.

She read the first sheet at the fourth hour and she read it word for word because she has read boards since she was twelve and she cannot help it, and then she read it a second time word for word, and then she put it down on the bench and put her hands flat on the bench on either side of it and sat there for a minute and a half.

## What Was On The Four Sheets

Seven sentences.

She counted them first, without reading them, because she counts, and because counting a thing before you take it in is the only way she has ever known of being sure that she has taken all of it.

Seven sentences across four sheets. The first one was on the day’s sheet at the top of the room a fortnight ago and it was the first thing he had written in twenty-two years that was going to anybody, and the other six were on the four sheets in the drawer, and he had written six of them in four days on a bench at the sixth hour with a boy of sixteen four feet away.

And it is not about him.

That is the thing she was not ready for and it took her until the seventh hour to get to the bottom of it. She had walked five days to a town to find out what a thing was, and a man had put the thing on four sheets of rice paper in a fair hand, and the thing took up half of it, and the other half of it was her.

## The One That Stopped Her

The fifth one is the one that stopped her, and she has not been able to write about the other six in order and she is not going to try, because they are on the sheets and they will still be there in nine years.

The fifth one is a girl who has been counting with a man for eight years.

She sat on the floor of that copying room at the seventh hour of the twenty-third of the month beginning 2,024 with her back against the end of the bench and read it four times and it did not change on any of the four, and here is what happened to her and it is the only part of it worth anybody else.

She has been told four true things about him in ten years by four mouths and none of the four arrived.

She has read boards, sheets, returns, a claim, a fair volume at the back of a bench, a page in a good hand in a low house at the north end of a furnace ground, and a shut book that a woman of forty-five turned the page of, and every one of those went in and stayed, and she has a book of eight leaves against her ribs with a name on the third of them in her own hand.

And Feng Lushi, a man of sixty-seven she had met twice, wrote four sentences on her in a fair hand at the sixth hour and she cannot put one word of them into her own book, because they are not hers and her hand cannot take another person’s sentences.

And because if she copied them onto a blank leaf then there would be two copies of the same four sentences ten inches apart in the same hand, and she has done a thing like that twice before and rubbed it out both times.

She said out loud, at the seventh hour and forty minutes, to the shelves:

“He wrote it and I cannot put it anywhere, and that is the first time in ten years that being written down by somebody else has been the thing that I could not get at.”

## The Two Halves Of One Sentence

The other six are on the four sheets and she read them all of them four times and she is going to write down here only the shape of them, because the shape is what she has not got anywhere.

Six sentences. About three of them are about a fire in the east and a piece of a thing with a name that a clerk of sixty-seven saw and would not write down, and a boy of nineteen and a piece of him that was not burned.

And about three of them are about a levy with a fraction in it on a board that goes down on the second of a month and does not tell anybody their own total.

She said out loud, at the seventh hour and fifty minutes of the twenty-third of the month beginning 2,024, standing at the end of the bench:

“Those are not two subjects. They are one sentence in two halves and I have been holding the halves apart since I was twelve years old, because the half about the fire is a thing that happened to him and the half on the levy is a thing that happens to everybody, and I read them at a wall and I read them at a fire and I never once in nine years put them on the same line.”

She said, “A clerk put them on the same line in four days with a brush.”

She said, “And he did not do it because he understood any of it. He did it because he saw it, and he wrote down what he saw, and that is a worse reason and it is the only one there is.”

## Three Days Of Carrying It Around A Room

She read all four sheets again at the sixth hour of the twenty-fourth and again at the sixth hour of the twenty-fifth and again at the sixth hour of the twenty-sixth, and on each of those three days the boy of sixteen came up at the eighth hour and copied what was on the rack and went down with the water, and on none of the three days did either of them say one word to the other about what was in the drawer.

On the twenty-sixth she copied the whole thing out for herself standing in the room, in the ordinary voice, at the ninth hour, in ten minutes.

She did it to test it. She wanted to know whether the reading survived being said, because a thing said in a room has a second copy in a person, and that is the only way she has ever made two copies of anything.

She got to the fifth sentence and she could not say it.

She got four words and then she had to stop, and she stood at the end of the bench with her hand on the wood for a count and a half and did not try again, because she had proved what she wanted to prove, which was that a page in a hand that is not hers does not go into a person by being read out loud. It goes in by being read, alone, at an hour when there is nothing to do on it.

## Bread And A Counter On The Twenty-Fifth

She went down four miles to the square at the eighth hour of the twenty-fifth and bought bread at the rate the woman behind the counter said out loud, which was two for a loaf, and she put her hand in and took two and did not count them and did not look at them after the second one, and she stood at the counter afterwards with the bread in her hand for two minutes longer than a person stands at a counter.

The woman said, “Nine.”

She said, “What.”

The woman said, “You have not had nine in this county and you have not been in this county in five years and I said nine because you have the face of a person counting metal in her head in front of me, and I have said nine at four people in nine years and you are the fifth.”

She said, “You have got a good memory.”

The woman said, “I have got a good memory and a bad chest and there is not one person left in this town who uses either of them. Two coppers a loaf. That has not changed in five years because there is not one person in this county who could change it.”

And a woman behind a counter said a number out loud to her and had it right, and it went into two places at once, and Ning Yan stood in a square four miles below a tower with a loaf under her arm and found that the thing that had happened in the last four days had not made the other thing any smaller.

## What She Took Out Of The Drawer And Why

On the twenty-seventh of the month beginning 2,024, at the sixth hour, in daylight, she opened the drawer and took the four sheets out and folded them along four existing folds and put them inside her coat against her ribs, on the side where the book is.

She said out loud, standing in a lit room with the light on them, to the shelves and the bench and the boy at the rack:

“I am taking these out of the drawer. There is no one in this county with any standing to stop me and there is no one in this county who is going to enter them anywhere and the lamp in the cage goes on burning with no keeper in the building and a bundle goes up a stair on a boy’s arm on the first of the next month whether I am here or not.”

She said, “I am not going to copy them. I have got five blank leaves and I am not going to write one word of this on any of them, because the last sentence on the fourth sheet is that one is never written twice, and I am not going to be the second one and I am not going to make his the first of two.”

She said, “And I want it said out loud in this room that a dead man’s handwriting is not mine and that I have taken it anyway, and that I did not steal it because there was nothing to steal, and that if a person came up these four flights in ten years and asked me where it came from I am going to tell them all of it including this.”

And then she got her book out of her coat and opened it on the bench to the fourth leaf and looked at the five blank ones for a minute.

And she closed it and put it back, and the five were blank, and they are blank now.

She was lighter at the tenth hour of the twenty-seventh of the month beginning 2,024 than she was at the sixth hour of it, which is four hundred and ninety days of it, counting the eighteenth of the month beginning 1,544 as the first, and not one of them going back.

And she was standing in a copying room four flights above a town with a bar against the end of the bench and four sheets of a fair hand against her ribs, and at the sixth hour of the next morning a boy of sixteen was going to come up with the water and find her there, and she was going to read all seven of them in front of him, in daylight, in a lit room.

Because she had worked out overnight that there is not one thing she has learned in ten years that she did not learn alone at the fourth hour, and that is why not one of it ever reached anybody else.
