# Six Hundred And Forty

She carried the bar a hundred yards off the road at the seventh hour of the twenty-eighth of the month beginning 1,814, put it down flat in the broken ground on the north side of the turn, and turned round and faced the road.

Daylight. A made road with a bench on it, a woman of sixty thirty feet off on the bench, a bar on two posts four hundred yards east with nothing chalked on it, and the whole of the east road going away toward a gate and a wall.

One at a time.

She had not said the words again and she was not going to. She had said them at the ninth hour of the twenty-fourth of a month, out loud, alone, in the dark, and he is the other one who has got them, and a thing asked twice is a thing refused twice.

So she said the small one, the one that is inside it, in the ordinary voice, on open ground, at the seventh hour.

“What did you do on an ordinary day.”

## What He Said

He said it in four seconds and she counted them, and it came out flat and ordinary and in the present tense, the way a person tells you where they were.

“Bricks.”

She did not say anything. She had a rule and the rule was one at a time and the rule was that a woman who has been given three inches of an answer does not grab at the fourth.

“Two hundred and forty courses in a day at six hundred and forty to a course,” he said. “I was the one who counted them. I have been counting bricks since I was eleven years old and I have never once been paid for it.”

She said, “A furnace.”

“A furnace,” he said. “East of here. A long way east of here. Not this sect and not any sect you have stood in.”

She said, “Where.”

And then she stopped, because she had opened her mouth, and because the rule is one at a time.

He said, “You are going to ask me that one next and you are not going to ask it next.”

“No,” she said. “I am not.”

## Nineteen

And then he gave her the third thing, and she had not asked for the third thing.

“I was nineteen when I stopped counting them,” he said. “I am nineteen now. Those are two different sentences and you are going to have to work out which one of them you believed just now, and I am not going to help you.”

She said, “Why did you give me three.”

He said, “Because you asked one.”

She said, “That is not an answer.”

He said, “It is the whole of the answer. You asked me a question and I gave you everything I had on the top of the question without being asked, because that is what I do, and I have been doing it to you for eight years, and you have never once stopped me.”

## What She Said To The Woman On The Bench

The woman of sixty was thirty feet off with her hands in her lap and she did not look up at any point in any of it.

That is the turn for a witness. That is all a turn is. It is not a gate and nothing was ever posted at it, and nine years of people turn at this turn and do not turn back, and on the twenty-eighth of the month beginning 1,814 a woman of sixty sat thirty feet off while a girl of nineteen asked a question out loud in daylight and got three answers to it and did not take a fourth.

Not one word of it reached her. She is sixty and she has said four sentences in four months and she was not going to start for this.

And that is what she wanted, and she had checked for it at the sixth hour, and she had checked for it twice: no carter due for nine days, no board, no chain, no walking party, nothing on the road within four hundred yards either way.

And then a carter came along the road at about the seventh hour and twenty minutes, on foot, with a dog, going east at a walk, and he came through the turn while she was still standing in the broken ground a hundred yards north of it.

He looked at her. He looked at the bar on the ground. He looked at the road he was on.

And he went on east.

That is all. Nine days of road and a carter and a dog and a woman standing a hundred yards off the turn in daylight doing something he had no way of naming, and he went on east, and she watched him for a quarter of a mile and did not call out.

He said, “You priced that yesterday.”

She said, “I priced that yesterday.”

He said, “And now you know the price.”

She said, “Now I know the price. It is one carter and a dog and a quarter of a mile, and he did not stop, and I would have been ruined if he had.”

## The Part That Is About Him And Not About Her

She stood in the broken ground a hundred yards north of a turn with a bar on the ground at her feet and worked that out in about two minutes, standing, in daylight.

He gave her a furnace and a number and an age. She asked one question.

That is not a man being generous with an answer. That is a man giving an entry — a whole one, with the place and the figure and the name of the person in it, all of it at once, because a person like that counts entries and not sentences.

He has been handing her whole entries since the eighth of a month ten years back. Every figure he has ever read out to her has arrived with more attached to it than she asked for, and she has taken all of them and put them where they go and never once asked him why.

She said, “You have been doing that since I was eight years old.”

He said, “Yes.”

She said, “And I have never once said no to it.”

He said, “No. You have never once said no to it and it is the only piece of ground you have ever stood on that I am certain of.”

## What She Did Not Do

She did not ask the second thing.

She had a rule and she had made the rule on the sixth stage and it was sixty-nine days old and it was the only piece of the whole arrangement she had never once broken.

He gave her a furnace. A number. Nineteen. Three things where she had asked for one, and she stood there and looked at all three of them and did not take a fourth.

She said, “One at a time.”

He said, “Yes.”

She said, “Then the rest of it waits.”

He said, “It has waited nineteen years. It will wait a day.”

She said, “You are not going to tell me what the rest of it is.”

He said, “No.”

She said, “You are not going to tell me what you were.”

He said, “No.”

And she stood there with a furnace in her head and a number and an age and neither of them said one word about any of it, and that is the terms working, and she had never in her life watched a rule keep two people apart and be the reason anything at all happened.

## What She Put Together On The Way Back

She came a hundred yards back to the turn at about the eighth hour with the bar on her left shoulder and she went and sat on the kerb by the trough and put the three things down in the order she had got them.

Two hundred and forty courses a day.

Six hundred and forty bricks to a course.

Nineteen.

And on the twenty-fourth of a month beginning 1,814 a man of forty-five with his hands burned into the creases stood on a cart track four miles west of this turn and gave her six sentences about a boy who weighed lime for a fire that nobody paid him.

She sat on that kerb for a quarter of an hour and did not move.

A boy at a fire, counting what nobody pays him to count. A man at a fire, counting the same thing, at two hundred and forty courses a day, with no one paying him for that either.

He said, “You have worked it out.”

She said, “I have worked out that there is a place where that happened.”

He said, “Yes.”

She said, “And you are not going to tell me where.”

He said, “I told you east. That is one piece and it is true and it is not an answer.”

She said, “It is enough for me to start walking.”

He said, “It is enough for you to start walking and it will take you further than a month and I am not going to be able to stop that either, and I have known since the ninth hour of the twenty-fourth that I was not going to be able to stop it.”

## What Is Left Of The Morning

She said the terms out loud one more time on the stones of the turn at the ninth hour of the twenty-eighth of the month beginning 1,814, in daylight, to the road and to the woman of sixty thirty feet off and to anybody who had come up the road in the next hour.

“One at a time.”

“In daylight.”

And then she said the thing she had not been able to say since the ninth hour of the twenty-fourth, and she said it out loud, on open ground, with a carter not due for nine days and the turn for a witness.

“You were a boy who counted what nobody paid him to count.”

He said, “Yes.”

She said, “And you counted bricks.”

He said, “Six hundred and forty to a course.”

And that is the whole of what she got on the twenty-eighth of the month beginning 1,814: a furnace, a number, and nineteen. She is nineteen herself, at six stages past a ceiling, one copper the lighter for it and the coppers the one thing she cannot count, and she is lighter on this morning than she was on the eighteenth of the month beginning 1,544 by two hundred and eighty-one days, and not one of them going back.

And she has a place to walk to.

He said, “Say it out loud.”

She said, “A furnace, a number, and nineteen.”

He said, “That is the smallest true thing I have.”

She said, “It is the smallest true thing anybody has ever handed me.”

He said, “Yes. And you will not be able to put it anywhere.”

She said, “No.”

He said, “There is nowhere to put it. Not a book, not a column, not a board, not a leaf.”

She said, “I know. I have been working on that for ten days.”

And then she stopped talking, and stood on the turn in daylight with a bar on her shoulder and a furnace in her head, and did not ask the second thing, and did not rub the lever out in the dust, and did not say thank you, because she is not going to and she is not going to start.
