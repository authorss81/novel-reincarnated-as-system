# The Loaf And The Number

She had nine coppers she could not count and the last piece of a loaf she has been eating a piece at a time for six days, and on the twenty-second of the month beginning 1,814 at the first hour she said the number out loud on the stones, twice, and then walked five miles west to a store behind a mill to spend it.

She ate the last piece standing at the trough at the fourth hour and then went west.

A man of seventy is at the mill. The wheel has not turned in nine years and there are four people in the building and two of them are under sixty and one of those two has a cough she can hear from the road.

He was on his own step with a thing in his hands and she said good and he said good and neither of them stopped, because that is what a road is. She has had four good mornings on this ground and every one of them was that.

## The Counter Behind The Mill

The store is one room with the wheel outside the door and it is open because the woman behind the counter is in it, and she has been behind that counter since the day she came to this ground and she is not going to be told anything by anybody.

Two coppers a loaf. That is the rate and it is said out loud to anybody who asks, and Ning Yan had worked it out on the twenty-first from a stone kerb a mile east of a shut door and she had it right.

She put the bag on the counter and took the nine coppers out and laid them in a row.

The woman looked at the row for a second and a half.

“Nine,” she said.

## The First Thing That Happened At That Counter

She said it without any hurry at all, in the ordinary voice, without counting anything, the way a person says the hour.

And Ning Yan stood there and waited for the ground to do something, because here is what had been going to happen and she had built the whole day on it: that a woman behind a counter would count nine pieces of metal and get a different figure, and that there would then be two figures, and that hers would be the one she had to keep.

The woman got nine.

And she has been able to get nine herself on the nineteenth, on the bar, in the head, four times out of seven, and not one single time has anybody else ever told her a figure she had not taken in first.

She is not a person who counts badly. She has never once been a person who counts badly. She is a person who has spent ten years building the only instrument she has ever had and it turns out that it works perfectly well on everything except the one thing that moves.

“Nine,” the woman said again. “You had nine on me in the month beginning 1,754 and you counted them on my counter until I wished you would go home. Are you buying or are you doing that again.”

## Two Coppers A Loaf

“Two coppers a loaf,” Ning Yan said. “That is the rate. I am buying.”

She put two of them on the counter. She did not pick which two. She put her hand in and took two and put them down and put her hand away, and she did not count them, and she did not look at them after.

The woman did not count them either. She pushed the nine back across the counter, and pushed a loaf across the counter after them, four inches, and kept her hand on it until Ning Yan took hold of it.

That is a trade. There was not one thing in it that was not a trade, and the woman behind the counter did not say a single word about a girl who cannot count money, because a woman behind a counter is not the business of what a girl’s head is doing and has never been.

## What She Did Not Say

She did not say thank you. She has said it once in nine years, to a man of nine hundred years, for four sentences about a house, and it bought nothing and she has not said it since.

She did not say her mother’s name, which was in her mouth the whole way up the store road and which she has not said out loud since she was eleven years old.

She did not ask the woman a single question. She has spent ten years asking questions of everybody she has ever met, and there was one available behind this counter, and she stood there and let it go because she had bread in her hand and she did not want to spend the day on an answer.

## The Loaf On The Wall

She ate a quarter of it sitting on the wall of the mill yard at the third hour and it was the first bread she has bought in her life.

And she sat there with the rest of it in her lap doing the thing her body does, which is to put the whole of it in the same place as the sum. Ten days to the board. A quarter of a loaf in a cloth and seven coppers in the front of a bag.

She worked it out on the wall with her mouth full and got the same answer she has got every morning since the eighteenth, which is that the loaf is four days and the sum is five, and that on the fifth she will be walking.

There is no arithmetic on nine days of road that changes that. She knows it and she has been walking on it since the first hour with her right arm out in front of her, and the loaf is in her lap and the mill wheel is four inches off her back and it has not turned since she was ten.

That is the whole of it and it took two coppers and five miles of walking and a quarter of an hour and she is aware of how small that is.

But here is the part she has not got past.

In ten years no one has ever given her anything that was not in exchange for a figure. The man with the chalk gives a rate and gives a board. The woman of fifty at the end of a lane puts a loaf in her hand and it is not asked for, and it is the only one of the four that has ever cost anybody nothing.

And a loaf you have bought is the first thing she has ever walked five miles for, and she has eaten a quarter of it, and it is bread, and there is nothing in it. There is no debt in it and nobody in it and no door in it.

She has not had anything in her life with no door in it.

## What A Number Said Out Loud Is

She got it out on the wall of the mill yard with the bread in her lap, and it took a while, and he helped her with it without being asked, in the way he has helped her for eight years, which is to say he would not have helped her if she had not been stuck.

A number you keep is a number that stays where you put it. She has eight years of those. Six people on a shut page. Two hundred and seventy-five days of going lighter. Nineteen stones in the third row of a wall by a trough.

A number you hand over is a number you do not keep. It goes on a counter and it becomes a fact about two people instead of one, and the second person has it now as well, and neither of you can change it.

That is the whole of why nine coppers will not go into her head. She has never in her life had to give a figure away and then live in a world where somebody else has been given it too.

And the cost of it is not the nine coppers and it is not the bread. The cost is that at the second hour of the twenty-second of the month beginning 1,814 a woman behind a counter said *nine* to her, and now two people in the world have that figure, and it cannot be unsaid by either of them.

He said, “That is not a small thing and you have written it as though it were a shop.”

She said, “It is the first thing I have ever done where the count was in two places.”

He said, “Then do not do it nine times.”

## He Came Past At The Dusk

She was a mile east of the mill at the eighth hour going back to the turn with three quarters of a loaf in a cloth in her right hand, and he came out of the mill yard behind her going west with a strap over his shoulder and a mill-sack on the end of it.

She knew him at four hundred yards by the way he walks.

She did not go into the road.

He came up and he stopped, the way two people stop at six feet, and he looked at the cloth in her hand and not at her face.

“You have eaten,” he said.

“I have.”

“That is a trade.”

“That is a trade,” she said.

And he went on west.

That is all of it. Five sentences between them in nine years and three of them were of bread, and she did not ask him one question, and she did not call after him when he got to a quarter of a mile, and she stood in the road for ten minutes afterwards.

## The Bill For Turning Round

Here is what she has got, and she did the arithmetic standing in a cart track with a loaf under her arm.

Four miles west on the twenty-first, to a shut door, and one mile short of him, and she turned round.

Five miles west on the twenty-second, and he went past her going the other way.

Two walkings in two days and one brother. He has been on this road twice in nine years and she has seen him twice in nine years and she has got one bread and two sentences of the kind that a person says when they can see you have eaten.

And the turning round cost her all of it. If she had gone the mile to the mill on the twenty-first she would have been in a building with him in it instead of on a road with him walking past her in the dusk.

She knew that on the twenty-first. She stood at a shut door and worked out that she wanted that mile so badly her chest went cold, and she turned round, and this is what turning round is worth.

He said nothing at all while she worked it out. He has not been in her head once since the seventeenth of a month beginning 1,814 and he is not going to start, and she stood in a cart track with three quarters of a loaf and let that be the arrangement.

She was lighter at the ninth hour than she was at the fifth. Two hundred and seventy-five days of it, counting the eighteenth of the month beginning 1,544 as the first.

She has bread and a number she said out loud and seven coppers she cannot count, and she ate, and she is not going to be able to buy anything at all with any of it before the board goes down in ten days.

Seven days of standing left at the turn. Four of them east of her and three of them west of her, and a bar on two posts with nothing on it, and she is going to get to the end of that on foot with a loaf in her bag and she has not the least idea in the world what she is going to do on the last of them.

He said, “You will do something.”

She said, “I will not.”

He said, “You have said that to me about a dozen things and you have done nine of them.”
