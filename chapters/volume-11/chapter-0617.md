# She Heard Every Word Of It

She was four miles along the made road at the fifth hour of the fifth of the month beginning 1,844, on the seventh day of walking, with the bar on her left shoulder and a heel of loaf in the bag and one copper she cannot count, when a woman came up the road at her going west.

## Eight Feet

She read the woman’s hands at eight feet, which she has done since she was six, and she stopped in the road to do it.

The burns are not a smith’s and they are not a cook’s. They go in the creases and they stay in the creases, and the fingers are held a little apart the whole time, the way a hand is held by somebody who has been feeling heat all day and has stopped noticing that it is heat.

There was stone dust on the shoulder of the woman’s coat and a basket on her arm with lime in it and a cloth over the top of the lime, and she was about forty-five and she walked with her right foot turned out a little.

Ning Yan said good, because that is what a person says on a made road.

The woman said good and kept walking, and then she stopped too, four feet past, and turned her head.

“You have a bar.”

“I have a bar.”

“A long one.”

“Nine feet nine,” Ning Yan said. “It goes on a strap out of my own coat lining.”

The woman looked at the strap and then at the bag and then at the four fingers and the thumb, and none of that took her any longer than it takes a person to look.

## The Question She Asked

She asks everybody about work. It is the first thing out of her mouth on nine days of road and it has been for two years, and it is the only way she has ever found her way into a building.

She said, “Is there stone worked out here.”

The woman said, “There is nothing worked for eight miles either way and there has not been for longer than that.”

“Then what is the lime for.”

“Going west,” the woman said. “To a post, for a kiln. It is my husband’s brother’s kiln and I go twice a season and I have made the walk since I was twenty-six.”

And then she said the thing that a woman who has been walking west with a basket for nineteen years says to a stranger with a bar.

“Where are you bound for.”

Ning Yan said, “East.”

“For what.”

There was a road and a bar on her shoulder and a woman four feet off her, and this was the fifth of a month and the light was off the hedges, and one at a time is a rule she made about the voice in her head and has never once broken in nine years of asking strangers about work.

She said it, because she says things out loud.

“For a furnace. I am looking for a furnace where a man counted bricks.”

## What The Woman Did With That

The woman put the basket down on the verge.

She did it slowly, which is how a person puts a basket down when they have been thinking about something and have forgotten to be standing up. She put it on the verge and stood over it with her hand still on the handle and she did not say anything for the length of four seconds.

Then she said, “Say that again.”

“A man counted bricks.”

“Not what a man does now.” The woman’s voice had not gone up. It had gone flat and careful, the way a voice goes careful on a wet floor. “A long time ago. A boy who counted.”

“Yes.”

“How many to a course.”

Ning Yan said, “Six hundred and forty.”

The woman took her hand off the basket.

## Nineteen Years Of A Husband

She told it standing on the verge with the basket between them and the light going, in the order a person tells a thing they have never told.

“He was senior at a sect that is not this one and not any one you have stood in. Nineteen years back he began a work on himself that does not finish, and the beginning went into him and the rest of it did not come.”

She said, “What is the rest of it.”

“You will want to sit down for the rest of it,” she said, “and there is nothing to sit on, so you will stand.”

She said, “I will stand.”

“The sect filled it. That is the word they used and it is a true word. They filled a stall in a work with a person, and the person was a boy off a fire, and the boy was the son of the man who keeps the fire.”

“Was it taken from the fire.”

“It was asked for and given. That is how it was done and I have had nineteen years to think of a better sentence and there is not one.”

## The Book

She said, “They gave me a book.”

“What book.”

“The book of the work. It came to me in the hands of a clerk with a good hand and it has my husband’s name at the front of it and the morning inside it.” She said, “They told me to write it down. All of it. The date and the place and the weight and the hour. And I wrote it down and I shut it and I put it on a shelf and it has been shut since that month.”

“Why did you shut it.”

“Because it was finished.”

She said that the way people say things that have been argued with once, early, by somebody who is dead.

“It was all in it and there was no more of it to come, and a thing that is all in a book does not get better for being looked at.”

Ning Yan said, “What is in it.”

“I do not know,” the woman said. “I wrote it. I have written a great many things in my life and I could not tell you one of them now. I know what is on the shelf. I have not opened it in nineteen years.”

## The Name

The light was nearly off the road by then and the woman stood over her basket and looked west and said:

“He had a name.”

Ning Yan said, “I did not ask you for it.”

“No,” the woman said. “You did not. I have been carrying it for nineteen years on the grounds that this road is empty, and a person has just come along it who is looking for the boy.”

She said it in the ordinary voice, the way a person says a number they have checked twice.

“Yuan Sheng.”

## The Telling Missing

She heard it.

That is the part she has gone over since, and the going over has not produced a better account of it. She heard two syllables in the ordinary voice from a woman standing four feet off her with a basket of lime, at the sixth hour of the fifth of a month, on a made road, in daylight.

She heard them the way she has heard every figure the man in her head has ever read out to her. She did not have to work for them. They arrived the way *six hundred and forty* arrives, with the whole of themselves, and she could have said them back word for word, and she had them.

And she put them somewhere, the way she puts everything.

She said them to the made road.

“Yuan Sheng.”

And she waited, standing on a made road with a bar on her shoulder, the way she waited in the broken ground a hundred yards north of the turn on the twenty-ninth of a month beginning 1,814.

Nothing sat down.

She said them again, slower, and the same words came up, in the same order, in her own voice, and there was no place behind them where a thing goes when it is kept.

She looked at the hedge and said them a third time and the woman four feet off her did not react at all, because a woman saying a name out loud to a hedge is not a thing that gets a reaction.

She said, “I have it.”

“You have it,” the woman agreed.

“It will not stay.”

The woman turned her head and looked at her for the first time properly, at her face rather than at her hands, and it took a while.

“Then you are the first person I have said that name to who is going to have to do something about it,” she said, “and I am not going to tell you what to do.”

## What She Works Out Standing There

She got it out on the road with the last of the light in it, and she said it, and the woman let her.

“A number goes in. A date goes in. A count of bricks in a course goes in and it has gone in every day of my life and I have never lost one.”

“Yes,” the woman said.

“A name does not go in. Not out of a mouth. It is a sound and then it is over.”

She said, “That is true of most things.”

“It is not true of most things and you know it is not and so do I.”

And she stood on a made road with a bar on her shoulder and worked out the whole of the rest of it, standing, in daylight, and it took her four minutes.

A voice counts things. A book writes things. Those are the two ways a thing has ever got into her, and she has ten years of both, and neither one has ever failed her.

A name that is only spoken goes nowhere. A name that is written goes in a book and stays in the book.

And the one book in the world with that name in it is on a shelf in a house she has not seen, kept by a woman of forty-five who wrote it once, nineteen years back, and has not opened it since, and is not going to open it for a girl with a bar.

## The Direction, And The Woman Going West

The woman picked the basket up at the seventh hour and put it on her arm.

“You are going to want a direction and I am going to give you one and it is not a kindness, it is because you have asked me one question I did not have to answer and I am square.”

“Go on.”

“Eleven miles of made road from the turn and then two past the gate to a wall with an opening standing open in it. Past that the ground goes up. Two days on the ground that goes up, maybe less, and you will know you are near when the air changes.”

“How will I know.”

“You will know when the air changes,” the woman said, “because there is a furnace on that ground and it has not been let out in nineteen years and the people there have not wanted anybody on it and have not had anybody.”

She said, “Have they said so.”

“They have never had to.”

And then she went on west at a walk with a basket of lime under a cloth, and she did not look back, and she was four hundred yards off before Ning Yan got the bar up onto her shoulder, and that was the last of her.

## Where The Name Is

She stood on the made road in the dark with one copper she cannot count and a heel of loaf and two syllables she can produce perfectly whenever she wants them.

The name is in the world in two places.

One of them is a road east of a gate, in the air, in two ears, and a road does not keep.

The other is a shut book on a shelf, and the book is not hers, and the woman who keeps it does not know what is written in it.

And there is a third place, which is against her own ribs, made out of a blanket, with six leaves in it that are hers and blank.

She put her hand on it through the front of her coat.

She could not put the name on it. She had just proved that she could not, standing in the road, in the last of the light, with the words still perfectly available and the whole thing a hand’s width away from the nib.

It had to be said and written in the same motion, in one hand, in the same breath, or it was not going to be written at all.

She walked east.
