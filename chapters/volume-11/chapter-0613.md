# The Count That Held Twice

She took the book out at about the eleventh hour of the twenty-seventh of the month beginning 1,814 and sat on the kerb by the trough with the light off and the stones cold under her, because she had one morning of daylight left and she was going to go into it with the whole of what she owns in her hands.

A book of eight leaves made out of a blanket. The edges are cut and not sewn and the stitching is a woman’s needle through a folded edge about a finger apart, and she did it herself in the month beginning 1,724 on a floor with no light in it.

The first leaf has a date and a place on it and nothing else.

The second has four sentences on it that she has read out loud once and will not read again.

Six are hers and they are blank.

## What She Did With Them

She counted them.

Six.

She counted them again with her thumb on the edge of the blanket, going round the corners, because she counts and because she could not stop herself.

Six.

She counted them a third time with her hand behind her back, which is the way she does a count she does not trust.

Six.

She sat on a kerb in the dark on the twenty-seventh of a month and did it a fourth and a fifth time and it was six every time and she has to write that down properly because it is the first count she has ever made that has never once come back different.

## Why

She worked that out in about four minutes and it took her the rest of the night to sit with it.

Nine coppers on the flat of a bar: nine, nine, eight, nine, eight, ten.

Barrow-loads into a low: eleven, eleven, twelve, eleven.

Two hundred and eighty days of going lighter: two hundred and eighty, two hundred and eighty.

Six leaves in a blanket, four months, five counts, and six every time.

And the reason is not that the leaves are easier than money and it is not that she is holding them.

Money leaves. Work leaves. Days leave — she has watched two hundred and eighty of them leave one at a time and has never once asked where they go.

A leaf does not go anywhere when you count it. It is a piece of a blanket against her ribs and it is there in the morning and it is there at the ninth hour and it is the same piece of blanket at the end of it, and the number of them is not a thing that can be spent or worked off or worn out by being looked at.

## The Second Leaf

She did not read the second leaf. She has not read it since the month beginning 1,724 and she said out loud, once, on that date, four sentences she had written with her own two hands in a room on her own, and she will not read them again.

They are about a house. A house with a standing stone in the yard. She has been to the yard twice.

She knows roughly what is on that leaf and she could reconstruct most of it, and she has never wanted to, and she has never once written a line of it out for anybody, and in ten years she has described it in full to a man of nine hundred years standing on a stone step at the eighth hour and not to one other person in the world.

Here is the finding in that, and it took her most of an hour.

The second leaf is not a record. She has been treating it as one since the day she wrote it. It is four sentences she said out loud to an empty room and then sealed, and it was never for anybody.

That is a different thing from keeping. Keeping is what you do with a thing for later. She did that on the first of the six and put the nib away.

## What She Worked Out About The Room

Then she got to the part she was actually there for.

She has decided not to write in them twice. She put a nib to the first of them at the fourth hour of the twenty-eighth of the month beginning 1,724 and she put it away. She took the whole book out at the ninth hour of the fourteenth of the month beginning 1,814 and held it for an hour in the dark with a stub of a nib and a cake of ink she has not used in two months and she put it back.

Four months of room and not a day of it.

And she had it in her head at the first hour of the twenty-eighth of the month beginning 1,814 that room has never been the problem.

Six leaves is more than any question she has ever carried. She has had four questions standing in a shut book nine hundred years deep and they have fitted in four places and she had room for the first of them the day she found the book.

Room is not what she has run out of.

## The Thing She Is Not Going To Put In Them

She made herself go through it, out loud, on a kerb in the dark, because she counts and because she would rather say a thing where no one is than carry it.

The six sentences a man of forty-five gave her at the turn on the twenty-fourth. They are the only thing anybody has handed her in a year that was not in exchange for a figure, and there is nowhere in the world she may write them down.

The date of the eighteenth of the month beginning 1,544, and the four things that are on it, and the lime and the wheel and the boy behind the last barrow and the hole in her.

Eleven barrows on a stone face on the twenty-sixth.

Four hundred yards of road walked on the twenty-first.

And a number she has not got yet.

And she said all of it out loud, and the book was open on her knee with six blank leaves in it, and she did not put the nib near any one of them.

## What She Did Not Do With The Ink

The nib is a stub. The cake of ink has not been opened in two months and there is enough of it in there for one leaf or two.

She has carried it for two months past the point where she had anything to write.

That is not a coincidence and she did not leave it alone on purpose either. She simply went past it. She was given the whole of a six-leaf book of her own and a nib and a cake of ink, and she carried all three of them, and she did not use them, and the reason underneath the reason is that she has never had a thought of her own that was worth the ink.

Every thought she has had in ten years came from a document, a keeper, a wall, a man with a chalk, a boy at a gate, a woman behind a counter or a voice in her head.

There has not been one of hers.

And she is going to a turn at the sixth hour of the twenty-eighth of the month beginning 1,814 with one question, and the question is going to be hers, and that is the first time in ten years that has been true of anything she is about to do.

## What Six Blank Leaves Are

Here is what she got out of the night and this is the finding.

She has been treating them as a place to keep things. That is wrong and it has been wrong since the month beginning 1,724.

They are not a place to keep things. There is not one answer in ten years she has ever been able to keep — not a figure, not a day, not a name, not a sentence she read out loud once — and she has never wanted to keep one in a blanket book, because she has known since the sixth stage that finding is not holding and holding is not keeping.

They are the only thing she owns that cannot be taken.

That is all. Not a room. Not a record. Not a place to put a question when there is nowhere else, which is how she has been using them in her head for four months, in the way a person uses a door they have never opened.

The paper against her ribs on the other side, opened with her own two hands four years back, is taken from her and cannot be taken back. The list has her name in it as *lost*. The coppers go. The work goes. The bar is stone and the strap is out of her own coat lining.

Six pieces of a blanket no one has ever wanted, in a book no one knows she has, in a coat she made herself.

And she has been guarding them for four months with nothing to guard, which is what a person does with a thing they are afraid of losing, and she has not lost it once.

## What She Said To Him About It

She said it out loud on the kerb at the second hour of the twenty-eighth of the month beginning 1,814, with the book shut on her knee.

“Six,” she said. “Five times. It has been six five times.”

He said, “I know. I have watched you do it.”

She said, “You have watched me fail at it twice in nine days and you have watched me do it five times in one night and it does not fail.”

He said, “It is the same blanket.”

She said, “It is the same blanket and the same six and I am not writing in them.”

He said, “I know that too. You said so on the twenty-eighth of a month beginning 1,724 and you said so again on the fourteenth of the month beginning 1,814 and you are going to say it a third time.”

She said, “What am I going to say it for.”

He said, “Because you have a morning at the sixth hour and something in your hand that is not a question, and if you spend the morning on what to write in a book you will come to the turn at the sixth hour with a page and nothing to say on it.”

She put the book away against the paper, where it has been since the month beginning 1,724, and put the bag on her left shoulder, and she had four hours of daylight in front of her and one question in it.

## What She Put Her Hand On Instead

In the end she did not go back to the book. She put her right hand flat on the blanket where it sat against her ribs and she left it there and she said one thing out loud on the kerb, at the third hour of the twenty-eighth of the month beginning 1,814, to a road with nothing on the board.

“I am not going to write any of it down,” she said. “The six sentences, the date, the barrows, the four miles, the number I have not got. None of it goes on a leaf and none of it goes on the second one and I am not opening the ink.”

He said, “Then what is the book for.”

She said, “It is the only thing I own that they cannot send a clerk to.”

He said, “Yes.”

She said, “And that is not nothing, and I am going to be nineteen for another year, and I am going to need a great deal of things that cannot be sent a clerk to, and I am not writing in them tonight because tonight is for the question.”

And she put the bag on her left shoulder and got the bar off the ground with two hands, and the right arm went, and she did not try to hold it, and that is the last thing she did before the light.
