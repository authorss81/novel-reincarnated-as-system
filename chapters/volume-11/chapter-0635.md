# She Walks It Herself

She did not go and stand on it for four days.

That is the first of it and she said it out loud at the gate at the seventh hour of the second of the month beginning 1,934 to a man who was in her head and has never once been asked to keep a fact for her.

He said, “Why not.”

She said, “Because it is there, and it has been there since the eighth hour of the twenty-sixth, and it is not going anywhere, and every hour I do not go on it is an hour it is only a patch of new stone with a gap in the north joint, and in four days it is going to be that again.”

He said, “That is not a reason. That is a procedure.”

She said, “It is the only one I have got and it has worked for me since I was eight.”

## Four Days Of Not Going

She worked. She put up barrows at a fifth of a man’s rate and the boy called the figures and the man of fifty-one wrote them on the board with a half on them now, which took two days to stop looking like an error, and she went on sleeping on the stones outside the gate with her bag under her head and the bar across her knees.

And four times a day she came within thirty feet of the patch and did not turn onto it.

What she found out in those four days was that a place does not wait. That is the thing about a place. It does not hold still and let you come to it. It sits exactly where it is and the longer you leave it the more it is only a patch of stone and less it is anything else.

And on the fifth day she caught herself walking over to look at the north joint and then walking away from it again, and she stood in the middle of the kiln floor at the sixth hour and said out loud, to a furnace two hundred yards behind her:

“I am using it. That is what I am doing with it. I am walking over to look at a join in some stone because it is the only place in the world where I know where a person was, and there is not one other thing I can do with it.”

## The Offer At The Board

The man of fifty-one put it to her at the seventh hour of the third, with the board in his hand and the figures for the day on it, and he was not being kind and he told her so before he started.

He said, “You have been on this ground seventy-nine days and you cannot say what is on it.”

She said, “I can say what is on it.”

He said, “You can say what a barrow weighs. You cannot say where the pit is, or which end the water comes in at, or what is under the west end of the kiln wall, or how far it is from the mouth to the gate, and you have walked across all of it and you have not kept one foot of it.”

She said, “I have kept the fence.”

He said, “You have kept the fence.”

She said, “Ninety-four paces the long side and fifty-eight the short.”

He said, “And you have never been north of the low houses or east of the trough, and there is half this ground you have not put your foot on at all, and you have been paid a fifth of a man’s rate for seventy-nine days and I have never once seen you go and look at anything that was not in front of your nose.”

She said, “Say the thing you are actually asking.”

He said, “I will walk it round with you on a day and say what everything is, and it will take two hours, and you will have the ground in your legs the way the rest of us have it, and then you can stand on your nine paces by six knowing where it is in all of it and not only where it is in a floor.”

He waited.

She said, “No.”

He said, “Say why, and say it plain, because you will be asked again.”

## Why She Said No

She said it standing at the board with the figures on it, in the ordinary voice, and it took her a minute and she got it out in four parts.

“One. You have been on this ground nineteen years and you cannot tell me which nine paces it is. You said so yourself and you said it to a woman kneeling on it, and you were right, and I am not going to take the one thing about this place that you cannot do and have it done for me in two hours with a hand on my shoulder.”

“Two. If you walk it with me I will keep the ground and I will not keep the walking.”

“And the walking is the only part of me that still works at anything. Four fingers and a thumb on one hand. Eight of eleven holds. Four counts of breath that go to seven on a rise and stay there. I have been carrying a bar nine feet nine for nine days at a fifth of a man’s rate and not one person on this ground has asked me how my arm is, and I am not asking.”

“And if you walk it with me then this ground is a thing that happened to somebody else and I watched it.”

“Three. A person who is walked round a ground ends up owning a description of it. I have had nine of those. I do not want a tenth.”

“Four. I want to have gone there.”

She said that last one and then stopped, because it was the true one and the other three were the reasons she had built round it.

He said, “That is four reasons for a yes and a no in it.”

She said, “The fourth one is the no.”

## The Route She Worked Out

She got it out of the man of seventy-one on the fourth, at the seventh hour, at twenty feet, standing in the open the way she was told to, and he gave it to her in pieces and did not give it to her as a favour.

Two acres inside a line of stone. The gate on the south-west. The trough nine steps from where she sleeps. The stack at the west. The mouth of the furnace in the brick at the west end with the iron going in and out of it all day. The kiln floor: the slope off the east, four paces down, nine paces along the north side, and all of it two hundred and forty paces long at the working edge.

And then the rest of it, which is the part no person counts: the west end of the kiln wall where the ninety-one courses are in the second course from the top. The heap of ash off the kiln floor by the north wall. The low houses, thirteen of them, along the north side. The bench at the north end and a low house with no door on the south side of it. The store of the ground with the iron and the spare brick. And the gate.

She walked all of it once at the seventh hour of the fifth, at a walk, with the boy of fifteen, and it took her fifty minutes, and the boy of fifteen did not say one word the whole way and looked at her the entire time.

## What Her Feet Have To Do

The furnace is drawn at the seventh and banked at the ninth and it is not let out, and between the second and the fourth hour of the afternoon the ground is the hottest part of the day.

Two acres of it. Two hundred and forty paces long. Fifty minutes at a walk with nothing on her and she has not walked fifty minutes without a bar since she was twelve years old and she had two hands then.

And four counts of breath that go to seven on a rise and stay at seven, and a stone lip off the west end nine feet high, and a furnace mouth on that same west end that has been worked nineteen years without once being let out.

She said all of it out loud at the sixth hour of the sixth, at the gate, standing, in daylight, with the ash going up on her left and the smoke going straight up on her right and not one person on that ground within nine feet of her.

He said, “You have worked out that you will not get through it.”

She said, “I have worked out that I will get through it and that I will not get out of it well, and that the getting out of it badly is a thing I am going to have to do and not talk about afterwards.”

## The Day She Named

She said it at the seventh hour of the seventh of the month beginning 1,934, at the gate, with the bar across her right shoulder and her right arm open and the stone line going round two acres in the dark behind her.

She said, “The twenty-first. The sixth hour, when the ground is hottest and there is not one person out on it but the men at the mouth, because they shift at the sixth and there are three of them and they will be at the west end and I will be at the east end and I will not have to say one word to anybody.”

“I am going to walk it. From the gate. Up the east slope, four paces down, nine paces along the north side, across the nine by six, along the rest of the floor to the west end, and back. And the bar stays at this wall and the bag stays at this gate and I am going to do it with the two hands I have got and the breath I have got.”

He said, “Say the part that is hard.”

She said, “I am not going to stop on the nine paces.”

He said, “Why.”

She said, “Because it is a place, and I have been eighty-three days on a ground learning what a place does to a person, and I watched myself walk over to look at a join in some stone four times in a day and walk away again, and if I stop on it I will be a person standing where a person was, and that is the end of me as an instrument.”

She said, “I want to know what he was. I am not going to get it by standing where he was. And I have got a place now, and a place is the one thing in the world you can walk off.”

She was lighter at the tenth hour of the seventh of the month beginning 1,934 than she was at the fifth hour of it, which is three hundred and eighty days of it, counting the eighteenth of the month beginning 1,544 as the first, and she had named a day fourteen days off and had not yet told one person on that ground that she was going to do anything on it.
