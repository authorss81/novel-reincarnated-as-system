# What Eighty Days Comes To

She was at the foot of the stack at the second hour of the fourth of the month beginning 1,994 with the bar laid across her knees and the stone line standing up out of the black on her left, and there was frost in the strap where it had been bound out of her own coat lining, and she had to break the frost with the heel of her hand before it would go on a shoulder.

The stage comes on this ground today. She has known that for eighty days.

She had done the count before she got up and she did it again sitting down in the grit with her palm flat on the stone.

Eighty days since the fourteenth of the month beginning 1,904. Seventy-five days before that one. Seventy before that one.

And then she said the rest of it out loud, to the ash and the cold, in the ordinary voice, because it had been standing at the back of her head since the second hour and she counts.

“The gaps are getting longer.”

Three stages in two hundred and twenty-five days, and every one of them further off than the last, and not one rule in nine years has told her that the next gap is the length of the last one, and not one rule has told her there is going to be a next one at all.

He said, “You have been carrying that since yesterday afternoon.”

She said, “I have.”

He said, “Say all of it and then stop, because there are two men forty feet off you who will think you have lost something.”

## What Eighty Days Is Made Of

She went through the eighty while the light was coming, sitting on the low wall at the north end with her feet in the grit and the frost going off the stone.

Thirty-one of them she had spent on the kiln floor with a bar of fire out of the pit at a fifth of a man’s rate.

Nineteen of them she had spent counting the courses of a kiln wall with a man of fifty-one who wrote down every figure she gave him and asked her for one back exactly once in five months.

Fourteen of them had gone on a question about a mouth nineteen years shut and a book that had been shut as long. And the rest of them had gone on sitting at a gate, or walking the two miles of flat with nothing between the gate and the wall to lie down on, or lying on stones with a bar under her head.

Nineteen years of a boy she never met. She had that too, on its own line, where she kept it.

That was the arithmetic of eighty days and she had it to the day and the sound did not come out of it.

And then she did the other one, which was the one that had kept her awake in the last four days of the month beginning 1,964, and she said it out loud to the wall because there was no one else on the ground awake at the second hour.

“Seventy. Seventy-five. Eighty. And no one has ever told me what the spacing is for, and I have assumed for four days that it is the layer getting slower, and if that is what it is then the ninth is the last one and there is a thing after the ninth that never happens.”

She said, “Or the spacing means nothing at all and the ninth is the last one because nine is the end of the count.”

She said, “Or I have three more of them at eighty-five and ninety and ninety-five and I will be forty years old on the ground where I am standing and none of this will matter.”

He said, “Those are three things and you cannot tell them apart.”

She said, “No. And that is the first time in three days I have said out loud that I cannot tell them apart, and I have said it to a wall at the second hour, which is where I say the things I cannot hold.”

## What She Asked The Man With The Board

She found him at the third hour at the long window in the low, with the cloth in his hand and the board on the nail behind him, and she had not asked him for anything since the fourteenth of the month beginning 1,904 and she stood four feet off and did not ask him for anything.

What she asked him for was a line in his hand a thing that had not happened yet.

“A stage is going to be taken out of me this morning before the light is properly up,” she said, “and there is no sound in it and there is not going to be any, and the ground does not know it happened and the boy will be calling the first figure of the day by the time it has.”

He said, “That is the fourth thing you have said to me in five months that I have not got a column for.”

She said, “I know. Write it outside the columns.”

He said, “I do not write outside the columns.”

She said, “Then put it at the top of the board with the day against it and nothing under it, and write it in your hand and not mine.”

He put the brush down square with the edge of the bench.

“What do you want the line to say.”

She said, “That a stage was taken at the tenth hour of the fourth of this month at the north end of this yard, in the open, with the ground looking at it, and that the person it was taken out of is standing at the west end of the yard and can say so if anybody asks her in nine years.”

He said, “That is a long line.”

She said, “It is the true length of it.”

He said, “You want it in a hand that is not yours.”

She said, “Yes.”

## Why She Wanted A Second Hand

She told him why, standing in the grit, in daylight, to one man with a board, and she told it short because it was four sentences and it had taken her four days.

“Everything I have ever learned about what he was, I learned out of my own head, and my head is the only copy of it in the world, and it is nineteen years old and it belongs to a person with a hole in her chest.”

He said, “And if you stop.”

She said, “Then it stops. And if it stops in the wrong place then a thing that was made to find people like me goes on being what it was made to be and no one has written down that it is.”

He said, “You are asking me to hold it.”

She said, “I am asking you to write one line on a board in a hand that is not mine, and it will be no use to anybody for nine years, and that is what I have got.”

He said, “I have had a board up every day for nineteen years with a figure on it that came from somewhere I have never been able to find out.”

She said, “Then we are in the same trade.”

## What Went On The Board

He wrote it at the fourth hour and ten minutes, standing at the long window with the brush in his right hand, and he wrote it the way he wrote the figures, square, and he did not hurry it, and she watched all of it go down.

*Stage taken. Tenth hour of the fourth. North end of the yard, in the open, ground looking on. A person present who can say so.*

He squared the board with the back of his hand and hung it on the nail where anybody could see it and went back to the columns.

Then he asked her one question, which was the first one he had asked her in five months and it was not for the work.

He said, “Why the tenth hour.”

She said, “Because the seventh hour is the hour I give to questions and it has been the hour I give to questions since the twenty-fourth of the month beginning 1,814, and I am not going to let the ninth take it as well.”

He said, “That is a small reason to put a stage in the middle of a working day.”

She said, “It is the only reason I have got.”

He said, “Then it is a better reason than most of the ones on that board.”

## The Hour She Chose

She worked the morning out at the fifth hour and she chose it in the middle of it, standing at the west end of the yard with the furnace going over behind her and the floor full and the boy of fifteen coming down the stack with his first figure of the day in his mouth.

The tenth hour is the end of the working day and it is the hour the board goes up and it is the hour this ground is fullest.

She said it out loud to the yard at the ninth hour, in the ordinary voice, standing, with a bar of fire out of the pit in her left hand and her right arm hanging, because she was not going to have it happen in front of one person out of thirteen.

She said, “At the tenth hour a thing is going to happen to me that will not make a noise and will not be on anybody’s board but mine and his, and I have told him to write it, and I am telling you now so that when the day ends and the board goes up you will have seen the day.”

He said, “You are telling thirteen people about a hole you have got.”

She said, “I am telling thirteen people that the day is going to have one thing in it that I have not written down anywhere else.”

And then she stood at the west end of the yard and waited for the tenth hour with her hands at her sides and the furnace making noise behind her the whole time, and she was lighter at the fifth hour of the fourth of the month beginning 1,994 than she was at the second hour of it, which is four hundred and thirty-seven days of it, counting the eighteenth of the month beginning 1,544 as the first, and not one of them going back.

And at the ninth hour and forty minutes she was still standing there, and the day had one hour left in it, and she had no idea at all what was going to come out of the ninth stage, and she had asked for it in writing, on a board, in a hand that was not hers, which was the only thing she had ever done in ten years that was not a count.
