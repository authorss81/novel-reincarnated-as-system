# Six Days Of One Question

She set herself a test on the nineteenth, at the gate, with her hand on the bar, and she wrote it out because she does not trust a thing she has not written down and has never once trusted a thing she has only thought.

Five questions. One a day. The twentieth to the twenty-fourth. On the back of the board, in her left hand, with a stub of a nib she has to grip sideways because the index on that side is gone.

She wrote them at the seventh hour of the nineteenth after the man of fifty-one had gone in, and she did not read them again until the morning of the twenty-fifth, and she was going to ask each one of him at the seventh hour on the day it was for, in daylight, standing, one at a time, in the terms, because that is all she has got and she is not going to break them to find out whether they still work.

Then she put the board on the wall face down.

## The First Three Days

The twentieth she asked him how many courses were in the second year along the top of the kiln wall, and he gave her ninety-one, and she had that one before she put the question out.

The twenty-first she asked him how many men were in the yard before the tenth came, and he gave her a number, and she wrote nothing down because there was nothing to write it on but the back of a board she had promised not to look at.

The twenty-second she asked him what the woman of forty-five was called before she was married, and he said he did not know and that it was not the kind of thing anybody on that ground was told, and she said that was fair, and that was the end of that day.

Three days. Three questions. And on the morning of the twenty-third she stood at the wall with the board face down and she turned it over and she read three lines in her own hand and she could say what each of them was about.

She could not say that she had asked any of them.

## Asking A Person Is A Different Thing

On the twenty-third she asked the boy of fifteen a question, because she wanted a comparison and she wanted it in daylight.

She said, “How many times a day does the water come.”

The boy said, “Three. It is not a question. It comes three times and it is the same three times.”

She said, “That is right.”

The boy said, “It is not a question, it is a tap.”

And that was all of it, and she stood there and was glad, which is not a thing she has been often in nine months.

Because she had asked him a question at the sixth hour of the twenty-third of the month beginning 1,904 and at the seventh hour she could put both halves of that exchange down in order: what she had said, what he had said, and the fact that she had said the first one.

A person leaves a mark. You can point at where a question went and there is a scuff on the air.

## Asking The Woman Of Forty-Five

On the twenty-second, in the last of the light, she went and stood at the low house where the woman of forty-five keeps a book she has not opened since the thirtieth of a month beginning 1,844.

She said, “What time does the fire get drawn.”

The woman said, “The seventh. It is drawn at the seventh and it is banked at the ninth and it is not let out.”

She said, “That is the one I wanted.”

The woman said, “That is a foolish question to walk a mile for.”

She said, “It is. I am checking a thing.”

The woman looked at her for a second longer than she ever has.

She said, “You have been sixty-seven days on this ground and you have not asked me one thing in sixty-seven days and I have noticed that every single day, and now you have come and asked me what time the fire is drawn.”

She said, “I know you noticed.”

The woman said, “Yes. And I am not going to tell you what I think about it, and I am not going to refuse you anything either, and both of those are the same thing coming out of my mouth and I have not worked out which is which.”

And she shut the door, and it was not a slam, and it was not unkind, and she stood on the step and did not go after her.

## The Fourth Day

On the twenty-fourth she asked him the fourth question and she got as far as the end of it and stopped.

She stood at the west end of the yard in daylight with the windbreak behind her and she got the whole question up out of the place it comes from and she could feel it in her mouth ready to go, and then it was out, and then for the count of four she had no idea whether she had said it or whether she had only finished thinking it.

She said nothing else that day. She did not ask it again to find out, and that was not restraint.

It was the terms. One at a time. A question put twice is a different act, and it is the one thing she has carried since the eighth of a month ten volumes back and has never once pulled, and I am not going to start pulling it to settle a question about my own head.

So she stood there for the better part of a minute with a question she might or might not have asked.

He said nothing at all.

She said, at the seventh hour and twenty minutes, out loud, to a yard:

“I do not know if I asked him that.”

And there was not one person in that yard who could tell her, and that is what the eighth stage did, and it is not a thing a person can be warned about in advance.

## The Fifth Morning

She was on the stones outside the gate at the fifth hour of the twenty-fifth with the second night running of the same thing in her head, and she got up off the stones with her right arm open and the bar off her shoulder and walked to the north end and started the day’s work.

And when the boy called the first figure at the sixth hour she found that she had a question ready.

She did not know when she had made it. She could not have said afterward whether she had asked it that morning or built it on the wall two days before or carried it up out of a dream, which is a thing that has happened to her twice in ten years and both times were about a number.

She said it out loud, to the stack, at the sixth hour and ten minutes.

“I have been carrying a question and I cannot tell you which morning I made it in, and it is a true question, and I am going to ask it. And if it has already been asked then I have asked it twice, and I am not going to find out.”

## What She Is Left With

She counted it on the back of the board at the seventh hour of the twenty-fifth, with the lines in her own hand covered by her own hand, and it took her ten minutes and she got the same answer at the end of it.

Five questions on the board.

Five days.

Five answers, all of them true, all of them things she can say out loud tomorrow and the day after that.

And not one of them attached to a moment when she opened her mouth.

She said it to the yard at the seventh hour and thirty minutes, and then she said the part of it that was about her and not about the instrument.

“I have been frightened for five days. Not of the furnace and not of the ground and not of the thing at the sixth hour. Of being nineteen years old and alone on two acres inside a line of stone with the only person in the world who has ever known I was in the room, and not being able to prove that he is in it.”

She said, “And I am not going to be comforted by the fact that I put a question down on a board in my own hand five times.”

## What She Decides To Do About It

She turned the board over and put it face down on the wall again and she stood looking at the low houses along the north side of that yard, which is thirteen buildings and a yard and a furnace and a gate, and she counted them the way she has counted everything since she was eight.

Ten men and two women and a boy of fifteen and a man of fifty-one with a board and a woman of forty-five with a shut book.

And she said, in the ordinary voice, at the ninth hour, to the man of fifty-one, who was the only one still outside and who did not stop writing while she said it:

“You write down what comes up a wall. You do not write down where a thing happened, and it has never occurred to you to, because on this ground everybody knows where things happen.”

He said, “That is right.”

She said, “Then I need a person who writes down places, and there is not one of you, and there is exactly one other person alive who ever wrote one down on this ground, and I have been seventy-one days here and I have not once asked whether he is still on it.”

He put the nib down and looked at her for the first time in nineteen days, which was not a long look, because he is a man of fifty-one and does not spend looks.

He said, “He is at the north end. A low house with a bench in front of it and no door on the south side. He is seventy-one and he has kept the book of this ground since before I came to it. He does not come out before the seventh hour, and he does not speak to a person he has not worked with.”

She said, “What is he called.”

He said, “He is called Accountant, the way everybody on this ground calls him, and he has not been called anything else since before the fire was drawn. And I would not go at the sixth hour. Go at the seventh, and let him see you from twenty feet before you are close enough to talk.”
