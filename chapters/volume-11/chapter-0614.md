# Asking Twice

The light came up on the twenty-eighth of the month beginning 1,814 at the fifth hour and she was already standing on the stones of the turn with the bar on the ground at her feet, and the woman of sixty was already on the bench thirty feet off, and the board four hundred yards east was bare.

She had one question and four hours.

## What She Ruled Out

She did it out loud on the stones in the cold, one at a time, because she counts and because she would rather hear a thing rejected where she is standing than carry it into the hour.

She cannot ask after the six. He gave her a number at the seventh hour of the nineteenth of a month beginning 1,784 and it was six and it was not her brother’s name, and it was a true number and she has it.

She cannot ask after her own entry. It is in a shut book as *lost*, no shut book can take it out, and asking does not open a door — she worked that out on a bank four feet off a barrow and has not found a way round it since.

She cannot ask after the wall. He opened it once, on her, from the inside, at her order, knowing the number of one and not whose it was, and he is on the wrong side of it now and for a long time, and she will not spend the only hour she has on a thing that is finished and is not what she is owed.

She cannot ask after the twenty-two. It came back on the twentieth of the month beginning 1,814 on a flat on the far side of a wall with no unit on it, she does not know what it counts, it is the one thing she would most like to put a name to, and it is not owed to her. It is only wanted.

She cannot ask why he let her ask.

That is five and there were more and none of them held.

## What She Is Owed

She worked the rest of it out sitting on the kerb by the trough at the fifth hour and forty minutes and it took her twenty minutes.

Here is what is owed.

Not the whole of it. He said one at a time and he said in daylight and she is going to keep both.

The first thing is the smallest thing. And the smallest thing is not what he was and it is not what he was called and it is not why there is a hole in her chest.

A person who has been in your head for eight years owes you one ordinary day of his life.

Not the harvest. Not the morning it went wrong. Not the name. A day.

So: what did he do, on an ordinary day, before he ever began counting anything at all.

And the way she knows that is the right one, and not a way she has been talked into, is that it is the first thing she has ever wanted to know about anybody, including herself, and she has been a counter since she was eight years old and has never once in ten years wanted to know what a day in a person’s life was like.

She wanted figures. She wanted what a day in a person’s life was like at the ninth hour of the twenty-third of the month beginning 1,814, standing on a turn, after a man of forty-five told her six sentences about a boy who weighed lime.

## The Lever In The Open

She took it out in her own head at the sixth hour in the open and looked at it, and she is putting down that she did it in the open and not in the dark, because she is not going to do a thing like that behind herself again.

The words are the words. What were you before you started counting. She said them once, out loud, alone, at the ninth hour of the twenty-fourth of a month beginning 1,814, and he is the other one who has got them.

If she says them again she has asked twice.

That is the lever. It is the only thing in the world that would make him get to the first answer sooner, and it is the only thing that would make the first answer never arrive at all, because a question asked twice is not a question.

She stood on the stones in daylight with it in her hand.

He said, “Say it again and I will have to answer it tonight.”

She said, “I am not going to.”

He said, “I know you are not. I am telling you the price so that you have chosen it.”

## Why She Is Not Going To

She said it out loud on the turn and the woman of sixty was thirty feet off and did not look up, and she said it for that reason and not for the turn.

“A thing you can ask twice is a thing you can be refused twice,” she said.

And then she said, “And on the ninth hour of the twenty-fourth you told me that you do not answer a thing twice, and I have never once in eight years found you wrong about a figure, and I am not going to start finding you wrong about that one on the morning I need it.”

He said, “That is two reasons and the second one is the true one.”

She said, “Yes.”

He said, “I am not going to correct you about it.”

## The Second Time She Has Said A Number Out Loud And Been Agreed To

Here is the part of the twenty-eighth of the month beginning 1,814 that is not about the question.

At the sixth hour she stood on the stones and said out loud, in the ordinary voice, to the woman of sixty thirty feet off and to a bar on two posts four hundred yards east with nothing chalked on it:

“I am going to have one question answered by a thing in my head in daylight on a turn on nine days of road, and I am going to say it out loud, and there is a carter on this ground every nine days and I cannot time him, and I am not going to try.”

That is two things said out loud where two people could hear, and one of the two people did not move.

And then she did a thing she has not done since the fifteenth of the month beginning 1,634, which is to ask the air for a number and wait for one, the way a girl asks a floor.

She said, “What is the hour at which I stop.”

She meant the hour at which she has to stop waiting and start asking, and she said it the way she says a figure to a man with a chalk, out loud, and clearly, and twice.

Nothing came back. Not a refusal. Nothing, the way a floor does not answer.

And she stood there in the daylight on a turn for a minute with her mouth shut, feeling the shape of a thing that has been in her head for eight years and that she has just discovered she is not obliged to address at all.

She put it away. She is not going to spend the hour before the question on a fight with the thing that is going to answer it.

And she said out loud, to the road: “I am not going to do that again today.”

## What She Is Asking With

She put her hand on the bag and on the book inside the bag and she knew exactly what was in her hand and it was not a question.

Six sentences. A boy who weighed lime before it went in, and nobody paid him, and his father fired, and a boy who counts what nobody is paying him to count is a boy the furnace will use, and it is not the same as keeping, and it happened without anybody meaning it and not one man there was cruel.

Given to her at a turn by a man of forty-five who wanted nothing for them.

That is what she is asking with. A thing a stranger said to her about a stranger, for nothing, which is the only thing any person has ever handed her that did not have a price on it, and she has had it in her hands since the ninth hour of the twenty-fourth and it has not faded once.

## What She Did Not Do For Twenty-Two Days

She made herself put it in one place before the hour, because she has learned in nine days that a thing carried sideways gets spent sideways.

Eleven days of standing at a turn after the eighteenth of the month beginning 1,814. Four hundred yards east to a board. Eight miles west on a bad arm. Nine coppers she cannot count, reduced to one. A shut door she did not open. A brother going west. Six sentences from a man of forty-five. One question on a stone face about a day in a person’s life.

That is everything she has spent since the eighteenth of the month beginning 1,814, and it came out of two hundred and eighty-one days of going lighter and one arm, and it got her one question.

She said that out loud on the stones at the sixth hour of the twenty-eighth of the month beginning 1,814, and then she said the sentence that is the whole of the decision.

“I am not going to go back and do any of it better,” she said. “I am going to ask one question at the seventh hour and I am going to take what I am given and I am not going to go and count any of it again.”

He said, “That is the first thing you have ever said that I have believed the whole of immediately.”

She said, “Because it is true and I have checked it.”

## The Terms Said Aloud One Last Time

“One at a time,” she said, on the stones, at the sixth hour of the twenty-eighth of the month beginning 1,814, in daylight.

“In daylight.”

She did not write them down. She said them once and she left them.

Then she picked up the bar and carried it a hundred yards off the road to the broken ground on the north side of the turn, where there is nothing to lie down on and nothing to see a person by, and set it down again, and turned round and faced the road.

There is a carter on nine days of road every nine days and she could not time one.

She went and sat down on the kerb by the trough to wait out the last half hour and she found out that she was not frightened any more, which she had not expected, and that the thing in the place of being frightened was a kind of flatness and an enormous want to be finished.
