# The Four Things That Were True

She read all seven of them out loud at the sixth hour of the twenty-eighth of the month beginning 2,024, in daylight, in a lit room, at the end of the bench, with the light on them and a boy of sixteen at the rack four feet away.

She had worked out the arrangement overnight and it took her twenty minutes and it is the only thing she planned in ten years that worked the first time.

A page read alone at the fourth hour goes into the person who reads it. That is what happened to her and it took three days and four readings and it is not reversible.

A page read out loud in daylight in front of one other person goes into both of them.

And there is not one other person in four miles. There is a woman in a square who sells bread at a rate and a carter and a woman from a door at the back of a tower, and not one of the four of them has anything to do with a register.

So it was the boy of sixteen, and she had not asked him to be there, and she did not lower her voice.

## What The Boy Said About Being In The Room

He put his brush down when she started and he did not pick it up again for a minute and a half, and he said, at the end of it, without any weight on it at all:

“You could have done that in the last four days.”

She said, “I could have.”

He said, “You waited until the sixth hour.”

She said, “I waited until the sixth hour because a man told me not to do it at the fourth and he is not here to be told that he was right, and the only way I was going to find out whether he was right was to do it in the morning in the light.”

He said, “Was he right.”

She said, “No.”

He said, “Then he was wrong about that and he was right about the other thing.”

She said, “Say which other thing.”

He said, “That you should not be alone in a room when you read it. He was right on that and he was not right on the hour, and both of those are about being alone in a room, and I have been in this room two years and I have never been alone in it in the morning.”

## The Four Things That Were True

She worked them out on the bench with the four sheets squared in front of her at the seventh hour of the twenty-eighth, in the order they came, because the boy was there and because a thing said in a lit room with a witness in it is a thing that has happened twice.

One. He was the fourth piece of a thing that had a name, and he was burned out of it at nineteen at a fire in the east, and what woke up was the piece of him that was not burned.

She said, “That is not a soul and it is not a man and it is not nothing. It is a piece that is still the same piece and has been nineteen years.”

Two. The thing he came off is not gone.

She said that one twice, out loud, in the room, because it is the one she had not been ready for at all and it took her two goes.

She said, “It is not gone. It has not said one word in nineteen years and it is not hiding and there is no hall and there is no room with a door and there is not one person on this ground or any other who has ever been told about it, and that is not because it is keeping itself quiet. It is because there is no one who passes anything on.”

Three. The levy is not for the ground.

She said, “One in twenty. Counted in days. It comes off a board before the board goes up and no one on that ground has ever been told their own total, and it was made to a figure by somebody who chose the figure, and it has never changed anywhere, and it is not for the ground and it is not for the ground’s work and it is not for the county. It is for a kind of person.”

“And I worked that out on a furnace ground with a stick in the grit in front of thirteen people and I said it out loud in a yard, and a clerk in a tower has written down that it is true and has written it in a hand that goes up no stair.”

Four. The name is in two places and neither of them needs her.

She said, “A leaf against my ribs in my own hand, with a date above it and a place above that, and a cloth book on a plank table at the north end of a furnace ground in a good hand that a man of seventy-one has never read back. Two hands, nineteen years apart, and they agree, and if I stop tomorrow the other one is still right.”

She said, “That is four. And I have been carrying them apart since I was twelve.”

## What Was On The Sheet That Was Not About Either Of Them

There was a fifth thing on the fourth sheet and it was the only part of it she could not lay on the bench, and she found it on the second reading and then found it again on the fourth.

A tally.

It is not in words. It is a column of short strokes down the right-hand margin of the fourth sheet, and there are forty-one of them, and the hand that made them is the hand that made the sentences, and he has not written one word about what they are.

She said, out loud, at the ninth hour of the twenty-eighth of the month beginning 2,024, standing at the end of the bench with a boy of sixteen in the room: “Forty-one.”

The boy said, “Forty-one of what.”

She said, “I do not know. And he did not write one word about it, and I have read that sheet four times and there is no line under the column and no heading and no name, and a clerk who has written announcements for twenty-two years put forty-one strokes down in a fair hand and then wrote a seventh sentence about carrying a person through a wall and stopped.”

She said, “He saw a tally and he did not know what it was and he wrote it down anyway, because that is what the hand is for.”

She said, “And I am not going to go and find out. Not this month and not on a road to a post with a tower on it, and not by asking him, because I cannot ask him anything and because a person who has been shown forty-one strokes by a man who trusted her is not going to walk nine days to find out what they were by asking somebody else in an ordinary voice.”

The boy said, “Then what are you going to do with them.”

She said, “Carry them. That is what I do. I have been carrying a number that is not mine since I was twelve years old and this is the first one that somebody else saw before I did.”

## What She Said About The Fifth Item In His Column

At the sixth hour of the twenty-ninth of the month beginning 2,024 she went to the second shelf with the boy of sixteen behind her and put her hand on the spine of the volume and told him one thing, standing in daylight, with her hand on the book.

She said, “That is his own and it is not indexed and it goes up with the quarter and no one has ever sent for it, and it is going to go up with the quarter for as long as there is a quarter.”

The boy said, “Should it be indexed.”

She said, “No.”

The boy said, “Then it cannot be known to be there.”

She said, “Then nobody will know it is there and that is exactly what a clerk wanted when he walled it behind four county copies and it is the only thing either of us is going to say about it in a room.”

The boy said, “And what do I do with the register.”

She said, “Copy what is on the rack. Put the days in the column and the days go up on the first. Do not ask me anything else on it, because I have no standing whatever in this building and a girl with a bar in a tower is the worst thing that has ever happened to a quarter’s bundle.”

And he said, “All right,” and went down four flights with a bundle that was not a bundle, and came back up empty, and that is what the stair does.

## The First Of The Month Beginning 2,054

She was on the last rise west of the town at the eighth hour of the first of the month beginning 2,054, with the bar on her shoulder and four sheets of a fair hand folded four times inside her coat against her ribs on the side where the book is, and a book of eight leaves with five of them blank.

And she stopped on the rise, because a thing had arrived while she was walking and it had arrived without a sound, the way everything in this life has arrived.

She said it on a road, standing, in daylight, to an empty wall.

“There is a piece of a thing with a name that a clerk would not write down, and it was made to find people like me, and I am nineteen and I am nine stages past a ceiling that no longer exists, and a clerk in a tower with a lamp in a cage is dead with a fair hand four feet from where he sat, and all of it is true, and I read it, and no one told me any of it.”

She said, “Forty-one strokes in a margin that a man saw and would not write a word about, and I am carrying them, and I am not going to find out what they are.”

He said, “Say what you are going to do about it.”

She said, “That is the thing I have been walking on for three days and I have not got an answer to it.”

He said, “Then you will say it when you have got one.”

She said, “No. I am going to say it before I have got one, because I have worked out in the last four days that everything I have learned in ten years I learned alone at the fourth hour and that is why not one of it ever got to anybody, and the man who taught me that was a clerk who wrote it down in a hand that goes up no stair.”

She said, “And a thing said once to one man in a head is not a thing that has happened. I have been told that in a yard on a furnace ground and I did not like it and he was right.”

She said, “So I am going to stand still in daylight, in a room, with one other person in it who has not been asked, and say it once, and it is going to be about me and there is not going to be anybody in the room who can answer it.”

She said, “And I am not saying it here. A road is not a room.”

And she walked west down the rise with the bar on her shoulder and four sheets of a fair hand against her ribs, and the next hour was going to be the sixth hour of the second of the month beginning 2,054, and there were four flights of stair between her and the only room in four miles that had a lamp in a cage in it and a person in it who had not been asked.
