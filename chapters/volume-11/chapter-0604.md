# The Mile She Did Not Walk

She walked west at the fifth hour of the twenty-first of the month beginning 1,814 with two hours and forty minutes of sleep behind her and the bar on her left shoulder, because a brother of nineteen had gone up this road at the dusk of the seventeenth with nothing on him and she had watched him go and had not gone with him and had not stopped him.

Four miles. A cottage with a shut door at the mouth of a lime bed that stopped nine years back.

## What She Went Out For

Not to follow him. She wrote the two words out in the dust at the turn before she picked the bar up so that she would not be able to pretend later that she had not.

Not to follow him.

She went west because he went west. There is a difference between those two things that has cost her nine years, and on the twenty-first she wanted to walk a road for once with nothing at the far end of it.

And because there was nothing else to do with a day. Twelve days of standing at a turn and she is on the fourth of them, and the board does not go down for eleven more, and the bar weighs nine feet nine and she has not carried it a mile since the month beginning 1,754.

## Four Miles Of Store Road

The store road runs four miles west from the turn to the cottage. It is a made road for two of them and then it is a cart track with lime in it, and the lime went hard nine years back and it is still there in the ruts and it is still white, and she did not put her hand in any of it.

She counted the miles, because she counts, and she counted them twice and they came out right both times.

And she counted everything else as well, because that is what the road is for, and she got through the cart track with four hundred yards to go and she had not noticed the counting stop.

He said, at the second hour, “You are counting.”

She said, “I am.”

He said, “You counted four miles of that road on the seventeenth of a month beginning 1,754 going the other way with a man of nine hundred years and you have not made one figure since.”

She walked another mile and did not answer him and did not start again.

## The Shut Door

The cottage is of one room with a lime bed at the back of it and a bar across the door on the outside, and the bar has been on there so long that the door has bowed out around it and there is a gap at the bottom a finger wide where you can see the floor of the room is not there any more.

She stood in front of it for an hour and ten minutes, which is longer than she has stood at anything that has not been a wall.

He said, “You have never knocked.”

She said, “No.”

He said, “It has not been opened in nine years and it is not going to be opened by you.”

She said, “I am not going to open it.”

She stood there another forty minutes with her hand four inches off the bar and did not touch it, and when she walked away she had dirt in the four fingers and a thumb of her right hand and she did not touch the bar with them on purpose.

## What Was Not There

Here is what she went four miles to find and it is worth putting down in the order she looked.

A boot-print at the door. A scuff on the threshold. A stone moved. A thing stood against the wall where a person had been standing. Hair in the lime. A thread on a bar.

Six things, and she looked for six things, and a boy of nineteen who came past a shut door in the dark four days ago did not stop, and did not lean, and did not stand there, and did not touch anything, and she had watched him go up the four miles from the turn at four hundred yards and she had seen the shape of him the whole way and he had not once slowed down.

She stood in the road outside the cottage and worked out that she had come four miles to confirm a thing she had known since the dusk of the seventeenth.

He said it himself, at the third hour, in the ordinary voice. It is the only time in four days he has said anything about her brother.

“He did not stop because there was nothing here to stop for. He is going to a wheel that has not turned in nine years because there are people in it.”

She said, “You have never asked me a thing about him.”

He said, “No.”

She said, “Is that kindness.”

He said, “That is a thing you would have worked out in a mile and I have saved you the mile.”

## Why She Turned Round At The Door

The mill is one mile further on. There is a man of seventy in it and a wheel that has not turned in nine years and it is the only place west of this road with a person in it, and he said on the dusk of the seventeenth that he had not been in one of those either.

She stood at the shut door and she wanted that mile so badly that her chest went cold, and she worked out where it came from, and it was not from wanting to see the mill.

It was from wanting to be the kind of sister who follows.

And she has one brother and she found him four days and a night ago at the first hour of the first of a month and got one sentence out of him with a door in it, and she is not going to walk into a mill at dusk on the twenty-first because a man of seventy is in it and the two of them would have to speak.

So she turned round on the stones outside a cottage she did not open, one mile short of him, and went east.

That is the whole of the twenty-first and it cost her the day and it is the first thing she has chosen to do since she was ten that nobody else made her do.

## What She Said Out Loud At The Door

She said it at the shut door in daylight with the road behind her and not a soul on it for four hundred yards either way, because she says things out loud and has said every thing that has ever mattered to her out loud to an empty room, and the four that are on the second leaf of her own book are four she has read out loud exactly once.

“Here is where you walked,” she said. “The seventeenth, at dusk, past a girl standing on a turn. Four miles to a shut door and on to a mill. I watched you go the whole way and you did not look back.”

She put her four fingers and her thumb flat on the wood four inches from the bar.

“I am not going to open this door,” she said. “I am not going to the mill. And I am not going to say thank you to you for walking past me, and I am not going to say anything else to you on it either, because there is one thing I owe you and it is not on this road and I am not going to start spending it in a cart track.”

The bar was where it had been for nine years and the gap at the bottom was the width of her finger and there was a floor that was not there.

Then she took her hand off the wood and looked at the dirt on it and did not wipe it on anything.

## What She Is Going To Do About Bread

She worked that out on the mile east, sitting down on a kerb for ten minutes with the bar across her knees and the arm hanging.

Bread at the store is two coppers a loaf. The store is behind the mill at five miles west of the turn. She has nine coppers she cannot count and one number said out loud on the stones yesterday that is the only count of money that has ever been any good to anybody.

Three days of standing used. Eleven days to the next board. No wages since the second of a month beginning 1,754.

So on the twenty-second she is going to walk five miles west to a counter and put a number she cannot count down on it and walk away with bread, and the number she says will be the number she says, and if a woman behind a counter counts them and gets a different figure then there will be two figures in the world and she will have to keep hers.

She sat on the kerb and said that last part out loud twice, to a cart track, in the ordinary voice.

## The Fourth Mile Back

She got four miles east.

On the first mile the arm went and stayed gone. She got the bar onto her left shoulder at the second mile and the right arm hung and she carried the weight on four fingers and a thumb and the heel of her hand, and at the third mile she put the bar down on the stones in the road.

She stood in a cart track a mile east of a cottage with a shut door and looked at nine feet nine of stone lying in the dust and worked out that she was going to have to walk the rest of it and come back for it.

That took ten minutes and she could not have done it at the twenty-fourth of a month beginning 1,754.

She picked it up at the turn at dusk and got it onto her shoulder with her left hand and both legs, and she was lighter at the ninth hour than she was at the fifth, which is two hundred and seventy-four days of it, and she had walked eight miles and opened no door and learned nothing about a wheel.

He said, a quarter of a mile short of the turn:

“You have eight days of standing left.”

She said, “Nine.”

He said, “No. It is eight. You have been spending them since the eighteenth and you did not notice, because you do not spend days, you spend miles.”

She did not argue with it, and that is the first thing that has happened on nine days of road that she has not argued with, and she has thought about that all night.
