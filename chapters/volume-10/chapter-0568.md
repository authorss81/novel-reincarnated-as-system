# One Loaf And Twenty-Two Miles

She walked forty-four miles west in two nights with a third of a loaf and no sleep, and she got in at the second hour of the twenty-third of the month beginning 1,634 with nothing, and there was still no answer, and the road had got narrower.

## Forty-Four Miles With A Third Of A Loaf

She went off the metalled road a mile short of the gate at the ninth hour of the twenty-second, into the low broken ground on the north side, because a made road carries sound and she had nothing left and did not want to be on it.

She ate the last of the loaf at the eleventh mile at the second hour of the morning.

Then she had nothing, and she had eleven miles of made road and nine of strip road in front of her, and she had walked for thirty-four hours.

## The Made Road In The Night

Here is the whole night and it is not a thing she is going to forget.

She went on at a mile an hour, which is a third of what she does on that road in the day, and her fourth count went for four at the third mile out of the low ground and it did not come back.

It did not come back at nine. It did not come back at fourteen.

She went off the metalled road on her hands and knees into the broken ground on the north side with a bar across her shoulders and lay on her face in the stone dust with her mouth against the cold and counted it out loud to keep it in her head, twice, and the second time it went to twenty-two.

She lay there for four minutes and then she got up, and she wrote that down afterwards as the whole of it: she got up.

The arm was open and it stayed open for the last seven miles of the made road and it was open for the first two of the strip road after that, which is two days in five in one night and one day, and she did not shut it and did not try.

## The Second Hour Of The Twenty-Third

She came onto the flat at the second hour of the twenty-third of the month beginning 1,634 and the post was a mile ahead of her and she went to it.

She did not eat. There was nothing to eat and she has been on nine days of road for two months and the last bread she had was at the store on the nineteenth and she has had three-quarters of a loaf since.

She put the bar down and she did the count, because that is what she does, and it took her eleven minutes.

Four fingers and a thumb on the right hand. The right arm open and it is going to shut in its own time and she cannot hurry it. Eight of eleven holds. Four counts of breath, and seven on a made road in the dark at the second hour.

The small open place low behind her fourth rib is the size of a coin and is not going to close and nothing has come out of it since the twenty-sixth of the month beginning 1,604.

She is nineteen years old and she is lighter than she has ever been, and she is not hungry, and she is not going to be hungry.

She put her hand flat on the flat stone under the ladder and it was bare and she had put that there with her own two hands three days ago.

## Twenty-Nine Days

Here is the count and she did it sitting on the flat stone with her back against the ladder upright, on the twenty-third of the month beginning 1,634, and it is the first count she has done that was not about her body.

She asked what else is on it at the seventh hour of the twenty-fourth of the month beginning 1,604, in daylight, with a boy of fifteen four feet off.

That was twenty-nine days ago.

Since then she has stood in that opening six times. The twenty-fourth and the twenty-fifth together. The thirtieth, on her own, for a question that got the terms back. The eighth of this month, for an hour, in silence. The fifteenth, for a question about four letters that got the terms back. The twenty-first and the twenty-second of this month.

Two hundred and thirty-one miles of road.

And there are three questions in a book nine hundred years deep and not one of them has been answered, and one of them is holding the rest of it, and she cannot tell which one.

## The Road Has Narrowed

It narrowed on the made road at the fourth hour of the twenty-third and she did not notice it happening and worked it out later at the flat stone.

Twenty-two miles used to be a walk.

Now it is two days, and two days is a loaf and a half she has to earn at a face four miles west of a turn at the end of a fourteen-mile walk, and every day of standing at that wall takes a day of stone out of a body that got lighter twice in a month and has not put either of them back.

And there are three terms and the third one is the one that has got her.

In daylight.

She cannot do any of it at night. She cannot do any of it in bad weather.

She cannot ask it into the ground along a made road at the eleventh mile to a man who cannot hear it, and she worked that out on the thirteenth of a month beginning 1,604, and it has cost her four months and four walks to learn that the only place in the world she may say a thing is the place where somebody can hear her say it, and it is eleven miles from any bed.

That is what has narrowed. Not the road. Her.

## What She Did About The Lightness

She got bread at the store on the twenty-fourth of the month beginning 1,634 at the seventh hour, from a woman of fifty who said two coppers a loaf out loud, and she did thirty sacks off a cart onto a sill in an hour and a bit and her right arm went open at the twenty-second and stayed open for the last eight and she did all eight of them with the arm hanging and her back and her knees.

Her fourth count went inside a loaded reach on the twenty-second and took four counts to come back, and she stood on a store floor with a sack under her right arm that she could not put down for nine seconds because putting it down was going to take both hands.

Then she put it down.

She has been lighter every day since the eighteenth of the month beginning 1,544 and lighter still since the twenty-sixth of the month beginning 1,604, and she put her hand flat on her own side that evening at a bench outside a store and there was less of her than there had been on the second of a month that is gone.

And she worked all of it out that evening in eleven minutes, sitting on a bench with her back to a shuttered window, and it is the only arithmetic of her body she has ever been glad to do.

Nothing came out of her on the thirty sacks.

That is the finding and she got it at the ninth hour of the twenty-fourth of a month beginning 1,634 on a bench with a cloth round a loaf and a hand flat on her own ribs.

It came out of her on the eighth of a month beginning 1,604, on listed work cleared past the line, on a road gang. It came out of her again on the twenty-sixth of that month, on the same gang, on listed work cleared past the line. It has not come out of her once on any work that a store is paying a figure for.

She does not know what that means and she has been turning it over for a month and it has got no further than that, and she is not going to put it near a man who keeps a book of everybody’s numbers because she has not asked him for one thing in eight years and she is not going to start by putting a finding in front of him that she cannot finish.

## What She Has To Ask For Next

She worked it out at the sixth hour of the twenty-third, sitting in the dust at the foot of a ladder with nothing in her bag and a book of eight leaves against her ribs.

There is a day.

That is the whole of what she has. She has no month, no year, no number, and she cannot ask for any of them because she has three questions standing in a book nine hundred years deep and the first one of them is holding everything else shut.

So the only thing left to do is to get him to say it without asking him.

That is not clever and she is not proud of it. She has been counting since she was eleven years old and the only arithmetic left is to stand in an opening in a wall in daylight, on her own side, with a person hearing her, and not say one word, and go on not saying one word, and going back, until a man of nine hundred years decides to say a date to somebody who will not stop standing there.

## What She Is Going To Carry Into The Morning

She named him out loud on the flat at the foot of a ladder at the sixth hour of the twenty-third of the month beginning 1,634, with nothing in the hearing but a boy of eleven holding a board forty feet off, which is the only name she has ever said out loud in nineteen years and she said it to nobody.

Ning Wu, she said.

Then she got up and did not say it again, and she went thirteen miles west to break stone, and she told herself the way she has told herself things since the fourteenth of a month beginning 1,634 that a day is not a thing she can hurry.

And then she had to go and find out whether that was true.
