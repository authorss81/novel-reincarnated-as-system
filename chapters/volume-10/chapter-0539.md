# What Four People Do Not Say

She stood four feet off a ladder at a post on a strip road for the rest of the ninth hour, and she did not thank him, and nothing at all came of that.

## The Ninth Hour Going

The light was on the west side of the post and it came down the wood an inch at a time while she stood there with her hands off the strap of the bar and her bag against her right hip.

There were four other people on the flat ground. The keeper with the paste pot had his back to her and was doing the last of the board. The carter of thirty-five was holding a horse by the halter and letting it drink. The boy of eleven had a board against his knee and was not carrying it anywhere. And the man with the bag was nine feet off, standing on his weight.

She could have said one word and it would have been short. She had it in her mouth and she did not put it out.

## Nine Feet Off

Nine feet is a long way on a flat with nothing on it. From where she stood she could see his hands and the strap across his chest and the place where the bag pulled the cloth on one side and not the other.

He had not moved since he said it. He had not put his weight back and taken it up again, which is what a man does when he is going to walk off.

She waited for it for a quarter of an hour and it did not come, and she went back over the two sentences and she could not find anything in them that was an answer to anything she had asked.

That is the whole of what he gave her in a month. She had a name on a page four days old and four sentences in a book on a stone and twenty-two miles of ground between where she was standing and the room, and a man said I am here and said her name, and neither of those is a thing a person has to answer.

It is what you say to a dog that has come back.

She did not say that out loud. She thought it once, standing up, and then she put it away, because it was not true, and because she has had three conversations with that man in fifteen days and none of them were with a dog.

## The Rag On The Rail

The keeper came down the ladder backwards, three rungs, the way a man comes down a ladder he has been on for nineteen years, and he washed the rag in the trough and wrung it out over the dust and hung it on the rail.

He did not look at the stone under the bottom of the ladder. He had walked over it going up and coming down, both times, and his boot had been on the flat stone at the corner of it.

Ning Yan watched him do that and had to put her hand against the bar.

Three names under that ladder for nineteen years and no office behind any of them and a man who has never asked her what she put down there. If he had looked at it she would have had to stand still for it.

He put the pot down on the rail and went into the post and shut the door behind him without latching it, which is what a man does in his own building.

## The Boy And The Horse

The boy of eleven looked at the stone for a while and then at her, and she thought he was going to say something, and he did not.

He has been looked at by her twice in two years and has never asked her one question, and she has said four names out loud in her life and none of them was to him.

The carter finished with the horse and led it four paces east and let it go, and the horse stood and put its head down over the trough.

“Right,” the carter said.

That was to the horse, or to the morning, or to the general arrangement of a strip road at the ninth hour. He had said good to her four hundred times in two years and he was not saying it now, and she was glad of that in a way she did not have time to look at.

## What She Has To Say Back

Here is what she wanted for the whole of the hour and what she was not going to get.

She wanted him to go east.

Not in order to be rid of him. She wanted the twenty-two miles between that post and that wall to be twenty-two miles again instead of a thing with a man standing at one end of it, and she wanted the four days of a room with a table in it to be four days ago instead of four days that a man walked out of. She wanted him to be nine miles east and a door's length from the book and to have his own trouble with the thing he had done and not to bring it out to a trough on a strip road where it can be seen.

She had wanted that at the fifth hour yesterday and at the seventh and at the eighth, and she had wanted it enough to go out and look up the strip road twice for him.

And she could not say one word of it, because saying any of it out loud would have been thanking him, and she is not going to thank him, and she has not decided why and she is not going to today.

## The Tenth Hour

The sun came off the top of the board and the paste went grey on the wood and the trough had a skin on it that the horse had not broken.

Nobody left.

The keeper's door stood open on a dark inside and a man that had come out and gone to sleep in it. The carter sat down on the low side of the rail with the horse's rein over his knee. The boy went on holding a board he was not carrying anywhere.

And the man with the bag put his weight on the other leg and stood nine feet off a girl with a bar on her shoulder and a book of her own on a stone, and he did not say anything else, and he did not go.

She understood why they were all still there, and it was not for her, and it was not for him either.

There is nothing at the end of that hour for any of them to go to. A man with a rag goes to sleep. A carter waits for a load. A boy holds what he is holding. A man who has walked twenty-two miles in three days stands where he stands because the walking is finished and he has not thought of anything to do with himself.

And she stood there because she did not know what to do with herself either, which is the first time that has been the whole of a reason since she was eleven.

## What Her Hands Did

For eight years she has known exactly what her right hand does and it does not do it.

Four fingers and a thumb, and it cannot write a line that is not true, and it will not take a thing and it will not put a thing into anybody else's hands, and she has never once in eight years put a question into another person's hearing at a post or a counter or a rail.

That is not modesty. She has said no to men who were owed nothing and she has said it out loud in daylight and in front of witnesses, and she can do all of it.

What she cannot do is the other one. She has never in eight years gone to a man who keeps a thing and asked him what was in it.

And she is not going to find out today standing at a trough with a boy and a carter and a horse, and the man she would ask it is nine feet off with his hand on the strap of a bag.

## The Second Hour

It went past the tenth hour and then past the eleventh and the light went off the board and along the ground to the rail and stopped there.

At the eleventh hour the carter got up and took his cart round to the west side of the post because the ground under the rail is soft and the wheel goes in. The boy went with him. The trough got a skin on it again.

And the two of them were on the flat at the end of it, and the man with the bag, and the open door of the post with a man asleep in it, and she.

That is five. There has never been five at that post at once in her life and there is no reason on nine days of road that there should be.

She looked at the book on the flat stone under the bottom of the ladder with an inch of stone between it and the other one.

She did not pick it up.

## The Thing She Does Not Say

She is going to say thank you.

That is the finding and she got it at the eleventh hour standing in the dust four feet off a ladder, and it is not about him.

She has been standing here for two hours with nothing to refuse. In eight years every room she has stood in has had a thing in it with her name on it and a way of saying no, and she has said no in front of people and been right, and the no has never cost her anything she could not carry.

There is no no here. He is not offering her a thing. He is standing nine feet off on a flat road in daylight and he said her name, and there is nothing in that she can refuse, and she has been building her whole self for eight years out of refusing things.

She does not have the other half of that and she is nineteen and she is standing in the dust finding out.

So she is not going to thank him, because a thank you is the end of something between two people and she has not started anything, and she is not going to say his name again, because she said it once at the eighth hour yesterday in front of three people and one time is all a name is worth.

## What She Does Instead

She bent down and picked the dipper up off the trough rail and filled the skin and hung it back.

It took her four minutes and it was the first useful thing she had done since the fourth hour of the twenty-fourth of a month that has gone, and she did it because her hands needed a job and not because anybody asked her for water.

The keeper's door was open. She could have gone in. There is a bench inside that goes round three sides of a post and a man asleep on it and nobody in the room who would have stopped her.

She did not go in. She has not been inside that post in two years and she was not going to go in on the twenty-sixth of a month because there was nothing else to do.

So she stood four feet off a ladder on a strip road nine miles west of a turn, with a skin full and a bag against her hip and a book of eight leaves on a stone she did not put it on, and she waited out the rest of the day with four other people who had no reason to be there.

The sun went down off the rail at the second hour and the bar got cold and she did not thank him.
