# One Question At A Time

He named the terms at the seventh hour of the twenty-fifth of the month beginning 1,604, at a wall, in daylight, with nobody there but the two of them, and they did not move for as long as she had them.

## What He Said

She had slept on the flat ground four feet outside the opening, with the bar under her and the bag against her back, and she had been up since the fourth hour, and there was nobody within twenty-two miles of her and the light was coming up on the east side of the wall.

He did not answer the question she had put there the day before. He put three things down instead, the way a man puts a hand flat on a table, and he said them once each and he did not say them again.

“One at a time,” he said. “At the wall. In daylight.”

She stood on the dressed stone and waited for the rest of it, because she had gone away for three days on four words and there was no arrangement in her where that was the whole of what he had.

He was not going to say anything else about it, and she could see that he was not going to, and the not-going was not a way of keeping it from her. It was the terms.

## Saying Them Again

“Say them again,” Ning Yan said.

He looked at her for four seconds and then he said them again, in the same three parts and the same voice.

“One at a time. At the wall. In daylight.”

She said them back. That is the part she wants written down because she was alone in it and there was nobody to check her.

“One at a time,” Ning Yan said. “At the wall. In daylight.”

He put his hands back on the plank table and did not say anything, and she stood there for a minute working out whether that was agreement.

It was agreement. He is a man who keeps a thing for nine hundred years and he has told her the rule three times in the same words and he has not added a word to it, and there is no part of her that thinks he would have added one.

## Why Those Three

“Why those three,” she said, and then heard herself and understood what she had done, about a second too late.

He waited. He did not rush her and he did not help.

“Because that is how it is done,” he said, “and because I have kept it that way for nine hundred years and I am not going to change it for anybody standing at the front of it.”

That is the whole of the reason and it is the reason of a man who has never once needed a better one.

She stood there for about a minute afterwards working out what had just happened, because she had asked him a question and he had answered it, and the terms say one at a time.

The answer to what else is on it is still standing open in a book nine hundred years deep with a date on it. He has not given her that and it is there waiting for her and it will be there when she comes back with the next one.

What he had given her was his own reason, out loud, once, and whether that is in the terms or outside them is not a thing he said and is not a thing she can go back and ask about, because asking that is a question and questions are one at a time and the first one is still open.

So she does not know whether asking why is free.

That is going to sit on her for the rest of the month and she has worked out already that she is not going to be able to find out by asking, because there is no arrangement on this ground for finding out what a question costs until you have paid for it.

## What Is Not A Term

Then he told her the part that had not been a term, and he told it to her without being asked, which she wrote down against him afterwards as the only thing he did that day that she did not have a price for.

“A question you ask me goes in the book,” he said. “The same as a name. It is a thing that has happened and it is on the list of things that have happened and I do not have the rest of it while there is one standing open.”

“You said you would answer it.”

“I said nothing about when.”

## Her Second Question

Whether or not the why had cost her anything, she went on and asked a second one on the same morning, out loud, in daylight, with her mouth still dry from the day before and her hands off the dressed stone.

She asked it once.

“When I asked you yesterday, did it go in.”

That was her second question and she had it ready and she had planned to have it ready and she had gone into the sixth hour of the morning telling herself it was free.

“Yes,” he said.

Nothing else. No shape to it, no room around it, nothing about how much of it there is or what shape it takes in a book nine hundred years deep. One word, and then he stood there on his own side of a plank table and waited, which is what he has been doing for ninety days.

## The Second Rule

She worked it out at about the ninth minute, standing on dressed stone in daylight, and it is the single most useful thing she has learned in nine months.

“You will not give me another one until you have answered that one.”

“No.”

“And if I do not wait.”

“Then it goes in twice,” he said, “and neither of them gets answered.”

She had that on the trough and in her head for eleven days and she is not going to have it in her head. A question that is being asked takes whatever he has, and whatever he has is nine hundred years of other people’s business, and she cannot get at any of it until the one standing open is shut.

She is not going to get a list out of him by asking him two things.

She is going to have to stand on a wall eleven miles off, on made ground, in daylight, and ask, and wait, and ask again, and wait again, and there is no arrangement on this ground for how many times that is going to be.

## What It Cannot Be

She said it to him because she needed to say it to somebody and there was one person in front of her and there was nobody behind her at all.

“It cannot be un-asked.”

“No,” he said.

He did not soften it and he did not enjoy it and he did not say one word about the cost of it to her, and she wants that written down as well, because she had gone out to that wall with an idea that a man who answers things is a man who can be got at, and there is not one part of what has happened since the seventh hour yesterday that was somebody trying to get at her.

She asked him what else he keeps at the seventh hour of the twenty-fourth of a month in daylight with a person hearing it, and it is in the book, and it cannot be got out of it, and it has cost her three days of road and six loaves and a night on a bench and the whole of a quarter of an hour in front of a wall yesterday with her mouth dry.

And it is the first thing anybody has ever written down about her that she put there herself.

## What Three Terms Cost In Miles

She did the arithmetic while she was waiting for him to say them again, standing on dressed stone at the west jamb at the seventh hour of the twenty-fifth of a month, because she is a person who does arithmetic and because the terms are the first thing in nine hundred years that have been given to her with a shape she can measure.

One at a time. At the wall. In daylight.

At the wall means nine miles of strip road and eleven of made road and two of the last of it, there and back. She did that on the twenty-third and twenty-fourth of a month that is going and it took three days, because the light goes at the eighth hour in that month and there is nothing between the gate and the wall to sleep against.

In daylight means she cannot do it at the dark and cannot do it in the dark and cannot do it in the rain of a month she has not had yet.

One at a time means she does not get the next one until this one is shut.

She did the sum twice.

If he takes a month, she walks sixty-six miles to stand in front of him four times. If he takes three months it is a hundred and ninety-eight.

She stood on the dressed stone with her arm shut and worked out what a person with no coppers and no wages and no place to stand does with a hundred and ninety-eight miles of road. The answer is that she does what she has been doing for four months, which is that she carries a bar, she eats when she has earned it, and she sleeps on the ground.

She is not going to be kept by the terms. Nobody on this ground is going to fold up because a man at a wall has said three sentences to her.

And she has no idea at all how many questions there are, and that is the other half of the bill, and it is the half she has been carrying since the twenty-fifth of a month that has gone and cannot put down.

## The Road West

She left the dressed stone at the eighth hour of the twenty-fifth of a month beginning 1,604 with the bar on her shoulder and a loaf and a bit in the bag, and she had no idea where she was going to sleep and no more idea than that about when she was going to be standing on that stone again.

Half a mile east of the wall, as she came past it in the last of the light, she looked at the window a hundred yards off.

It did not change.

She went on west, and she is not going to put the question about that window to him at the next one, and she is not going to put it to him at the one after that either, and she has not asked and she has not forgotten it.

## What She Is Carrying Home

Twenty-two miles of strip road and made road between here and a post. One loaf and a bit. An arm that opens four days in five, and it was open on the eleventh mile and she did not shut it, because shutting it is not a thing she can do on demand.

And a question standing open in a book nine hundred years deep with a date on it, and a rule about the next one, and three terms that are ordinary and that will not move.

She got four miles out before the light went and she lay down in the low broken ground off the north side of the made road with the bar under her head, and there is nothing between the gate and the wall to lie down on except ground with stones standing out of it four inches apart.

And the thing that is going to sit on her all the way west is not the window.

It is that she asked, and he has not answered, and there is no arrangement anywhere on this ground by which a girl who cannot pay for anything finds out how long a man of nine hundred years takes to get to the end of a list.
