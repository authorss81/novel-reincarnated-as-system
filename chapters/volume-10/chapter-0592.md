# The Cut In The Made Road

The road dips some miles west of the gate and the dip is where the burn comes through, and there is a cut in the road at the bottom of it nine feet deep with three planks across it and a chain at the near end.

She got there at the third hour of the tenth of the month beginning 1,784 with her bar on her left shoulder and nine days in front of her and the crossing lying open in front of her like a mouth.

## Eleven Miles Of Made Road

She had walked it from the gate and it is the worst walking of anything she has done this year and it is not because of the distance.

The made road is made. Somebody graded it and laid stone on it and it holds a cart, and it goes dead flat for eight miles with a hard crown in it and no ditch on the north side and no tree on it anywhere, and there is no place anywhere on it to lie down.

She went nine miles in one long day and stopped twice and both times she stopped because her breath went to seven and not because her leg went.

At the fourth hour of the fifteenth she sat down on the crown of the road with a bar across her knees and got her count out, the way she does.

Nine.

Then thirty-one.

Then she wrote the nine out and looked at it and did not rub it out this time, because she had decided on the nineteenth of the month beginning 1,754 that she counts from that morning and not from the twenty-fifth of the month beginning 1,724, and she has kept to it for twenty-eight days and she was not going to break it on a made road at the fourth hour with seven in her breath.

One. Today.

And then whatever tomorrow turned out to be, and she would not know what it was until she was standing in it.

## The Cut

There is water in it. Not much — a foot and a half at the bottom, moving, over stones the size of two fists.

The planks are three and they are not level and they have been walked on for a long time and the middle of the second one has gone soft at one end.

The chain is at the near end on a post that has been set in the ground and packed with stone, and it comes up to the height of a person’s chest and it goes across to a post on the far side the same height, and it is a chain and not a rail and it is wet.

She stood at the near end for forty minutes and looked at all three things before she put a foot on anything, which is what she has done at a face and at a counter and at a wall for eight years and is the only habit she has that has never once got her killed.

A man was sitting on the near bank with his back to a stone and a kettle on three stones beside him and a dog that was not old.

## The Man Who Took It Over

He was fifty-five or near it and he had the hands of somebody who has not done this for long.

“The man that had it was called Peth,” he said. “He had it fourteen years. He is not dead and he is not here. He comes on the road and he does not stop and he does not come to this end of it, and that has been three years.”

“Where does he go.”

“He goes east.” The man tipped the kettle. “That is the only thing I can tell you about it and that is all of what I know.”

“Then why did you take it.”

“Because a crossing with no one at it is a place where a child goes in.” He said it without weight, which is how a man says something that has never once occurred to him to be remarkable. “I do not know where Peth goes and I have not asked him and he has not offered. I keep it. That is the trade and I do it four days in five.”

That is a man who is correct, and she worked that out in about nine seconds standing on a made road with a burn at the bottom of it, and it did not cost her anything.

A man who keeps a crossing is not an office. He has a kettle and a dog and a post with a chain on it and he does it four days in five. He has never been asked what it is for and never will be, and he is not going to tell anybody, because he does not know.

## Why She Went Over

Here is all of it and she is not going to dress it up.

She went over at the fifth hour of the eleventh because a rut in wet ground comes out on the far side of a wall a quarter of a mile beyond the opening, and the four who went on a cart nine years back went east, and the four who are going on the seventeenth are going east.

And because there are four miles the long way round and she has four counts of breath and a body that goes lighter every day and nine days, and four miles is a day she did not have.

She put the bar across her back first, on a string, so that both her hands were free, and she went out to the near plank with her right hand on the chain.

It is a chain and it is wet and it is not a rail.

Her right hand has four fingers and a thumb on it and the arm was open that day and she knew the arm was open because she had written it on the flat stone six days before, and she went with her left hand low on the chain and her right hand on the post and she got two feet out and the plank moved.

Not much. It moved the way a thing moves when it is wet at one end and the other end is holding nine feet nine of iron.

She went down on her left knee on the second plank and she put her whole weight onto the chain and the chain took it and she stood there for four seconds with her face over a burn with a foot and a half of moving water in it.

And then she was across, and she came up the far bank on her hands and knees with the bar still on her back, and she lay on the far side in the grass for eleven minutes with her face in the grass.

There is nothing to lie down on for the whole length of that road and there is grass on this side of the cut and she had not known that until she was in it.

## What He Is Correct About

She got up at the sixth hour and went back to the near end and stood on the bank, and the man was still sitting with his kettle and his dog, and he had not come across.

“You are not going to open the chain for me at night.”

“No.”

“Why not.”

“Because I do not know you and the cut is nine feet deep and it fills after two days of rain,” he said. “There is a gate two miles east and a wall two miles past that and I keep a crossing. If I open a chain in the dark for anybody at all then I am not keeping a crossing, I am running a bridge, and a bridge that a man can open is a bridge a man can shut.”

She stood on the bank and looked at him and worked out in a few seconds that he was right about the chain and he was right about the rain and he was right about the night, and that not one part of it had anything to do with her.

And then he said the other thing, and he said it in the ordinary voice, and it is the only time in five years that he has said it to anybody.

“A road that keeps a person is a road that keeps a person,” he said. “You have been standing at the near end of it for forty minutes deciding whether to come over. Everybody who comes over this way stands there a while. The ones who get taken east do not stand there at all, because they did not decide anything. They were told on the other side.”

“And how do you tell the difference.”

“I do not have to,” he said. “That is the trade. I open the chain in daylight for anybody who asks and I do not write anybody down.”

## What She Got From That

She crossed back at the seventh hour on the seventeenth... no.

Let me fix: she crossed at the fifth hour of the sixteenth and stayed on the far side.

She had intended to cross back.

She got to the bank at the sixth hour and turned round and looked at nine feet of cut with three planks on it, and worked out in four seconds that she could not cross it at night and that the man was not going to open it.

And there were nine miles of made road and a gate and a wall and a line in wet ground on the other side of all of that.

So she was on the far side of the cut from the fifth hour of the eleventh of the month beginning 1,784 until the morning of the twelfth, and she had no bread and no place to lie down on and nine days.

She lay in the grass on the far bank that night and it was the first soft ground she had had since the fourth of the month beginning 1,694.

And she lay there and thought about what a man who keeps a crossing has in front of him all day long, and what it costs him not to write anybody down, and that there is no name on earth for that trade and it is not an office and it is not a rank.

There is a man of fifty-five at a cut in a made road, and he does it four days in five, and he has never once been asked what the road is for, and he does not know, and he has not offered.

That is a man in a book of her own making and she is not going to write him in.

The man at the chain has not given her his name and she did not ask for it, and the one that had it before him is called Peth, and she said Peth back to herself once to be sure she had it right, and then she left it alone.

## The Night On The Far Bank

She lay in the grass with the bar under her and the bag under her head and she slept for two hours and forty minutes and woke twice and did not remember either time.

There is nothing on this bank. There is a post and a chain and a cut with water in it and a cart track going off north over flat ground, and it is empty. That is all of the east side of that crossing and she has more room than she has had anywhere since the wall.

At the second hour she got out the nine coppers and counted them on the flat of her own hand.

Nine.

She put them back and counted them again from the bag.

Nine.

She lay there in the grass on the far side of a cut in a made road and got the same answer twice and had to stop, because twice is not a proof and a girl who counts does not let two stand in for a proof.

The sixth stage takes things out of her in about two hours. It did not take those.

She did not work out why and she did not try, and it is the first time in her life that a thing did not go out of her and she has written down that she noticed it and that she did not look at it.

At the fourth hour of the twelfth he got up off the bank and stirred the fire under the kettle and looked east up the made road.

“They come at the fourth hour,” he said. “They always come at the fourth hour.”
