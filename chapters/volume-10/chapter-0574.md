# What The Mill Remembers

She got to the mill at the ninth hour of the seventh of the month beginning 1,694, one mile west of a shut door, and asked a man of seventy a question she was not allowed to ask at a wall, because a wall is twenty-two miles east and a mill is a mile.

## The Wheel

The wheel is on the north side of the building and it is a wheel, and it has not turned since before she came to this ground, and the race under it is dry and grown over with the same weed that is in the yard.

The building is sound. The axle is greased. The stones are dressed and dressed true, and somebody oiled them inside the last year, and there is a bench by the door with a fire ring and a pot on three stones the way the woman at the end of the lane has one.

There is a new pair of millstones leaning against the end wall that have never had water near them, and they are dressed by somebody who knows what he is doing, and there is no mill in this ground with the use for them.

The man was seventy or thereabouts and he was not doing anything and he had not been doing anything for a while. He had a dog at his feet that was old too and got up when she came and lay down again when she did not.

“A mill is a building,” he said, before she had got a question out. “You can be in it.”

She had walked four miles to ask one thing and he had answered a different one before she opened her mouth, and she sat down on the bench, which is what he wanted.

## What He Said About The Wheel

He said it three times across two days and it came out the same three ways, and she wrote all of it down in the dust of his yard with a finger on the eighth of the month because she does not trust herself to keep a thing she only heard once.

The wheel has not turned because the water is not there, and the water is not there because it was taken off above a mile back for a lime bed that is not worked now.

There is no trade here. There has been no trade here for nine years and I am not going to tell you what I do because I do not go anywhere.

And the last one, which he said with his hands still, and which she took down exactly: *People stop coming down a road, and then the ground goes quiet, and what was on it stays on it, and that is why I know more about that cottage than anybody alive and less than it was.*

She asked him what he does, on the first day, twice, and he gave her the same sentence both times, and the second time he added that he has a dog and four hens and a roof, and that is everything, and that nobody has asked him that in nine years either.

## What A Dead Mill Is For

She worked it out at the second hour of the eighth of the month beginning 1,694, sitting on the bench with the fire out, and it took her nine minutes and it is the most useful thing anybody has given her in seventy-four days.

A mill that is still turning forgets. Everything that comes down the road after you is a newer version of the place and in ten years the newer version is the one everybody answers for.

A mill that has stopped does not forget, because there has not been anything newer to get in the way.

That is what a dead mill is for. She had come one mile to a building where a man had not been asked a question about the lime cottage in nine years, and so he had not had to decide how much of it to say.

She had never thought of that. It is not on her list of methods and it is the first thing in a month that she did not already know.

And then she thought one step past it, lying on the step that night with the dog at her feet, and that step is the reason she has not been to a second mill on this ground and is not going to.

A man who has not been asked anything in nine years will tell you everything he has. That is what she has just been shown, and it is a thing she could use, and she has used it once already, and she wrote it in the dust on the eighth of the month beginning 1,694 and then scratched it out, because she could not stand to own it.

## The Boy At The Lime Cottage

She asked him on the eighth at the seventh hour, standing at his bench, with her arms down and her voice in the ordinary shape.

She did not ask about a name. There is no name to ask about; she has never said it out loud to a person and she was not going to start in a mill yard.

She asked: was there a boy at the cottage four miles east who counted loads out loud.

And the man of seventy said yes, and then he said nine or ten sentences across two hours, and she has them all.

The boy was not one of the four who lived there. The boy came to the cottage with the lime and stayed and slept and did not ask for wages and did not go east.

He counted the loads out loud, because the four could not count and a load that comes up short is a load somebody has to answer for.

He was there two summers and a bit of a third, and the boy was good at it, and the foreman liked him and said so out loud in front of the barrows, which is a thing that happens.

The last day the lime stopped there was in the ninth year back, and it was not a day anybody decided. The stone stopped being good and then the water stopped and then there was a month with no trade in it.

And the boy was still there in that month, sleeping in the cottage and eating what the four had, with nowhere to have been to and nothing said about where he was going.

That is the ninth sentence and it is the one she has gone back to, and it is the one that does the damage.

## The Part That Is The Past And Not The Present

And here is where she went wrong and it took her the whole walk home to see it.

She asked him what became of the four and he told her one thing after another, the way he would tell anybody, and it was a true answer and every part of it was ten years old.

The four went on a road in the month the lime stopped. There was a carter at the turn with a board and he put four names on the board and the board went east, and the four went east with it, and the carter came back without them and was not the next one back for eleven days.

And then the man of seventy said, at the ninth hour of the eighth, in the ordinary voice, and he had no idea what he was handing her:

“He says the boy went with them. The one who counted. He says the boy walked behind the last barrow because he would not get up on one.”

That is the past. That is nine years old and it was said by a man who was not asking her a question and who has not thought about it since he said it.

## What She Did Not Ask Him

She had two questions in her mouth for the whole of the second hour and she did not ask either of them, and the reason she will put both of them down here is that they are the reason she got what she got.

She wanted to ask where he went after the month when there was no trade. That is the question the sentences of the miller were leaning towards and she let them lean.

And she wanted to ask whether the boy was still alive nine years back, which is a smaller question and a worse one, because a man of seventy in a yard will answer it either way and she cannot un-hear it, and a man who says he was fine in the winter about somebody who has been gone for ten years does not know what he is handing over.

She asked neither, and what she got instead was nine sentences from a man who had not been asked anything in nine years, and it cost her nothing and it is the only free thing she has had in two months.

It is also nine years old.

## What She Did With It

She stood in the yard of a mill with a wheel that has not turned for nine years and did the arithmetic, and it took her four seconds.

A carter came down the road with a board.

Four people were put on it and the board went east and the four went east with it and were not seen on that road again by anybody on this ground.

She has been standing four feet into an opening in a wall on her own side for thirty-eight days, and there is a man on the other side of a plank table who puts names down, and it is the same instrument as a board on a cart.

It is the same instrument. That is all. There is no name for it and there is no house over it and no one in this world has ever said out loud why a man with a board comes down a road.

But she knows what a board is for now, and she knows it because a man of seventy told her about nine years ago in a yard, and there is no rule anywhere that says a true thing told for nothing does not count.

She got her bar off the wall of the mill and put it on her shoulder and looked east down four miles of nothing with the light going, and there was a road out there that goes nine miles of strip and eleven of made and two past a gate to a place where four names went nine years back and were never heard of again on any ground she can walk.

She had a hundred days and a mill behind her and a woman of fifty at the end of a lane and four questions standing in a book nine hundred years deep.
