# What The Boy Carries

A board came down the strip road to the post on the seventeenth of the month beginning 1,694 and the boy of eleven carried the near end of it, and she was at the flat stone when it came, because she had nowhere else to be.

## The Board

It is a rail board off a cart and it is a long one, and it goes across two shoulders and the boy of eleven took the near end because he is the one who is supposed to take the near end, and a boy of thirteen who is not named took the far end.

They put it down against the rail and the boy of eleven stood there and got his breath back, which took him longer than it should have.

She had watched him do this eight times in two years. He has been at the post since he was nine. He does not read a board and does not ask what it is for and does not stay at the post after he has put it down, except on a day when there is more than one, and on the seventeenth of this month there was more than one.

## Why She Asked Him

She asked it at the seventh hour on the seventeenth of the month beginning 1,694, sitting on the flat stone at the foot of the ladder with the boy of eleven standing four feet off with his hands at his sides, and she had it ready before he came, which is not how she usually works.

She had had it ready for twenty-four days.

Because she said a thing out loud on this flat at the sixth hour of the twenty-third of a month beginning 1,634, with a boy holding a board forty feet off with his back turned, and she has gone over that morning since more times than she will write down, and there are two versions of it in her head, and in one of them a person heard it.

There is no third version. There is a version where he did not hear it, and a version where he did, and there is no way on this ground to tell the difference between those two from where she was standing.

## What She Asked Him

She did not ask him what she had said.

She has been asking that question of herself for twenty-four days and there are only two answers to it and both of them are bad, so she asked the other one instead, which was the one she had actually come to.

“What do you do with a thing you have heard,” Ning Yan said.

He did not answer for a while. He looked at the board on the ground and then at the rail and then up the strip road east, which is the direction a person looks on that ground when a person has asked them something and they are working out whether to answer it.

“What do you mean by a thing.”

“Anything,” she said. “A thing somebody says out loud in front of you.”

That is the only part of it that was a question and she asked it once and she did not ask it again.

## What The Boy Said

He did not think about it for very long, and he did not put his hands behind him, and he said five things and then he stopped.

“It goes with me.”

“I have carried boards down here two years and I have never been asked to keep one and I am not going to start.”

“A thing said out loud where two people are is out. You cannot go back and say it quieter.”

“You cannot un-say it to me either. I do not know whose it was and I am not going to be told and it is not my board.”

“You asked me what I do with it and I am telling you there is nothing to do with it. It comes down the road with me like the boards do.”

That is all of it, and it is eight sentences and he has never made one of them twice.

## What She Did Not Say

She did not say thank you, and she did not say forget it, and she did not say it was the name of a brother, and the last of those three cost her more than the six loaves did.

She wanted to. She has not written down what she wanted to, and the reason is four days old and she has thought about it since.

If she tells him what the thing was, then he is a person who has been told something, and there is no way on this ground to make a person into somebody who has not been told something. It goes the other way. You cannot un-carry a board.

She has had one experience of that in her life and it is a book nine hundred years deep, and she has been eight years learning what it is to have somebody else’s knowing put on you whether you agreed or not.

## The Boy Of Eleven Is

Here is the whole of him and it took her the rest of the day to get it, sitting on the flat stone with a boy of thirteen six feet off, changing the chocks on the bar.

He is eleven. He has been carrying boards since he was nine. He holds the near end because that is the end that goes down first on a bad road and he is the one who goes down first.

He does not read. He does not ask what a board is for. He is not paid a figure, he is paid a loaf and a half loaf on alternate days and he has never once been asked what he did with the day.

And on the twenty-third of a month beginning 1,634, forty feet off, with his back turned, he watched a girl of nineteen say three words out loud into an empty yard, and he did not turn round, because he does not turn round, and he has not thought about it since.

She has worked out since that he has the same instrument she has, which is a count.

He counts the boards off the cart and he counts the near end and he counts how many are left at the rail before the light, and a person who counts something all day knows exactly what it costs to be told one more thing by a person who is not going anywhere.

That is why his eight sentences came out flat and in the same voice he uses with a chalk, and it is why she got eight sentences and not one sentence with anything in it, and she is not going to pretend to him or to a reader that the flatness hurt. It is the most useful voice anybody has spoken to her in eight years.

## The Part She Took East

She took it east that night and she has it now and it is the first thing in eighty-three days that has made the twenty-fifth of a month begin to look like a day and not a number.

A name said out loud in the open is said where it cannot be taken back.

Not by a man at a wall. Not by a keeper. Not by a book nine hundred years deep. By any person standing four feet off with a board in his hands.

She said it once already, into a yard, on the twenty-third, and the yard kept it, and the boy did not turn round, and the thing has been out for twenty-four days and she cannot get it back by wanting it back.

And on the twenty-fifth of a month beginning 1,724 she is going to stand four feet into an opening in a wall in daylight on her own side and say it where a man of nine hundred years can hear her, and it will be out there too, in a worse place than a yard, in front of the only instrument in the world that writes.

## The Twenty-Four Days In Between

Here is what she did with the twenty-fourth, the twenty-fifth, the twenty-sixth and the twenty-seventh of that month, because it is not nothing and she does not want it written as nothing.

She worked the face on all four days, nine barrows a day, and she got her arm up and kept it up, and she did not go four miles west to a shut door on any of them.

She went back and looked at the barrow-way at the end of the fourth.

She lay in the broken ground north of the made road twice and did the count and did not sleep.

And on the twenty-seventh, at the seventh hour, four miles from the turn, she said his name out loud to a barrow one-handed with her right arm open, which she had not done before and has not done since, and nobody was within four hundred yards, and it was still out.

That is what she learned from eight sentences by a boy of eleven. It is not a thing that a person can put back in a quiet place afterwards. It is out at the moment it leaves, and the quiet afterwards is a thing she tells herself.

She put her hand flat on the flat stone under the bottom of the ladder and it was bare.

The boy of eleven took the near end of a second board and went east down the strip road without looking back at her, which is what he does, and she has never once known what it costs a person to be the one who does not look back.
