# The Gap In The Line

She got herself to the post for it. That was the whole reason she walked west in the dark, and it was the reason she had told herself on the made road at the gate, and it turned out to be right, and she was on the flat at the second hour of the twenty-sixth of the month beginning 1,604 and nowhere near the ground she had wanted to be on.

## The Muster At The Sixth Hour

The gang mustered at the sixth hour at the mile west of the post and the foreman counts the hands before anybody picks up anything, because the gang’s ration is drawn against the number of hands and has been since before she came to this road.

He did it with a finger. He has done it the same way for eleven years and he went along the line of them pointing, one at each man, and he counted out loud in the ordinary voice because it is a thing you count out loud.

“Nine.”

He was on the ninth man and there were nine men on a road gang and then there was her.

“Ten,” he said, and the finger went onto her, and she was counted.

That is the last time she was counted by a person on this ground. She did not know that at the sixth hour of the twenty-sixth and she would not have been glad of it.

## What She Had Come Home For

She had one job on this ground for the day and the job was to be here, in daylight, on flat ground with a bar in her hands, when it came, and to have somebody on the road who would find her if it took her where she could not get up from.

She had thought about that for three days on a made road with a boy of fifteen four feet off and she had got it down to one sentence: if it happens eleven miles east I do not want to be eleven miles east

So she had walked twenty-two miles in a day and a night to be at a post on a strip road at the sixth hour of the twenty-sixth, and the post has a rail and a trough and a ladder and a flat stone and a keeper who is inside with his pot, and there is no arrangement on nine days of road for what you do if you go down.

She said that to nobody and she has not said it to herself twice.

## The First Four Hours Of The Day

The gang loads at the second hour and she was on the cart for the first two loads and then on the bar.

A dressed kerb is two hundredweight and a bit and it goes on a bar with four people, or with two and a roller. There is one roller on that mile and it is nine hundred feet behind the kerb run, so it does four stones a day and there are nine stones.

Nine stones went in with four people on the bar and she was on the light end of every one of them, and she did the shovel work and the packing behind them, and at the ninth hour she asked the foreman whether the roller could come forward.

He said the roller could come forward.

That is not a thing she had done in eight years. She had not once asked a man on a road gang for a tool to be moved, and it took her four seconds to work out that it was not a favour either, because a roller is a thing that has to be somewhere and she had told him where it had to be.

## The Ninth Hour

It came at the ninth hour with the bar loaded and her right arm shut and the foreman on the cart and the nine men forty feet off at a drain.

Nothing about it was different from the last one. It did not hurt and it was not slow and she was not able to stop it, and it took the time it takes to put a hand flat on a table, and the men were forty feet off and nobody on nine days of road saw a thing.

Something went out of the place low behind her fourth rib, down through her, into the packed bed of the road she was standing on.

She was on her knees in the stone dust for two minutes and then she was on her feet with the bar down and her hand flat on the packed stone, and it was over.

## What She Checked

She did the same check she did on the eighth of a month and she did it more slowly, because there is more of her now and she has had to learn the whole list again.

Four fingers and a thumb on the right hand and the missing finger gone for good. The arm shut. Eight of eleven holds in a loaded reach, which has been eight of eleven since she was seventeen and is not one of the things that went.

The count of her breath, four, on flat ground standing still, and four inside a loaded reach.

The small open place low behind her fourth rib was where it went out of. It is not shut and it is not going to shut and it is the size of a coin and she put her hand flat on it through her coat and it was not warm and it was not cold.

And the thing she could not put a name on: she is lighter again, and she has been lighter every day since the eighteenth of the month beginning 1,544, and it came a second time this month and it came for eight hundred feet of drain and forty feet of kerb.

## She Was Stronger Afterwards And She Felt It

Here is the thing she has not got a place for, and she noticed it inside a quarter of an hour and then she went on working and noticed it again for the rest of the day.

She was quicker.

The bar came up off the bed and she put it down twice where she would have put it down once, and at the tenth hour she took the heavy end of a dressed kerb with her left hand under it and her right hooked over the top and lifted, and it came up, and she stood with it while two men got a roller under it and did not need to.

She has not been able to take the heavy end since she was eighteen. It is not strength. Everything that has come out of her has come out of her foundation and she has been lighter every day since the eighteenth of the month beginning 1,544 and this is the first thing that has gone the other way.

She felt it and she was frightened by it for four minutes and then she worked out that a body three stages past a ceiling that nobody in this world holds the top of does not owe anybody an explanation, and she went on lifting kerb until the light went.

And then she stood in the middle of a mile of road at the fourth hour of the afternoon with a bar in her hands and worked out the one thing about it that she is not going to be able to put down.

Twice, on the ninth of a month beginning 1,604 and on the twenty-sixth of it, something came out of the small open place low behind her fourth rib and went into the ground under the road she had just packed.

That was nine days ago and four days ago. Two times in nine days, on listed work cleared past the line, and the work gang is measured by the weight and the length and neither of them has moved.

She cannot tell what is being paid for and she is not going to be told by a man who keeps a book of everybody’s numbers, and she went on and the light went and the foreman marked off the day.

## The Count At Dusk

The foreman marks the length at dusk with a cord and a peg and then he says who is on in the morning, and he said it on the twenty-sixth of the month beginning 1,604 because there is forty feet of kerb left on that mile and he wants the same nine in the morning.

He did the finger again. He went along the line of them, one at each man, counting out loud, and she stood at the end of it where she stands.

“Nine.”

The finger went past the place where she was standing and landed on the last man and came back, and he did not put it on her a second time.

Then he looked along the line of them, one face at a time, counting mouths instead of hands, and it took him longer and he got to the end and stood there.

“Ten.”

There were ten people standing in that line.

## Nine And Then Ten

She stood still and she did not say anything and she watched him do it again.

He started at the other end, which he does not do, and he went along the whole line with his finger a second time and it went over the same place at the same point and landed on the same man, and he said nine again.

Then he counted mouths again, slowly, and he got ten again.

Then he looked straight at her, four feet off, in daylight, with the cord in his hand and nine men watching.

“You are standing in the gap,” he said.

She moved six feet to the left, out onto the flat beside the last man, and he counted the line again with his finger.

It came out nine with the finger and ten with his eyes.

## What He Did About It

He put the peg in the ground and bent over it for a minute and a half, and nine men stood in the dust and watched a foreman work something out.

Then he came up and walked the line a third time, slowly, and he stopped with his finger on the last man and he looked at her and then he counted from the other end again, and got nine with the finger both times and ten with his eyes both times.

“All right,” the foreman said.

And that was all of it. He did not ask her how it happened and he did not say anything about it to the nine men and he did not go and get the board.

He said one more thing, at the end, in the ordinary voice, to a kerb.

“I do not have a name for it,” the foreman said, “and I am not going to stand here and make one up, and the cord is the cord.”

## What She Felt About Being Missed

Here is what she felt and she is going to have it written down at the ninth hour of the night of the twenty-sixth of the month beginning 1,604, lying on the flat stone at the foot of a ladder with the book nine inches off her left knee.

She was not insulted. She had eight years of practice at being refused by a man with a document and she knows exactly what it feels like and this was not it.

She was relieved.

That is the part she has not been able to look at since and it is the part she wrote down.

She stood at the foot of a ladder for eleven days in the month before wanting a man with a paste pot to write her into a flat book with three names in it, and she wanted it badly enough to be angry about it.

And on the twenty-sixth of a month beginning 1,604 a foreman with a finger on a line of ten men told her that there is no way of getting her into a number at all, and she went to the gang in the morning and worked.

A list that finds names and not persons is a thing she has known since the twentieth of a month beginning 1,574. She has never once been able to say what being a person instead of a name is worth, and on the twenty-sixth she stood on a made road at the ninth hour and found out roughly what it is worth to her.

It is worth not being able to be fed.

## What Is Left Of It

She worked out at the ninth hour of the night, lying on the flat stone at the foot of the ladder with the book nine inches off her left knee, that there are two different things and only one of them has her in it.

She can look at him. He can look at her. He said you are standing in the gap to her face and he was right and she was four feet off and the light was on.

He cannot put her in a number.

That is all of it. She is not smaller and she is not slower and she is not less there. She is on flat ground in daylight at the end of a road gang of ten people and she cannot be counted where she stands, and the counting is the only thing on this ground that any of these ten people have ever done to each other.

The foreman is a careful man and his length is right and his cord is right and his peg is right, and in the morning he will draw nine rations off a cart and put her at the end of the line again, and nobody at that store is going to count his hands.

She is going to eat what she earns and she is going to walk twenty-two miles to ask a man a question, and if she stands still on the flat in front of a foreman he will not be able to say that she was there.
