# What Waiting Costs In Road

She stood on dressed stone at a wall on the eighth of the month beginning 1,634 for the better part of an hour in daylight on her own side and nothing whatever happened, and she came home forty-four miles in three days having learned what that costs.

## The Seventh Of A Month

She had a loaf and a half out of the store on the sixth and she went east at the second hour of the seventh with the bar on her shoulder.

Nine miles of strip road and eleven of made road and two more past the gate, and she made the twenty-two between the second hour and the ninth, which is the best she has done and she did not once stop.

The arm opened at the seventh mile on the strip road and shut at the eleventh and opened again a mile past the gate, which is two days in five in one day, and she did not shut it and did not try.

She lay down a mile short of the wall in the low broken ground off the north side of the made road and she had half a loaf and she ate half of it and drank a quarter of a cup out of the skin.

She was up at the third hour and she did the last two in the dark, because the light does not come up on that ground until the sixth hour in that month, and she was at the bar on two posts with the wall standing up ahead of her before the sky had finished.

## The Eighth Hour At The Wall

He was on his own side of the plank table and his hands were flat on the wood, the same as they had been the first time she had ever seen them.

That is what she sees now when she comes to that opening. Not a man waiting. A man at a table, at the seventh hour, on his own side, with his hands flat on the wood, the way he has been every hour since the fourteenth of a month beginning 1,574.

She stopped four feet into the opening at the west jamb with the metalled road at her back and she did not say anything and she did not go any further than that, because the terms are hers as well as his and there was nothing she wanted to say that was free.

She waited a count of four and then four times that, and then she waited until the light came off the top of the wall, which took an hour, and she stood in all of it with her hands down at her sides and her arm open on the one side.

## What She Waited For

Here is the list and it is short and she had it ready before she got there.

That he would say something.

That he would take his hands off the plank.

That he would say she had asked too soon, or that she had not asked properly, or that the four words had been put in the air and not into the book, or that a man would come out from behind the wall and tell her a thing he had not been told to tell her.

She had nine days of nothing and a rule with no end on it and she worked out, standing on dressed stone in daylight with the light going off the wall, that what she had actually been hoping for was a person, and not a door.

## What She Got

At the seventh minute he put his hands down at his sides and stood up straight, which took a while, and he looked at her for the first time since she had been standing there.

That is what she got.

He looked at her and he did not say one word about the question standing open in a book with a date on it, and then he put his hands back on the plank and stood there.

She waited another quarter of an hour after that, on the off chance, and he did not say anything else, and at the eighth hour she took herself off the dressed stone and went west.

## The Boy Of Fifteen And The Second Of An Hour

She went past the gate at the ninth hour and the boy of fifteen was asleep sitting up against the post with the chain on his knees, and she did not wake him.

She stood at the gate for four minutes in the dark and worked out what she had wanted and what she was not going to have.

She wanted him there. Not to hear her ask, because she was not going to ask. She wanted a person four feet off while she stood on a dressed stone for an hour being looked at once, because an hour of that with nobody behind her is a thing she has not done in her life and it took her four minutes at a gate in the dark to work out that she cannot do it.

She is not going to ask a boy of fifteen to get out of bed at the ninth hour to stand behind her while she is refused. He did it once, in daylight, because she asked him in daylight and he had a cart most hours and she said put the chain up and do not come after me.

She walked on past the gate and she said good to him about a second later and he did not wake up.

## The Made Road In The Dark Again

She made the two hundred yards out to the bar on two posts in six minutes and she did not stop at that either, and she got on west past the gate at a quarter of an hour, and she was not going to wake a boy of fifteen up at night to stand four feet off a wall on a day when there had not been one word.

She ate the other half of the loaf about the eleventh mile and she went the eleven miles of made road between the eighth hour and the third hour of the morning, which is half the rate she does it in the day, and she sat down twice in the ditch on the south side.

The count went for four at the fourth mile out of the made road and it did not come back until the sixth, and there was a cart behind her at the second hour with a man on the tail who did not put a light on it, and she went off the metalled road into the broken ground on the north side and sat down with a bar across her knees until it was gone.

It came back in fourteen.

## The Ninth Of A Month And What It Cost

Here is the bill and she wrote it out at the flat stone at the foot of the ladder at the third hour of the ninth, in the dust, with a finger, and then she did not rub it out.

Forty-four miles in three days. Half a loaf and a half. An arm open four days in five that went for seven at the second hour on a made road with a cart behind her. A body that is lighter than she was on the twenty-sixth of the month before.

And one hour standing on a dressed stone at a wall in daylight on her own side, at which a man of nine hundred years looked at her once and said nothing at all.

That is what it cost, and she has been counting things for eight years and this is the first bill she has drawn where the largest single line is a thing that did not happen.

## What The Store Woman Said To Her

She got bread on the ninth of the month beginning 1,634 at the eighth hour, from a woman of fifty behind a counter, who said the rate out loud to her and to a man at the end of the counter and to nobody in particular, which is what a rate is.

“That is two coppers a loaf,” the woman said. “Same as it has been two years, and I have said it to a girl who has had none since the twenty-sixth.”

“You have said it before.”

“I have said it four hundred times,” the woman said, “and I am going to say it to the next one as well.”

She did not ask her one question about where she had been and she did not ask her one question about the arm, and she took work against the bread and said what the work was so there could be no mistake about it later, exactly the way she said it on the second of the month beginning 1,604.

That is everything a woman of fifty has ever been good to her for. Two years of it and a figure said out loud every time and not one single word about why.

## The Thing She Cannot Do At A Wall

She worked it out on the ninth of the month at the fourth hour and she has not been able to put it down since.

The terms ask her for three things and she can do all of them.

One at a time: she is doing that.

At the wall: she can walk twenty-two miles twice inside three days and she has done it four times in a month.

In daylight: she can stand on a dressed stone for an hour with her arm open and the wind off a flat and nothing on her to drink.

What the terms do not have in them is anything about what happens while she stands there.

There is no term for the waiting. There is no term for how long. There is no number of days in it and there is no person on this ground she could go and ask, because the only person who knows is the one who will not say.

She has spent eight years refusing documents that did not say what they were for. She has just discovered that a rule can leave out the only part that matters and still be a rule that has been kept for nine hundred years without a single word of it being a lie.

And she is not going to be able to fix that by standing there again.

She looked at the stone.

The book was on it. Forty-four days now, since the ninth hour of the twenty-fifth of the month beginning 1,574, and an inch of stone between it and the keeper’s flat book under the bottom of the ladder, and she had not looked at it since the twenty-eighth of the month before.

She stood there for a long time with her arm hanging and the bread gone out of her bag.
