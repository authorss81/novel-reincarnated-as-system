# A Body That Cannot Stop

She had seventy-six days of furnace behind her and the twenty-ninth was the last of them, and there was no way on nine days of road to get to it that did not involve asking somebody for something, and she had nobody to ask.

The seventh stage had come on the thirteenth of a month at the fourth hour in front of nine men on a lifting floor, after a dressed stone went the length of her own arm, and she had been at it since.

Forty-nine of those seventy-six days there had been nothing in that shed to put a hand on.

That is the whole of the arithmetic and she had it out on the boards on the twenty-second in the ordinary hand of a woman who has been writing months on them since she was eleven, and it comes out the same way every time she does it.

## Why Seventy-Six

A stage in this world has always cost her something somebody else paid for.

The board outside the lifting floor is full and cannot pay her and is not going to. Every line on the rail at the works is measured by the weight of what comes out of the ground or by the length of it, and there is not one of fifty-one that she can take, and being able to read is not a thing this ground has ever paid anybody for and is not going to start.

She cannot be given anything, so no material arrives, ever, in any weather.

And the furnace will not light itself. A stage cannot be built by one pair of hands and it never has been.

So the choice is a person standing in a shed with her while it goes, and there is nobody inside nine days of road she is entitled to stand in a shed with and try.

It is the same sentence she has had since the third of a month nine months back. Seventy-six days is the longest it has ever taken her. The one before it was forty-one and she took that at the seventh hour on a bank about sixty feet long with two of nine men nine feet off her.

## The Woman Who Came Up The Four Miles

She came up the four miles at the sixth hour on the twenty-second and stood in the yard with a coil of rope over her shoulder and nothing else, and she did not knock, because there is nothing to knock on.

“There is a room to sit in for six days,” she said. “I am here until the twenty-ninth at the sixth hour because I am to be at a gate at the sixth hour on the twenty-ninth, and there is nowhere on that road for me to be on the twenty-ninth except outside it.”

“You could sleep at the mill.”

“I have a room,” Xie Changyin said. “It is nine feet by eleven and it has a board floor and a shelf and I am not going to be talking about it for six days.”

She put the coil of rope down against the wall and looked at the ring of clay on the boards and the box it came out of and the bar standing on end by the door.

“I have read one chapter in my life about a furnace,” she said. “Nineteen forty-something, in a room, from a man who had read it in a room. I can hold a rim with two hands and I know when to let go of one. I am not a builder and I am not going to pretend to be one, and I have held one rim in my life and I did not let go of it and it is the only thing I am qualified for.”

“Then why are you here.”

“You told me once that I could not leave without leaving something,” Xie Changyin said. “I did not understand that for nine years. I understand it now and I have four hours of it to spend and this is what I am spending it on.”

## The Two Sets Of Hands

It went wrong once.

It went wrong the way it went wrong in front of nine men on a lifting floor on the thirteenth of a month, which is that the near side came up four inches and turned and went sideways, and a woman of sixty-one who had told her she could hold a rim did hold the rim and did not let go and put a foot on it, and the whole thing came back onto the floor of the ring and sat there.

Nobody was burned.

There was no fire in that shed and there has not been a fire in it since before she came, and the thing that comes up out of the ground under a ring of clay on boards is not a fire and does not burn anybody; it took the skin off two inches of a man’s palm once, in a different year, in a different room, and it has been shut for nine months.

It happened again about four hours in, and it was smaller, and then for three days nothing came up at all.

Then it came up.

## The Bracket

It came up out of the ground under the boards at the fourth hour of the twenty-ninth and it went through the boards and not into them and the boards did not move and the clay ring at the low end of it did not move, and it came up four inches and stopped.

It had been at a finger’s width for seventy-six days and there had been nothing in the shed to put a hand on for forty-nine of them, and Xie Changyin had held a rim with two hands for two hours of the six days and had not been asked and had not pretended to be anything else, and a woman of sixty-one was sitting on the boards at the fourth hour of the twenty-ninth with two blistered palms and had not said one word.

It said its one sentence and stopped.

## Nobody Was Burned

She said it out loud, in the shed, at the fourth hour, to a woman with two blistered palms sitting on the boards with her back against the door frame.

“Nobody was burned.”

“All right.”

“Look at your hands.”

“There is nothing on them that is going to keep me from reading tomorrow,” Xie Changyin said, “and I have read a hand in four seconds since I was twenty-two and I will read one tomorrow. That is the whole of it.”

She looked at her own palms for four seconds.

“Nobody has ever said that to me,” she said. “In forty-two years, at about nine in the morning, when a stage comes up in a room with other people in it, the first thing that is said is what it cost the room. And nobody has said the other one.”

“All right.”

“You have said it four times in about eleven minutes and you said it before you looked at your own arm.”

## What She Paid With

She did not find it for two hours.

That is the way it works and it has worked three times in seven months. Nothing comes off her hands and nothing comes off her arm and there is no limb and no hold and no count of breath gone, and what is gone is not the thing that was there first.

She had gone back over it at the sixth hour on the boards the way she goes back over it every time, because she is a person who keeps an account of what she has and what she has not, and she found it in about ninety seconds standing up.

A road she has entered. An hour she has agreed to. A room she has walked into.

Once a thing she is in has started, her word does not turn it.

She can still say no. She said no in a room with a lamp in it a hundred days ago and the no went up the same road every answer has gone up and nothing was written and no book will not shut. She can say no to this woman, to this shed, to a gate at the end of a road, to the twenty-ninth, to anything.

She just cannot make the no take.

## Four Ways

She went up the made road at the ninth hour on the twenty-ninth with the bar over her right shoulder and she worked out four ways to tell Xie Changyin she was not coming, standing in the flat light about a mile from the shed.

The first is the plain one. She walks to the mill, four miles, and says it in the ordinary voice, and the woman of sixty-one is a woman of sixty-one and will not argue.

The second is to leave the bar at the door. That is not a refusal of a date, it is a signal, and a woman with two blistered palms will read a bar left at a door exactly as it is meant, and she will go to the gate at the sixth hour on her own and stand there.

The third is to dig the run at the gate and not be back, which is a thing she has done on four days in seven months and which not one person has ever asked her about.

The fourth is to go to the made road, stand in it at the ninth hour, and wait, which is what she is doing now.

All four of them work.

That is the part that matters and she stood in the middle of a made road for about a minute working out why.

None of them will do. Not because of a rule and not because the woman would stop her and not because a book would shut on a date and not because anything anywhere in the world failed.

Because she said it in a doorway at the seventh hour in daylight on the eighteenth of a month, and it went on the bottom of a sheet in the ordinary hand, and she is not able to take it back.

## I Am Not Going

She stood in the made road a mile from her own shed with the bar over her right shoulder and said it out loud, in daylight, to nobody.

“I am not going.”

It is true. It was true when she said it and it was true four seconds afterwards.

It went nowhere.

There was nobody within nine hundred yards to hear it. It changed nothing on this ground and it did not go into anything. It did not fail, which is the thing she has learned to look for in a refusal since the twenty-sixth of a month in a different year, because a refusal that fails is a thing somebody can undo, and this one did not fail and it did not hold either.

She can still say no. She said one a hundred days ago and it cost her everything she wanted and it stood.

She just cannot make this no take.

## The Sixth Hour Of The Twenty-Ninth

She walked out at the sixth hour.

Eleven miles. The made road goes four miles to a mill whose wheel has not turned since before she came and keeps going for another seven, and there is kerb on it at the seventh mile, and at the end of it a gate with a chain and a man and a stool and a cup.

She had a bar she could not hold at the heavy end, and a right arm that had been open for two years, and four fingers and a thumb, and eight of eleven holds, and short breath at four counts that had been at four counts inside a loaded reach for three days which has never happened, and nineteen coppers in the fold in the inside of the door frame.

She was through the turnstile by the second hour on the thirtieth, because the chain was up and the man inside it had the cup in his hand and did not ask her a question and did not say one word about the eleventh of a month and wrote nothing down.

There was a long low lime-washed building at about two hundred yards with a lamp in every window and about a hundred people at tables down the length of it.

It was warm.

Not one of them looked up.
