# Inside The Counter

She went inside the counter on the fifth of the month because the man with the brush asked her to carry.

The near end holds the counter and the desk set sideways and the bundles tied with string. Past that the room runs the length of the building, a hundred and forty feet, tables down the middle, lamps on every table, about a hundred people reading. She had seen it from the stool. Now she saw it from the counter in, standing where the brush stands.

It is one storey. The roof sits low. The windows stand in a row and every one holds a lamp. The floor is board, swept, the boards fitted close. There is no hall in it and nothing in it that is called a hall. One building, one book, one rate, one rule. No department. No floor. No rank list. No committee. No wing. No title beyond the fact of being recruited.

The work goes like this from the inside. Bundles come in and are untied. Sheets are squared and put down the tables in order of difficulty. Readers take them and mark them and pass them to the far end where the man of sixty-one keeps the book in his hand. The marked sheets go into folders. The folders go onto a shelf. Nobody hurries. Nobody wastes motion.

She carried bundles to the third table and back for the empty string. She squared sheets the way the man showed her, edges exact. Her left hand did the work. Her right hung open. Nobody corrected her grip.

“You square well,” the man with the brush said.

“I have squared worse,” she said.

## The Far End

She walked the length of the room once with bundles, counter to far end, to see it whole. The tables run true, gaps wide enough for carriers. The lamps stand one to a table, flames straight, glass wiped. Readers bend over sheets, brushes moving, lips moving once on hard lines.

At the far end the man of sixty-one sits with the book under his arm, folders shelved behind him, dates running back forty-two years. He looks up once as she passes, nods, looks down again. No hall. Nothing called a hall. One building, one book, one rate, one rule.

The air is warmest at the far end, a hundred breaths gathered under a low roof. It touches her face like summer. She has not been warm like that since the shed in winter with a ring coming up a finger’s width. She stands in it a moment longer than the carry needs, letting warmth reach the ache in her shoulder, then goes back for the empty string. Work first. Warmth is a thing that comes with work, not instead of it.

## The Oil Hour

At the sixth hour the carriers trim every lamp down the room, each with a practised turn. She watched from the counter in, flames dipping and standing straight again, glass wiped, oil filled. The smell of hot oil sits in the warm air. On nine days of road oil is counted by drops. Here it is counted by nights, a hundred lamps a night, forty-two years of nights.

She worked no figures. She let the smell sit in her chest beside the warmth. A good trade tends its lamps. This one does. That is why it tempts, and why it will take her all the same if she lets it. She carries the next bundle steady, edges exact, and does not let wanting touch her hands.

## The Rate Board

The rate is painted on a board behind the counter where everybody can see it. Two coppers a page. A second rate by the difficulty of the hand. A third rate at twice the first for a hand nobody in the building can read. She read the board twice to make sure she had it right. Two coppers a page. She had nine coppers in her fold and bread for one day.

She stood before it a long time. Carriers passed behind her with bundles. Readers called for sheets. The lamp flames stood straight. Two coppers a page. Forty hands a month. A room nine feet by eleven with a board floor and a shelf. Standing. A name in a book that cannot come off. Every term reasonable. One of them fatal, that it makes her a post, though she does not yet have words for why.

She worked what forty hands a month means in coppers, plain, the way she works everything. Even at the lowest rate it is more coin in a month than she has held in seven months. It would buy bread and oil and twine and a winter floor. It would buy a roof that does not need tying. She cannot touch it.

Her hand stops in the air over money given for reading. It stopped over four coppers on a plank bench. It stopped over eight on Wei Ran’s book. It will stop here. That is the whole of what she is worth in that room, measured true: everything to the room, nothing to her hand.

She turned away from the board and went back to carrying. The bundles were light. The paper cut clean. The work steadied her.

The tied sheet with two hands on it still waited on the counter, set aside, string round it. Four people had failed at it and the work went on round it. The young reader did not look at it. The woman with the thumb did not look at it. Everybody knew it was there and nobody touched it. That is what a competent room does with a thing it cannot read: it sets it aside and goes on.

At midday she ate outside with her back to the lime wash. Xie Changyin sat with her and ate bread too, though Xie Changyin has a room and standing and could eat inside.

“You carry well,” Xie Changyin said.

“So I am told,” Ning Yan said.

“You have nine coppers.”

“Nine,” Ning Yan said. She did not ask how the woman knew. Hands that read know coppers the way they know hands.

“The price of a page is two,” Xie Changyin said. “You have done the arithmetic.”

“I have,” Ning Yan said. “And I cannot be paid. So the arithmetic goes nowhere.”

Xie Changyin nodded. She did not argue it. She had heard it on the second of a month in a doorway and had not stopped thinking about it since, and she was not going to be coy about it now.

In the afternoon Ning Yan stood inside the counter and watched a hundred people read by lamplight in ordinary rooms and understood what the building is. It is not a conspiracy and it does not meet in secret and no member of it is hiding. It is a trade built out of a thing Dai has done for twenty-six years inside nine days of road, reading a hand nobody else can read, and somebody past the turn built a house for it with lamps and a rate and a book.

She felt reckless for a moment, standing there with empty hands, wanting to pick up the tied sheet and read it aloud the way she read Geng’s sheet aloud on a lifting floor and stopped a man in the middle of it. She shut her mouth on it. She had refused that hand forever on purpose and she was not going to pick it up for pride.

At the eighth hour she went out past the windows and stood in the road. Her arm ached. Her breath ran four counts. The building was warm behind her.

She had stood inside the counter and seen one building and one book and no hall, and she had carried bundles and squared sheets and read a rate board, and nothing in it was wrong and everything in it was fatal to her anyway, though she did not yet know why.

That night at the gate she said it out loud once.

“It is a good trade.”

“It is,” he said in the dark. “I am here and I hear you.”

“And I cannot be part of it.”

“No,” he said. “I am not going to tell you that you are wrong.”

She slept with the lime wash behind her eyelids and the lamps burning steady.
