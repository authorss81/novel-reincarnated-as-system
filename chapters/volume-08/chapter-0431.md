# The Second Attempt

She came back west up the long road at the second hour on the sixth of a month and she got the leg back to a mile and a half an hour and stopped it there and finished the rest on the slow count, and she came off the made road at the seventh mile and walked the rest of it into a shed at a mill whose wheel has not turned since before she came.

She had been eleven days getting out and back and she had not gone inside a building on that road once.

## Every Single Person Has Been Right

She sat down on the boards at the fourth hour with the bar against the wall and worked it out the way she works everything out, which is to take it apart and put it back and take it apart again.

The man behind the counter was right. A line is a name and a first day and a last day and a figure, and she is past the end of a page, and there is nothing above nine on any book in that building.

The boy of sixteen was right about the cart. The man of thirty was right about the stone. The woman with the stove was right about the day the season turned.

Dai was right about the finger and right about the arm and right about the layer being a fault, in a room with a lamp in it, with nothing owed and nothing forced, and he said a denial is an entry and he was correct.

Rao was right that there is no line on that floor for her and right that being able to read is not a thing this ground pays anybody for.

Geng was right that a floor is a block short and right that he has no term for it.

A woman of sixty-one was right that recruitment is a reading performed on a person, by somebody who is qualified, in a room, on the condition that the person confirms or denies.

That is eight people. She put the eight out on the boards in the ordinary hand, one under the other, and there was not one word written against any of them.

She had no counter-evidence of any kind.

The one thing she knew that none of them knew was in a room two miles up a made road in a groove with two other sheets in it, and she had given it up on purpose, forever, in daylight, with no force in the room, and it was not theirs to have.

## The Thing Nobody Had Said

That was the fifth time she had done this — taken a thing apart, laid it out, and gone looking for the place where it did not add up — and on the sixth of a month at the fifth hour she found the place where it did not add up, and it was not a place where somebody had been wrong.

It was a count.

A reader is called in when a hand cannot be read.

She has been called in four times in a month by four people who did not know what they were calling. And across that same ground there is a book with four hundred and six names in it, and somebody at that end had already written down one thing about a woman at a gate on a ground where no hand can read a line.

Nobody had said it out loud, and nobody had said it cruelly, and it had not been said to her face.

It had simply been counted.

And she had been a reader in front of strangers for two years, on a third floor and at a lifting floor and at a rail and at a yard at a turn, and there had never been a price on it by anybody, and the first time anything had ever been attached to it by another person was eleven miles east of here, and she could not find out what the price was without saying yes to it.

And she was not going to say yes in order to find out what yes cost. That is the whole shape of her and there has not been a single day in seven months when it has not been the whole shape of her.

## The Hedge East Of The Turn

On the eighth of a month there were two hundred yards of hedge by the length, east of the turn, on the rail at the works, the third listing of that ground, put in on the fourth with the ninth against it.

She cut it in a day and a half with a bar she cannot hold at the heavy end and four fingers and a thumb, and it paid out of nothing because there is nothing at that rail for her, and on the ninth the man with the rule in his hand walked it with a stick and a line and did not hurry any part of it and rubbed it off with his own thumb.

Then he came back up to the rail and stood where he stands.

“Two hundred yards,” he said. “Third time on that ground and it is the first one of the three that has come out true.”

“Yes.”

“There is no second column on this one and I want you to hear me say that out loud, because you have put one into a sheet four miles up the road and I have read about it from two men and I have not got a word for it.”

He put the chalk in his pocket.

“This one is six days and two hours.”

## What Was Still Running When She Got Back

She had been out of this shed for eleven days and the ring had come up every night of them, a finger’s width, and she had not put a lump in it once because she had been walking and there was nothing in a road to put a hand on.

That was the arrangement and it had worked, and she had also worked out somewhere around the ninth mile that it was going to be the last time it worked, because there was no ninth mile left after this and the whole of what she could reach from that shed had been walked to its end and back in eleven days.

She said that out loud at the boards too, on the ninth, at the second hour, and she wrote it out underneath four names and a number.

“There is nothing left to walk to. That is the end of the easy part and it happened on a bank at the seventh mile of the long road and I did not notice it happening.”

## What She Said With It

She got home at the tenth hour and she put a lump out of the box in at the low end of the ring, and it came up a finger’s width and stopped, and she lay down on the boards and said the number out loud twice, because a man at a rail says a number twice to a person who has to carry it.

“Six days and two hours.”

“Six days and two hours.”

And then she said the price, which she did not know, out loud, into a shed with no fire in it, which is the thing she does before she spends anything.

“I do not know what that costs. Eleven miles in two days and a leg that stopped for four hundred yards and eleven coppers left out of twenty-one, and I am not going to tell myself that is the answer to a number a man put on a rail, because I know what comes out of me and I know where it comes out of and it is not the leg.”

## What He Did With It

He took it.

That is what he does. She has watched him do it three times in seven months and every single time he has taken it out of the ground under her and not out of her hands, and told her the number first, and told her the cost first, and said afterwards that she was not going to be able to find out what it cost in anything except having less.

He said it in the present tense and it was four sentences and it bought nothing.

“I am taking all of it and not a part of it.”

“It comes out of the boards and not out of you and you will not feel it in your hands.”

“I am telling you first because the last woman on this ground who had one of these was told about it once by a man who was in a hurry, and you have heard me say that number twice tonight, which is why I am allowed to take it.”

“You will find it by having less of something. Not today.”

And the ground under her went cold the way a floor goes cold under a man, and there was nothing in the shed to put her hand on and she did not go and find something.

## What Was Missing Afterwards

On the tenth of a month she put a lump out of the box in at the low end of the ring at the second hour and it did not come up at all.

Not the width of a finger. Not a quarter of it. Nothing.

She did it nine times over two hours and got the same answer nine times, and there was a ring of clay on the boards of a shed at a mill and no fire in it and nothing on nine days of road she could put a hand on and have answer her.

She worked out that it was the eleven miles and she did not believe that for four seconds, and then she worked out that it was the eleven miles anyway, because believing the other one meant going and standing in a yard at a turn and asking a man with a rule in his hand a question, and she was not going to do that over a ring of clay at the second hour of a month.

## Six Words Again

She lay on the boards and said the six words out loud once.

“No. I will not hear it.”

And then she said them a second time because she wanted to hear whether they sounded any different the second time.

They did not.

She lay there for a while with the strip against her ribs and the boards under her back and the ring cold at the low end of it, and she did not connect the two of anything, because there was nothing to connect and she was not going to build a thing out of a coincidence at the second hour.

## What She Said Standing Up

She got up off the boards at about the fourth hour on the eleventh of a month with the bar against the wall and fifteen coppers in the fold and nine days left.

And she said out loud, into an empty shed, in the ordinary voice:

“I am going to go.”

She stood there after that.

“And I want it written down.”

She did not say it after that. She stood in the middle of the boards for about as long as it takes to count nine and then she got a lump out of the box and put it in at the low end of the ring and went to sleep in her coat.

It was the sixth hour when she woke and the ring was cold and the ground outside was flat and there were nine days between that shed and the twenty-ninth.
