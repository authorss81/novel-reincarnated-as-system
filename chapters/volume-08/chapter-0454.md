# The Man Who Took The Board

She went north up the track on the second morning because she could not sit at the shed and not go, and she was out four hundred yards up it before she saw him.

The track runs out of the lane through a gate and on between two hedges for two hundred yards, and then it comes to a ditch. The ditch is a made thing, straight sided, four feet across and two feet deep, cut to take the water off the country and carry it east to the cut at the floor. There is a sill laid across it the whole width of the track, dressed stone, and that is what was loose.

He had his bar with a stone on the end of it standing against the hedge. He had a lever, a length of iron with a crook on it. He had four wooden wedges cut to shape, held together with an iron ring, and he was on his knees at the low end with his face close to the stone.

She stopped at forty feet and did not go closer.

He had the sill half up already when she came round the bend, and there were five sets of wedges along the low side of the ditch where he had been working, and he had been at it since the previous evening.

He worked it the way she has watched a hundred men work. One wedge, driven home with the heel of his hand. A second, a finger's width proud. The lever under the lip and a long slow lift on the handle with his weight coming on it from his back leg and not his arms, because his arms are fifty years old and his back is not, and she knows that arithmetic, she has done it for two years at a rail.

The stone came up about a quarter of an inch. He took the weight on the lever and got his fingers under the face.

Then the near end went.

## Two Feet

It went the way a dressed stone goes when a sill has been loose for a season and a man has trusted a lever. It lifted at his end, turned, and came down with its bottom edge off the bank, and it took him with it into the ditch on his left side.

It did not fall on him. That is the thing she has had to hold since. It stood up out of the ditch on its edge like a door off its jamb and leaned there, and the man went down beside it on his back with his leg under the stone's shoulder and his hands still on the lever, and it stood and did not come over.

She was forty feet away and she could have moved it the length of its own length before he finished falling.

She did not.

She stood on a cart track four feet off the ground with her right hand open at her side and she felt the whole thing arrive in her — the sill, and the ditch, and the man — and nothing went.

Not because she held it back. Because it does not go out of a room she has not been asked into, and she had not been asked, and a man on his knees at a gate who has taken a line off a board in the ordinary way has not asked her for anything.

That is the rule. She has had it since a floor at the end of a made road and it has never once cost her anything.

## What She Did

She ran the last forty feet.

She got down into the ditch on her knees and got both hands on the sill at the low end and put her shoulder under it with her good arm and said, in the ordinary voice, out loud, the way a person says a thing to another person:

“Get your leg out.”

He got his leg out.

It took her perhaps four seconds and she did not move the stone at all. She held the weight of it while he drew his leg along the ditch and out, and then she let it come back down onto its bed in the ditch with a noise like a door and lay there against the bank.

She was out of the ditch before he was. She went up the bank backwards on her left arm.

He lay on his back in the ditch for a while with his eyes shut and his hand open on the stone, and she stood at the top with her breath running four counts and her arm hanging open, and she did not say one word, and the sun was a finger's width off the hedge tops.

## Afterward

He got himself out. He took a long time and he did it the way a man of fifty does a thing like that, which is slowly and without comment, and he got the lever and set the wedges in the ring and made her hold them while he set the sill, and she held them.

Forty yards by the length. A sill does not go the length of its own length, that is not a thing stone does, so it goes a foot at a time, wedge and lever and a man on his knees and a woman holding four wedges in a ring on a cart track in the last of the light.

He had done this before. She understood that from the way he did not think about it.

## Two Hours Of Wedges

They finished it at the last of the light. He set the last wedge and stood and looked down the track at the sill and put his thumb on it once and took his thumb away.

Forty yards by the length does not go in an hour. She knows that; she has laid bed and kerb and a sill at a mill with a wedge and a lever and it is a quarter of an hour of grinding and an hour and a half of sitting in the mud while the stone decides whether it will come.

So they sat.

He dug a hole with his heel and sat down in it at the low end and put the lever across his knees and looked up the track at nothing for a while, and she sat on the bank with the four wedges in the iron ring in her lap and the cold coming up off the ditch.

Nobody spoke for about a quarter of an hour. She has worked rails and gates and a lifting floor for two years and she has never once sat still in the middle of a job with another person and had nothing to do and let it be that.

He got up after a while and worked the next foot. She got up and put the wedges in where he showed her, which was without being shown, because a man holding a lever and looking at a stone has his weight wrong and everybody on this ground can read it.

At about the second hour he said, “You have done this before.”

“I have done an edge of it.”

“Where.”

“A yard is a yard,” Ning Yan said, “and there is not a great deal to get wrong with one.”

He worked another foot.

“You do not talk much.”

“No,” she said.

“Why.”

“Because I have been told the things I say go somewhere,” Ning Yan said, “and I have not worked out yet whether that is true and I have decided not to find out.”

He did not laugh and he did not ask what she meant. He said, “That is the first thing anybody has said to me at this gate in four years that I did not have to answer,” and worked another foot, and that was all of the conversation they had on a cart track in the dark.

He never asked her name and she never asked his. He said, “That was done proper,” and she said, “Yes,” and those were the whole of the talk they had after that.

At the crossing he put the bar back against the wall and lifted the chain across and went into his hut and shut the door, and the light went out on the country inside the hedges the way it does everywhere.

## The Hour Before She Went Down The Lane

She sat at the foot of the near post for a quarter of an hour after he went in, in the wet, with the bar across her knees.

A man of fifty had gone into a ditch and come out of it in front of her and had then carried out two hours of work with a woman holding his wedges and had not once looked at her hand to see what it was.

She has spent two years being the thing in the room that gets the answer. Out here she was a pair of hands holding four wedges in an iron ring in the cold, and it was worth something to somebody, and it was not her reach and it was not her name and nobody on this ground is going to remember that it happened.

That is the whole of what she has ever been able to give anybody on nine days of road and she had no idea until she had given it once.

## The Lane

She walked the mile down the lane behind him at a distance, because there was nowhere else for him to go, and she watched a man carry a bar at the light end with four fingers and a thumb on it and a left leg he was favouring.

He went past the shed and past the low door in the far bank and did not turn in at it. He carried on down the lane at his own pace, out past the crossing, past the gate, and away west into the country, and the light went and she lost him at the top of the rise.

That is ordinary. A man finishes a sill at the last of the light and goes home, and he says so when he next comes down the lane. She has done it herself with a roof cord and a bar eleven miles in three days.

She stood in the lane for a while after that with nothing to feel about it at all.

Then she walked back to the shed with the bar over her shoulder and her heart still going hard from the ditch, and she lay on the boards with her forearms across her face until it went.

She had stood at forty feet with a dressed stone in the air in front of her and done nothing whatever, and it had been correct, and the man had gone into a ditch anyway and come out of it, and she had held four wedges in a ring in the dark on a cart track for two hours and neither of them had thought there was anything remarkable about a woman doing it.

Nothing remarkable about it. She reads a fine hand that a hundred people in a building cannot read. That is remarkable and it has cost her three entries and half an arm and nine months.

Four wedges is not remarkable anywhere. She found that out standing in a lane in the dark.
