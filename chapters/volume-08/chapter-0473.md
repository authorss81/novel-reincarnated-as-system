# The Sheet Read Aloud To Her

The first of a month. She had said a date out loud in a room three days before and it had cost her three days of walking, and this one cost her nothing, because the building was two hundred yards from the shed and she had been living in it for nine days.

She was at the gate at the fourth hour with nine days of nothing in her body and a figure in her head that was twenty-six thousand six hundred and twenty-two hours, and she had not slept, and she was not going to be able to explain that afterwards either.

The room had the shutters up and about thirty people at tables and a lamp on every one of them that was worth nothing at that hour.

Xie Changyin had the sheet in her own hand.

The man of sixty-one with the book was at the end of the table. The man with the brush was at his desk and did not come over. The young reader of twenty-six was at the far end with his brush down. The woman with the thumb gone at the first joint was not there and Ning Yan noticed that and did not know why it mattered until the fourth hour.

## The Reading

She did not do it well, at the start. She had done this two hundred times, and she stood at a table with the shutters up and the sun on the paper and lost the place twice, and lost it again four lines further on.

“I am going to start again,” she said. “That was four lines and I lost it at four lines, and I have never lost a place in a document in forty-two years, and I would rather say it out loud than let you work out what it means.”

“Start again,” Ning Yan said, and Xie Changyin started again, and the second reading was the reading.

## What It Says

“The first is the terms. A paper that says a person whose reading has been confirmed in this room by another person in this room is a person whose reading may be relied on inside a set of walls, and it does not go out of those walls and it does not have to.”

“That is the first line. It is the same sentence you said in a shed at a mill five months ago.”

“It is the same sentence and I have never improved it in forty-two years, because it is not mine.” Xie Changyin turned the sheet a quarter turn. “The second. A rate, four coppers for every page, of any hand, of any difficulty, with no second rate and no third rate. Paid on the seventh, the seventeenth and the twenty-seventh, in the ordinary way, into a fold in the inside of a coat.”

“The third. A room, nine feet by eleven, board floor and a shelf and a lamp. It is yours. Nobody comes into it. There are four of them in this building and three have been empty for eleven years.”

“The fourth. Standing. If you are in a room and a thing cannot be read, you are a person they can call, and it is not a favour and it is not a debt and nobody is permitted to call you twice in a day.”

“The fifth.”

She stopped there for longer than she had stopped anywhere else.

“The fifth is the name,” she said. “Your name, in the book, in the ordinary hand, with the date against it. And a name on it cannot be un-recruited. There is no hand in this building that takes one off and there has never been one, and I have looked twice, for my own.”

## The Sixth Line

“And the sixth,” Xie Changyin said, “is the last one and it is the shortest one on the paper and I have never once had a person look at it first, and you will.”

She put the sheet down flat and put her hand on it.

“This document has been read to you. Do you agree to it.”

That is the whole of it. One sentence, on a line, at the end, after five lines of the most reasonable terms anybody has ever put in front of her in eight years, in a room with the shutters up and the sun on the paper.

Every one of the five was fair. She has been doing this for eight years and she can tell in four counts whether a person is being handed something, and she was not being handed anything. She was being offered a trade by a woman who was right about nine things in ten and who had walked eleven miles once and had said no to nothing in forty-two years.

And the sixth was the room. Not the room with a lamp and a shelf. The room, with the shutters up and the sun on it, in which a qualified person holds a document about you in her own hand and reads it to you and then asks you to say one word, and the word goes in a book, and the book is behind the counter and the man with it has written four hundred and seven before this one.

She sat in a room and let a document be read to her and then asked to say one word.

She had done that before. Four months ago, at the eighth hour, in a room with a lamp in it, with a man of forty-three and a sheet turned round so that she could see it, and he had said he was going to say four things, and the first thing on it had her name at the head of it.

Both times she had said no.

## What She Asked For, And What She Was Given

“You have not put a column on it,” Ning Yan said.

“There is not.”

“You said in a shed at a mill that if she would be one properly you would put the list of what it costs in front of her in a column, with the days and the hours against each line, and that she could argue with a number instead of with a feeling.”

“I did say that and I meant it and it is not on this sheet, because this sheet is not about you. It is about the trade. That is the second time I have told you that and it is the same answer both times.”

“Then give me the column.”

Xie Changyin went behind the counter and got a sheet of her own and wrote on it for four minutes in her own hand, and she did not read it out loud, and she put it face down on the table and put her hand flat on it.

“You asked me once for one question and I answered it in this building at the fourth hour and it went nowhere,” she said. “This is the other half of that arrangement and I am not going to read it to you, because a column of costs is the only thing in forty-two years I have ever been ashamed of handing a person in a piece of paper form.”

“Why.”

“Because I write it myself. There is no hand in this world that can check a figure I have put down in my own hand, and if I put a cost down that is wrong then nobody in eleven hundred years of practice will ever be able to show me that it is wrong, and that is exactly what happened to you at a mill with a bar over your shoulder, and you were fifteen or sixteen.”

She lifted her hand off the paper.

“It is the honest version and the honest version cannot be checked. Read it and take it or leave it and do not tell me what you think of it, because I have never once wanted to know.”

## What Was On The Column

She read it twice with her right arm out of the way of the table and her breath at four counts.

It is four lines long and it is in a woman’s hand that has been keeping a written count for forty-two years.

Four fingers on the right hand, gone at the far end, not returned.

Half an arm, open, not returned.

The turn, gone.

The order, taken first, leaving the strength.

And then, underneath, in the same hand, the fifth line, which is not a cost and which Xie Changyin has evidently written more than once:

Nine of them are in that room and I do not know one of their names.

Ning Yan put the sheet down.

## The Woman Of Twenty-Two At The Door

The woman of twenty-two was at the door of the room through all of it and did not come in, and after the reading she said one thing and then she went out into the sun.

“You could have asked me,” she said.

“For what.”

“For whether it is a bad hand.” She had her arms folded and her hand was where it always was.

“You did not ask me last time either. You stood in a room with four people in it and they asked you a question and you answered it and it went in a book, and I have been not knowing for five months, and you have been not knowing about that.”

She turned her head about a quarter of the way.

“I have just spent an hour listening to a woman read five lines of paper out loud and I am the only person at that door who is going to have to wait a month for the rest of it.”

“Yes,” Ning Yan said.

“So I am asking you now, in this room, with the shutters up, and you are going to answer me, and I am asking it because I am twenty-two and I am frightened and I have worked out in nine seconds that you are the only person on nine days of road who will not lie to me about it.”

And Ning Yan looked at a woman of twenty-two in a doorway and said, “I cannot tell you that.”

“You could.”

“Yes,” Ning Yan said. “I can. And I am not going to, and I have not got a reason you would accept and I am not going to invent one at you to get out of the room. There is no version of the truth where it is free, and I am not the person who decides what is free for you.”

The woman of twenty-two stood in the doorway for about three seconds.

“That is a no with a whole hand behind it,” she said, and went out into the sun.

## She Was Going To Answer Tomorrow

She stood up, which she did not have to do, and squared her chair back under the table, which she had not done in eight years.

“I will give you an answer tomorrow,” she said, “at the fourth hour, in this room, with the man who keeps the book at the end of the table, and you will read it back to me before it goes in whatever it is.”

“That is the ordinary condition and I have it ready.”

“You have had it ready for forty-two years.”

“I have had it ready for forty-two years and I have used it twice and I am not going to pretend to you that I am calm,” Xie Changyin said, “and I would like it on the page, in your own head, in the ordinary place where you keep things, that a woman of sixty-one told a person her heart was going at about nine o’clock in the morning and that she said it because she lies to nobody and not because it changed anything.”

She picked the four sheets up and squared them and put the string through.

“Do you know what you have just done,” Ning Yan said.

“No,” Xie Changyin said. “I have never once known. That is in the sixth line and it is not the one she reads.”
