# Nobody Has Ever Answered One

The second of a month beginning 1,094 was the seventh hour and the woman was still standing at the door with one hand on the frame, and she had been standing there for eleven minutes, and she had not come in and she had not gone away.

Ning Yan came out of the doorway.

“Whatever it is,” she said, “I cannot give you an hour. I have not got one.”

“I have not asked you for one.”

“You have been standing at my door for eleven minutes.”

“I have. And I would have stood another hour, and I am going to tell you why in three sentences and then you can tell me to go away and I will go away. I would rather have said them at a doorway than have you spend anything on hearing them.”

“All right.”

## The Three Sentences

“There is a trade in the country eleven miles east of here where a person reads a hand that other people cannot read.”

“There is a rule in it, and the rule is that a hand is not read on a person’s word. It is read in a room, and the person it is about confirms or denies it.”

“And on the nineteenth of a month three months back a line went up out of this ground with a holder’s answer against it, and there is not one hand on this side of the far road that answered it, and nobody has ever answered one.”

She said it the way the man with a brush says a number, which is without weight.

“Who put it up,” Ning Yan said.

“That is not mine to know and I have not asked.”

“Who read it?”

“I do not know that either. I know what a line is and I know that nobody answered it. That is the whole of what I have.”

## What She Could Ask And What She Could Not

She could have asked this woman where she had come from and the woman would have told her, because she had said already that she had been turned back at a gate at the end of a road and that the gate had a man with a chain at it.

She could have asked how many people were in the building and the woman would have told her, because she had said about a hundred.

She could have asked what a page was worth and the woman would have told her that too, and she would have given the number twice.

She could not ask how a hand gets to a person and cannot read it.

That one was the whole of why the woman was standing in the yard and every other question was the price of standing there, and she worked it out standing up with her weight on her left leg in about nine seconds, and she got the same answer nine times, and she did not ask any of them.

She had spent two years learning that a thing a person says to a room is a thing a column can be read having, and she has four people a month who would ask her about a cup of something hot.

## The Two Lines

The woman put her hand in the inside of her coat and took out a folded sheet and did not open it.

“There were two lines on it,” she said. “It says, a line has not been carried to a room it was made out for. And then it says, a holder is required against it.”

She said the words the way a man reads a figure off a board, which is exactly, and without putting anything of her own into them.

“The date on it is the eighteenth of a month three months back and the answer was put up on the nineteenth, and the eighteenth and the nineteenth are two different days and they are both on the same sheet and both of them were written by a person who was not in a hurry.”

Ning Yan stood in the open doorway with the boards showing behind her and the bar over her right shoulder and did not move for four seconds.

She did not answer that.

## Nine Years Of Standing At Gates

“You have walked eleven miles to a shed with boards on the floor,” Ning Yan said. “You could have said that to a man behind a counter at that counting house two miles up this road and he would have written it down.”

“He would have written down a woman of sixty-one in a plain coat who walks,” Xie Changyin said. “That is not the same thing and it is not worth four miles of anybody’s time.”

“All right.”

“And you would have taken it off him and I would have taken it off you, and then it would have been a thing I said to a room, and I have said things to rooms for nine years and it is how a person like me spends a life.”

She shifted her weight off her right leg.

“You have asked me nothing at all and you have stood in your own doorway and told me three things about a trade and then told me you were not going to buy anything with any of it, and I want to put the number on it, because it is the number I would have bet on.”

“What number.”

“Four,” she said. “I would have put four on it, and I have been wrong once in nine years and that one is the reason I am standing here instead of sitting in a room.”

## What She Was Asked And By Whom

The woman put her hand flat on the folded sheet against her coat for a second and then took it off it.

“You have not said anything,” she said.

“No.”

“You have been standing in your own doorway for four seconds without saying anything, which is a longer time than it takes me to read a page.”

“All right.”

“I am not going to ask you why and I am not going to ask you again, and I want to tell you that in the ordinary voice and not as a favour, because there is a rule where I come from about a person who does not answer in a doorway, and it is the only rule in it that I have never once had cause to be glad of and I am glad of it now.”

Because the only way to answer that question correctly is to tell the truth, and the truth is a thing she has never said out loud to anybody, and saying it out loud in a doorway to a stranger with a folded sheet in her coat is how a voice gets into a book.

He was answerable. That is not a word anybody on nine days of road has for him, and it is not a word she has ever had for anything. He has been answerable since the nineteenth of a month three months back and nobody had asked him anything at all until a woman of sixty-one stood in a doorway four miles east of a mill and quoted two lines and a date and the ordinary voice, and she had no third thing.

He had said, on the nineteenth, in his own words, that it would happen, and that the somebody would have only the two lines and the date and the ordinary voice.

He had not said she would be standing in the open with the boards showing.

“She is not asking me,” he said.

“No,” said Ning Yan.

“She is asking about a hand.”

“No.”

“Then say that.”

## The Four Miles She Had To Do It Over

It is four miles from that shed to a gate and two miles back up a made road to a counting house, and she did not go and she thought about going and she did not go.

What she thought about was this. There is a woman of forty-five on a third floor with forty books open on a table the length of nine men who has never once said a thing to her that was not correct. There is a man with a brush on the floor below who has asked three times to see a slip and said yes the third time. There is a man behind a counter who keeps the only book on nine days of road that shuts.

If a woman of sixty-one can carry a thing eleven miles east on a cart, then a woman of eighteen with a strip against her ribs can carry the same strip two miles up a made road on foot, and it will go into a book that shuts, and it will be read, and it will be said out loud to her in a room with a lamp in it.

That is not a threat. That is four sentences of arithmetic and she did all of them standing in a yard in about eleven seconds and they came out the same every time, and she did not do it.

She has wanted that word read for a hundred and thirty days. She wanted it at the seventh hour of the twenty-eighth so badly that her hand was on the bar and her right arm was open, and she stood in that doorway for eleven minutes with a stranger in a yard and did not go up the hill.

## What Ning Yan Said Instead

She said nothing.

That was the whole of it, and it took her about eleven seconds, and she did it with her right arm standing open at her side and four fingers and a thumb on the hand that was holding the bar and her weight on her left leg, and she did not lie once while she did it.

The woman watched her for the length of it and then nodded, once, at nothing in particular.

“That is the correct answer,” she said, “and you did not give it to me. I am going to put it down as a person who did not answer, because that is what happened and I am not going to have a room eleven miles east of here holding anything else about you.”

Then Ning Yan said it out loud, in the ordinary voice, at seven feet, because the alternative was a stranger carrying a sentence east in her coat.

“That is fair,” the woman said. “You do not know me and I have not told you one thing about myself that I can back up, and you have been standing in a doorway a quarter of an hour and I am not going to pretend that was a mistake in your arithmetic.”

She put the folded sheet down on the boards on the threshold.

“I have walked to the end of this road and back and I have been turned back at a gate at the end of it and I have done that twice now, on two different days, and I would do it again. That is eleven miles each way and there is a gate at the end of it and a man with a chain, and he was not unkind about it and I am not going to say one word against him.”

“All right.”

“I did not come eleven miles to ask a question.”

She straightened up about half an inch and looked past Ning Yan at the boards, at the bar lying against the wall, at the ring of clay on the floor with the box beside it.

“I came because there is a line in a room eleven miles east of here that has not been answered in three months, and I am sixty-one years old, and I have read a hand in four seconds since I was twenty-two, and there is nothing else in this that I want.”

She did not open the sheet.

“If you ever want a stranger to read something for you, that is what I am. And I am not asking you about anything today and I am not going to ask you about anything this month.”
