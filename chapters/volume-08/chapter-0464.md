# Eighty-Four Days

She counted them into the boards.

It was on the eighteenth, in the afternoon, with the rain off and the sun on the shed door, and she had nothing in her hands and nothing in her lap and nothing in the lane, and she took the point out of the bar and made a small mark on the edge of a board and counted out loud the way she counts everything, which is the only way she has ever counted anything.

Eight rows of ten along the board and four over, and the point went in and came out and went in again, and eighty-four marks are in a shed at the end of a lane four miles east of a mill whose wheel has not turned since before she came.

She did not count toward anything. There was no date at the end of it to count toward.

That is the honest shape of it and she went and stood with it for a while: she has counted every day of her life to something. A pay day that did not exist on that ground. A date said out loud on a road. A month begun at a gate so she would know when to come back.

Eighty-four is what you get when there is nothing on the other end of the count.

## What Stopping Costs In Coin

Nothing.

That is the first thing she put down and she could hardly make her hand do it.

Five coppers in the fold in the inside of her coat. The rate in the building two hundred yards west of here is two coppers a page, and a second rate by the difficulty, and a third at twice the first for a hand nobody in the building can read. She read that board three times and could not touch it then and cannot touch it now.

The floor at the end of the lane pays a man a yard of sill by the yard and will not tell a stranger what it is, and she is not on the floor and she does not work there.

She has not spent a copper in seven days and there is nothing in this shed that costs money and there is nothing in this shed she is allowed to be given.

Stopping has cost her five coppers and no bread since the eleventh and a bar strap she bound herself out of her own coat lining with the point of the bar and her teeth.

That is what it comes to in money. That is the whole bill.

## What It Costs In Days

The furnace will not light itself.

That has been true since a shed in a winter a long way from here and it is not going to stop being true because she has done the right thing, and she has known it for as long as she has had a body and has never once had to put it next to anything else.

Every stage she has ever taken has cost somebody a pair of hands in a room for a number of hours. She asked once. She got a woman of sixty-one for two hours of six days, and that was the last time anybody on this ground offered her anything, and it was the beginning of the worst thing that has ever happened to her.

A stage needs a second pair of hands.

Nobody is going to turn up at the end of a lane with two hours of a day in them for eighty-four days. The floor closes its books on the third and pays nine men out of what it takes in and it is not short. The building pays two coppers a page and it is not short. The store five miles west sells bread at two coppers a loaf and she has five coppers.

There is no floor between here and the mill with a second pair of hands in it that anybody has offered her, and there has not been one since the second of a month, and there is not going to be one in eighty-four days, because nobody is paying for anything at all for eighty-four days.

Eighty-four days is two months and twenty-four days.

## The Stage That Is Not Coming

She has taken six stages in four years and every one of them cost somebody else something, and that is not a complaint, it is the shape of the trade, and she has never once minded paying it out of her own body.

She has minded paying it out of theirs.

A stage needs a room, a second pair of hands, and hours. Not a wish and not a resolution and not a woman deciding at the sixth hour on a lane that she has said the word and will now do the thing. Hands, in a room, for hours, and somebody has to give up something to put them there.

She asked a woman of sixty-one once, at a mill, for two hours of six days, and she was answered at the fourth hour and it took eleven days to come, and the whole of what she has got to show for eight months of it is a ring of clay and a name in a book she cannot get out of.

Nobody is paying for anything for eighty-four days.

That is the sentence, and she works it out on a board at the end of a lane, and there is not one person on nine days of road to say it to, and it is not a discovery. It is an addition. It goes on top of everything else she already knows, and it is the first time in eight years that the reason she cannot get stronger is that she has told somebody to stop.

## The Two Things That Are Not A Cost And Are A Cost

The first is that she is not hungry. Her body has not wanted bread since she was fourteen and it does not want it now and she is going to go eighty-four days on five coppers and water out of her own skin and she will not once go short, and there is nobody on nine days of road she can explain that to.

That is not a gift. She has worked out since the twenty-ninth of a month that a body which does not want anything is not a body that has been spared. It is a body that has had something taken out of it a long time ago by something that decided she would not be given anything.

The second is that she is eighteen years old, at the top of what this ground can measure, with four fingers and a thumb on one hand, an arm open half the time, eight of eleven holds, and short breath at four counts everywhere.

She cannot be paid, cannot be given anything, cannot put a false line in a book, cannot put a thing she has reached back where it came from, cannot call a halt to a thing she is in once it has started, and she has no wages and no season.

And at the end of eighty-four days she will be exactly that.

## The Hut

The woman of fifty came out to the shed on the nineteenth in the afternoon and stood in the doorway and looked at the marks on the board.

“Who counted those.”

“I did,” Ning Yan said.

The woman looked at them for a while.

“That is a strange thing to do on a floor,” she said.

“There is nothing on this ground that will pay for a thing to be done,” Ning Yan said. “So I am counting.”

“No,” the woman of fifty said. “I have been running this floor nineteen years and I have never once seen anybody count.”

She stood in the doorway with her hands behind her back for a while longer and then she said, “You are not on this floor.”

“No.”

“You are welcome at the sixth hour and the ninth hour.”

“Yes,” Ning Yan said.

“And I am going to tell you a thing I have not told anybody,” the woman said, “and you will not like it and you may do what you want with it.”

“Go on.”

“There is a girl,” the woman of fifty said, “who came down this lane twenty days before the thirteenth with a bundle of paper and stood at that table for four days and said nothing to anybody at all.”

Ning Yan stood at the door and did not say anything.

“She had a bar over her left shoulder and her right arm was open at the side,” the woman said. “I did not know who she was and I did not ask and I did not write her down, and she has been at my table eight mornings and I have not said one word about it.”

The rain came off the roof and found the bucket where it has always been.

“Ten days ago my man at the frame told me there was a woman sleeping in the shed at the end of the lane,” the woman said. “And I worked out that she was her, and I have been not saying anything about that for ten days as well, and that is me being exactly the same kind of coward I was about the gate.”

She turned round on the step.

“Do not thank me for telling you,” she said. “You have not done anything for me to be thanked for. You read my columns and you have never once been wrong.”

She went back down the lane.

Ning Yan stood at the door with that in her chest and found that she did not want to go and read a column at the sixth hour tomorrow more than she has wanted anything in this batch, and that a woman of fifty has just handed her the one thing nobody on nine days of road west has ever handed her, which is being known, and has made her ask for it.

## The Last Mark

She went back in and got the point out of the bar and put one more mark on the board, well away from the rows, at the other end, in a place where it could not be counted with the rest.

That is the one she is keeping for herself, the way she put a third pebble on a stone off a road once and swept it off in the same minute.

Eighty-four marks in a row and one mark at the other end of the board.

She does not know what is at the end of the eighty-fourth day and she is not going to pretend to herself that she does, and she wrote a mark on a board at the end of a lane at the sixth hour on the nineteenth day of a month with a door that does not shut properly and a bar she cannot hold at the heavy end and a folded sheet in a building behind her that says a name on a string.

She put the point in the bar and squared the blade and put the bar against the wall where it goes, and she went out and shut the door, and it did not shut properly, and she did not fix it.
