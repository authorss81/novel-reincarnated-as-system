# She Makes Him Stop

She went and shut the door of the shed on the thirteenth of a month, at the ninth hour, with the last of the light going off the lane, and she stood with her back against it.

It is nine feet by six and it has a bench on one side and a shelf with a lamp on it that has no oil in it and boards on the floor and the bar against the wall. She has slept in it since the twenty-sixth of a month and it has smelled of standing water the whole time.

She shut the door because a thing said in the open goes out into the air, and she has had two years of her own answers going up a road on the first and she was not having another one.

She stood there and waited until the light was gone and then she said it.

“Stop.”

## What She Said After

She did not say it twice. She has never asked anybody a question twice and she was not going to start with the one thing she had shut a door to say.

“I have been counting since the fifth,” she said. “I have been at the sixth hour every morning with your books on that bench and I have read every line of every one of them, and there are thirteen lines with a day in the left column that nobody on this floor put there.”

The boards were cold under her feet. Her breath ran four counts. Her arm hung open.

“Twelve of them are four years old. One of them is the fifteenth of this month and it is Zheng, and it is a load off the low end of the bank, two of them by the weight, and he took a day over it with two helpers, and the woman of fifty says he has been on that frame for eleven years.”

She stopped and made herself go on, because she had promised herself when she shut the door that she would say all of it in one piece.

“There are two figures against his line,” she said. “Both the same. Four days apart. The second one went into the book on the third day of this month.”

“A man at that gate laid a sill on the ninth of a month and came down the lane on the thirteenth and told a woman of fifty about it, and she paid him for it out of the take. He laid the same forty yards again on the twenty-first because the board said so. He was found on the fourth of this month in a hut four steps off the ground with his boots off and nothing done to him by anybody.”

She put her hand flat on the door behind her.

“You told me one number,” she said. “On the fifth of this month, in this shed, and you told me before you did it, and I want you to know that I noticed you telling me before you did it, because it is the only reason I have any of this at all.”

The shed was quiet.

“You have spent three times,” Ning Yan said, “and you told me once.”

## What He Said

He said nothing for a long time, which is not what she had expected and is the only thing that was left for him to do.

When he did speak it was in the present tense and it was short and it was not an argument, because he does not argue, because he told her at a shed at a mill four months ago that he was not going to argue it again and he has kept to it.

“I know what it is,” he said. “I have known all of it since the sixth.”

“Say it.”

“A listing is work and I am not on one,” he said. “There is no line in the ground for me to put a thing on, and there never has been, and I have looked. I put work where a hand already is and the hand is the only thing in it. I have never once been able to do the version where it is me.”

He said that the way he has said everything since she found him, in the present tense, in the ordinary voice, and she stood against a door and found that there was nothing whatever in it she could argue with, which is a thing she had not expected to get and had hoped for all the way up the lane.

## Stop

“I have asked you for one thing and I am asking you for it now,” she said. “I am not asking you for the number and I am not asking you for the man at the gate. I am asking you to stop putting work on boards where a hand will find it.”

“Your foundation.”

“Yes.”

“That is the whole of what you are asking me for,” he said. “Not the man. The man is not mine. The man was a man at a gate who did what a board said.”

“That is the whole of what I am asking you for.”

He did not answer for a while.

“There is a fourth one,” he said. “It has been in my hand since the twenty-second of a month and I have not spent it.”

“Then put it down.”

He did not put it down at that moment and she felt that happen, and she let it happen, because she cannot make a no take and she had learned that in a shed at a mill four months ago and she was not going to learn it again on the boards.

“I am not going to say that I will,” he said. “I am not going to say it and take it back. You have asked me for a thing I have never once in seven years put down for anybody, including you, and I am not going to lie to you in a shed tonight.”

“Then what is this.”

“This,” he said. “I have spent three times and told you once and a man is dead four steps off this lane and I have nothing to spend and you have found all of it out of a book with your face four inches off the paper.”

That was all of it. He did not agree to anything. He gave her no promise and no refusal and no reason and no name.

He took the fourth one and he put it down.

She felt it go. It was not like the drain and it was not like a floor going cold. It was a thing she had been carrying that was not there any more, and the place where it had been was quiet, and it was four days and it did not cost her anything at all, and she did not enjoy it.

## What She Did Not Do

She did not thank him. She stood against the door in the dark and she was not going to thank a man for doing the first thing he had ever done that she had not had to catch him at.

She did not forgive him. Those are two different things and she was careful about them in the dark because she has had a long time to get good at telling them apart and she was not going to put them in the same place tonight to make herself feel better.

She did not ask what it was worth.

That one she wanted. She stood on the boards in an empty shed at the ninth hour with a door shut against her back and she wanted very much to ask him for the sum of it, in days and hours, in one figure, the way he has given her every other one, and she did not ask, because she had just made him put a thing down and a woman who does that does not go straight back and ask what it came to.

## The Third Thing

There was a third thing and she got to it at the end and she had not planned it.

“You told me once,” she said. “Between the sixth and the twenty-second there were three spends and you told me about one of them.”

“Yes.”

“Why the first one.”

He was quiet a moment.

“Because you would have asked me not to do it,” he said.

The shed was very quiet and the rain came off the roof into the bucket at the low end.

“That is the answer,” Ning Yan said, in the ordinary voice, to nobody, on the boards. “You told me once because I would have stopped you, and then you did it twice more and you did not tell me either time, and a man went into a ditch on the ninth and lay down on the fourth and nobody on this ground but the three of us and the layer has any idea why.”

“I know what it is,” he said.

She stood against the door and let it stand and did not make it smaller and did not make it larger, because she has had two years of being entered by everything she says and she was not going to enter this.

“You have known since the sixth,” she said. “That is what you said in this shed a moment ago.”

“I have known what it is since the sixth,” he said. “I have not known that I was going to be asked to stop until the door shut.”

It was the last thing he said that night. She let it be the last thing.

## The Shut

She shut nothing else. She went out and pulled the door to and did not bar it, because there is nothing in that shed worth barring a door over and because a shut door on the end of a lane is a thing a person notices.

She stood in the lane in the dark for a while with the water coming off the cut and the frame standing empty along it.

The cut was running. The frame was empty. The building two miles west was lit window by window with a hundred people reading, and four hundred and seven names in a book in the ordinary hand under a man's arm, and the tied folder waiting on its shelf with its date on the string, and two yeses she had said without meaning in a room on a road.

She had nine coppers on the second of a month and five now, and no bread, and a rate of two coppers a page that she cannot touch, and an arm open half the time, and a place low behind her fourth rib that stayed quiet.

She walked down the lane to the crossing out of pure habit and stood four feet off the post.

The chair was empty. The board had five lines of chalk on it. She could not read a word of any of them and there was not going to be a sixth.

She stood there for a long while and then she said something out loud, to nobody, into the rain, in the ordinary voice, and she was aware while she was saying it that it was the first time since the twentieth of a month that she had said his name into an open room and got nothing back at all, and that she had expected it, and that expecting it was the worst part.
