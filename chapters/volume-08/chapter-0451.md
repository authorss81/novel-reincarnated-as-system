# The Board She Could Read

She went back on the twenty-seventh and the twenty-eighth and the twenty-ninth and the thirtieth, and nothing on the ground asked her a single thing.

That is not the same as being ignored. She knows the difference and she has been on the ignored end of it for two years, in rooms of nine men and a counter and a rail, where she was not asked, because nobody there had a reason to ask.

This was different. There were reasons. There were nine men on a frame and a chain and a cut and forty loads a day coming up the lane, and every one of them had something to be doing at the hour she arrived. The floor had simply not made room in the day for a woman who reads columns.

She learned that on the first morning and it settled her.

She slept in the shed at the floor and ate one loaf on the twenty-seventh and one on the twenty-eighth and had nothing on the twenty-ninth and was not hungry, and being not hungry on the twenty-ninth with an empty bag sat in her chest the way the four coppers sat in her chest at a plank bench, and she made herself look at it plainly instead of turning away from it.

Her body does not want bread. That has been true since the first stage and it has never once been a gift.

## The Day Sheet

On the second morning the man who had told her she was not on the list came along the bank with a sheet of paper in his hand and gave it to her without a word and went on up the bank.

She took it in her left hand.

The sheet was a foot and a half long, ruled in two columns with a line under each, and it was written in an ordinary hand, fast, with the pen held the way a man holds a pen who has been writing all day and has stopped noticing himself doing it.

She could not read it.

She stood on the bank for a quarter of an hour and looked at it and got nothing, the way she has got nothing off a hand since she was eleven years old. The letters ran together in places and separated in others. Where the writer had gone faster the letters leaned. Three words in the middle of the sheet had been gone over twice.

She has looked at a great many hands in her life and has never once been able to read a single word of one of them.

She turned it round and held it at arm's length, the way she has held every sheet a man has ever put in her hand, and she did what she does instead.

She read the figures.

## What She Read Instead

Six numbers stood in the left column. Eight in the right.

The left column was what came in and the right column was what went out over, and the two were not the same shape, and she worked them the way she works a column on a third floor, line against line, the one against the other, taking the totals the way a woman who has done it for two years takes them without needing to be told what a total is.

The right column had a number against six of the eight lines and a blank against two.

That is ordinary. You do not go back and write a number into a day's figures because you did not have it when you wrote the line.

She looked at the two blanks and then at the left column, and there was one figure in the left that had no match anywhere on the right, and it was the last line, and it was in a hand that had not hurried, because it had been written last with a clear pen and a steady arm.

She looked at it a long time.

Six in and eight out. One of the six went nowhere. On a floor where everything that comes in goes out over the cut, one line of the day's work has no other half.

She did not know what it was. She knew what it looked like, because she had spent two years looking at exactly this shape on a third floor with forty books open on it, where a column and its other column did not agree and nine days of road west there was not one other pair of eyes for it.

## The Line She Could Read

Under the figures, at the foot of the sheet, the writer had put the day's work in a line of plain words.

She read that.

It went something like this, and she would have said it the same way herself:

“Six carts off the country road. Four over by the load. Two by the weight. Sill at the crossing thirty yards, the old one, by the length, loose at the low end. Chain stiff, oil it. Zheng's leg, no.”

She stood on the bank and read that line twice and felt something go out of her shoulders that she had not known was up.

Because that is a floor talking. That is a man writing down the shape of a day in the words the day was made of, and every one of those words is a word she has used for two years. Loads by the load. Weight by the weight. A length of sill by the length, because a sill is not measured by what comes off it. A chain that needs oil. A man's leg.

She could not read a word of his hand.

She could read every word of his day.

## What She Said About It

She carried the sheet to the low door in the far bank and put it on the table inside, square, the way a man with a brush squares a sheet, and she did not say anything about it.

Then she waited, because she has learned to wait.

At the end of two hours the man who had given it to her came out and picked it up and looked at it and looked at her.

“You squared it,” he said.

“Yes,” she said.

“Nobody squares it.”

“They should,” she said. “It goes in a book.”

He looked at the sheet for a while and then he said, “Say the other thing.”

She said it in the ordinary voice, on a bank, in the open, the way she has said a floor sheet out loud in a room about a hundred feet long with nine men in it:

“Six carts came in and eight lines went out and two of those eight are the same cart twice, which is ordinary, because a cart that comes over loaded and goes back light is two lines and not one. The last line has no other half. That is the only line on the sheet that has not been finished, and there is a length of sill at the crossing at the foot of the page, and I have not had that line explained to me and I am not asking.”

He looked at the sheet again.

“That is true,” he said. “And the sill is not on the sheet yet. That is the day’s work. It goes on when he says he has done it.”

“When does he say he has done it.”

“When he comes down the lane,” the man said. “He does not come down the lane every day. He comes when the chain wants oiling or he wants something off the floor and he talks on his way past, and then I put the day against it, and that is the day I have. That is not the day he did it.”

He went inside. She stood on the bank and watched nine men go over a cut and did not follow him in.

## The Lane End

She carried the day sheet in on the twenty-ninth because it had come to the point where she could not put it down without checking it, and she stood at the table in the low door at the fourth hour in the afternoon with it in her left hand.

The man was not there. The room was.

She put it on the table and squared it and stood looking at it for a quarter of an hour.

The left-hand column was empty.

She had been looking at that column every day for four days without once stopping to notice what it was telling her, and she stopped now, on a floor at the end of a lane, in a nine-foot room with a grey stove, with a day sheet in front of her and the room to herself.

Every line in it has two cells side by side under one rule and the right-hand cell has something in it and the left-hand cell has nothing in it at all.

Six lines. Six empty cells going down a ruled column.

She put her thumb on one of them and it came away clean and she looked at the thumb and there was nothing on it.

Nobody on nine days of road west has ever put a date in a left-hand column on anything. She has read forty thousand columns. She has read a third floor for two years and a lifting floor sheet once and a book of four years on the end of a lane, and every column anybody has ever drawn for her has had one thing in it and that thing was the day the thing happened.

Two columns and one of them ruled for nothing.

That is not an error. Errors get corrected; a ruled line that has been under a pen for a hundred years and a day does not get left empty by accident by two people who both know what they are doing.

She left the sheet square and went out into the rain.

## The Lane End

At dusk on the thirtieth she walked east along the lane for the first time in her life, past the frame and past the shed, to where the lane ends.

It ends at a crossing. A gate across the lane on two posts, with a chain hung between them, and a gate track running off north and south into country she had no idea was there because on nine days of road nothing had ever come out of it.

There is a board nailed to the near post. It is a foot wide and a foot high, painted, with the day's work chalked on it in a hand that does not hurry at all.

She could not read the chalk. Not one word of it.

She stood four feet off the gate and looked at a board she could not read on a gate she had never been through, at the end of a lane she had walked for the first time, in a country she had reached by walking two hundred yards past a building full of lamps.

She did not touch the board. She did not go through the gate. She turned round and walked the mile back to the shed with the bar over her shoulder and the water smell in her clothes.

The last of her bread was gone by then and she was not hungry and she slept on boards with her hands behind her head and lay there working out six carts and eight lines and one of the six with nothing on the other side of it.
