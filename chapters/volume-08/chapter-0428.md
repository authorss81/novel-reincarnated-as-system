# What He Has Watched Twice

The made road east of the turn runs two miles between a field on one side and a hedge on the other, and he was on it at the ninth hour on the twenty-third of a month with the notebook shut under his arm and nine feet off it.

She came round the hedge from the yard at the turn and he had been there before she came round and he did not move.

She had come out with nothing in her hands and there was a lamp burning in a building she could not see from there, and she was going to stand nine feet off a man she had asked four times in seven months and she had never once got a second question out of.

She had one.

## The Question

She stopped at eleven feet.

“Can a thing that has been read by somebody else be read back by the person it is about.”

He did not make her wait.

“Yes.”

“Does that happen.”

“It happens.”

“How often.”

“Twice,” he said. “I have watched it happen twice and I have watched both of them start with a person who asked me a question about somebody else first.”

She stood in the dry grass.

“Then it is not usually a good idea.”

“It is not usually a good idea and it is not always a bad one and I am not going to help you work out which it is going to be for you, because I have got two of them and two is not enough to be a pattern.”

“Who were the two.”

“I am not going to tell you that.”

“All right.”

“You are not going to ask me twice,” he said. “You have not asked me twice about anything in seven months and that is not an accident and it is not politeness and I want you to know I have noticed it.”

## The Nine Feet

He had been standing in that grass for six months and there were two places in it worn down the length of a boot and nothing else in the grass worn at all.

“I have got a question and it is about somebody else and I am going to spend it on you and then I am going to go down this road and dig a run,” Ning Yan said.

“All right.”

“I want to know whether it is a thing anybody does, or whether it is a thing I have invented.”

“It is a thing people do,” he said, “and the reason I can tell you that in four seconds and the reason I cannot tell you what happens next in any number of seconds is that it is done at both ends. Somebody reads it, and then somebody else stands in a room and hears it, and the second one is a person and not a page, and by the time it is a person it is not in your hand any more.”

“You have seen it.”

“I have seen it be put on and I have seen it come off again.”

“Those are different.”

“They are,” he said, “and I am not going to pretend to you that they are the same, because the first one is a trade and the second one is an injury and I have watched a woman take the second one on the strength of the first one.”

He shifted the notebook about nine inches under his arm.

“You came up that road to ask me that and you did not ask me a single question about myself, and I have been waiting six months for you to do that and it is the reason I have never once moved off this grass.”

## What He Bought

He bought nothing. He has never bought anything.

There was a notebook under his arm and it was hers on no page in it, and he did not take it out, and he did not write anything down, and he had not come nine feet closer.

He had wanted something from her since the spring before last and had never been refused it and never been given it, and he said none of that, and the people who live on that line still did not know that she existed.

He told her a true thing about a question she had been carrying for three days, at nine feet off, in the ordinary voice, and it had cost him nothing and it had not been a warning and he had not made it into one.

That is the whole of the twenty-third of a month.

## The Fourth Hour On The Twenty-Fourth

It was the fourth hour and it was one of the four, and she went up the nine steps at the fourth hour with the strip in her left hand, because going up that stair with something in her hands had been the arrangement since the eighth of a month four months back and she was not going to break it on the twenty-fourth.

Rao had the same pencil and the same knife and forty books open on a table the length of nine men.

She read nine columns out loud from the top, dates down, hours against them, stop at the end, in the ordinary voice, and she got all nine right.

Rao did not say anything about it.

She had said nothing for sixteen years about a column getting read correctly on that floor, and she was not going to start, and she turned the page over at the end of the ninth one and moved the pencil to the margin.

## The Man With The Brush

He was on the floor below with the tray on the bench and the book open under it.

He asked her for the slip.

She had known he would, because he had asked three times, and the third time he had said yes before she had got the words out, and she had gone away up the made road four miles afterwards and worked out that a man who says yes in the ordinary voice to a thing he has asked for three times is a man who has been waiting for it.

“Again,” he said.

“Yes.”

He did not say thank you. He put the brush down in the groove and squared the second sheet and copied four words out of a slip of paper into a book that shuts, and he read them over once as he wrote them and did not ask her what they were about.

The margin correction on that floor was four. It had been four since before she came.

## What Rao Did Not Say

Rao turned the page over at the end of the ninth column and moved the pencil into the margin, which is what she does at the end of every set, and she did not say one word about nine columns having been read correctly on that floor.

She has said nothing about it for sixteen years and she was not going to start on the twenty-fourth of a month, and there is not a person on nine days of road who has ever been paid for reading and there is not going to be one.

She read two of them back at the eighth hour because she wanted them checked, which she has never done for anybody at that table and did not say that she had done it.

Then she said, “You have gone up this stair on the fourth and the eleventh and the eighteenth and the twenty-fourth for two months and you have not climbed one flight of this building, and I have noticed, and I have decided not to say anything about it.”

“All right.”

“You are here to read.”

“Yes.”

“Next time you are here, read.”

She put the pencil down flat and picked up the knife.

## What Was On The Board

She went down to the rail at the sixth hour on the twenty-fourth and there were thirty-four lines on it, and she read them all and got them the same twice, and she could take none of them, and every line was measured by the weight or by the length and there was not one of them measured by the voice.

That was a board that had been at eight lines for a year and had gone to fifty-one in a month, and a man of forty-one had told her standing at the rail with his back to the hut door that he wanted her to know it was the worst thing that can happen to a list.

She had gone down to the rail every morning at the sixth hour for seven months and there was nowhere else to go at the sixth hour.

## The Yard At The Turn

There is a yard about six hundred feet east of the turn where nine men work on four men’s work and it is three days in seven and she does not stop going, and she was there on the twenty-sixth at the fourth hour with nothing in her hands.

The office end had a pot in it and a cup in it and a woman in it who has never once asked her name, and the woman came out and put it into her hands and went back in.

Her hand took it, and that was the whole of what her hand did.

She stood at the low side of that yard for a minute and a quarter and then put it down on the edge of the bench with the office end against it, which is where things go at that yard, and watched it stop being hot.

And then a woman of about fifty came out of a doorway at the other end of that yard with a cup of something hot in it and looked down nine hundred feet of flat ground at a woman at the low side with an empty cup on a bench.

Ning Yan watched a woman with a cup decide, in four seconds, not to walk nine hundred feet.

She worked that out on the walk back, standing up, in nine seconds. The woman had four men and a bench and a yard and a thing she was carrying that was hot, and nine hundred feet of flat ground is not far, and in a different country a person would have walked it.

And that this is the second time.

The first one had been a man at the far end of a bench at that same yard, who had been told no in front of strangers and had not said one word about it afterwards and had got on with his day.

She put that on the boards on the twenty-sixth at the second hour with everything else, because it is the same shape both times, and she does not throw lines away.

## On The Way Back

She was coming up the four miles in the flat light at about the sixth hour with the bar over her right shoulder and there was a yard on the low side with nine men on the north end, and it is not her yard and she has no business on it, and two of the nine were standing at a gate post about nine feet inside it.

She heard one of them ask the other a question.

She did not stop and she did not go into the cut and she went past at the speed a person goes past at when they have a bar over their right shoulder and four fingers and a thumb on it, and she caught about two thirds of it.

“— the one that reads.”

“What did you say about her.”

“I asked four of them and I am not telling four of you what I asked.”

They went back to work. That was all of it. She was nine feet past the gate and going up the made road with the mill on the left of it the whole way.

She stood at the flat ground in front of a shed at a mill whose wheel has not turned since before she came and she worked out, standing up, in nine seconds, what a man at the north end of a yard does when he has worked out something about a woman at a gate.

He asks four men a question about her out loud.

And he tells nobody what the answer was.
