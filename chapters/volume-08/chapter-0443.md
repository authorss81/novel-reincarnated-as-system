# What He Decided In His Second Year

She asked him on the first of the month, at the sixth hour in the morning, with the road empty both ways.

She had waited for it. She had been turned back twice and let through five times and had never asked him for the reason. She had watched him lift the chain and set the cup down without sound and look at everybody for four seconds. She had counted four hundred and more in his count and had never asked him what the count cost him.

“May I ask you something,” she said.

“You may,” he said. “I may not answer it.”

“What does your rule tell you to do with the people you turn back.”

He set the cup down on the stool. He looked at her for four seconds and then at the road. He was about fifty and he had been at that gate nine years and there was nothing in him in the way of anything.

He said, “It does not tell me.”

He picked the cup up and put it down again.

He said, “I decided in my second year what to do about that on my own.”

He looked at the building with the lamps in it and back at her.

He said, “I am not going to say what I decided.”

He said, “And I am not going to be asked twice.”

That was all. Five sentences, short, broken across beats with pauses where he handled the cup, never a speech, never in the same words as anything he had said before. She counted them because she counts everything. They were his own beat, spoken once, at the same turnstile, and not again.

“All right,” she said.

She went and stood off to the side where people who are waiting stand. She did not ask twice. She would never ask twice. The condition was plain and she kept it the way she keeps every condition men at gates put on her.

## The Cup Again

At midday he drank from his cup and set it down without sound. She watched because watching steady hands steadies her. Nine years at that gate, four hundred and more turned back, nine hundred carts nodded through. The cup has been lifted and set down ten thousand times. It still sets down without sound. That is practice. She respects practice. She has squared sheets and carried bars and read columns until the work sits in her shoulders, and she knows what ten thousand repetitions cost and what they buy.

He caught her watching and lifted the cup a finger’s width in salute before drinking. She nodded back. No words. Gate men and women waiting understand each other. The chain hangs. The road runs. The building works on, paper and breath, a hundred readers by lamplight. His five sentences sit in her chest, separate, each in its own hour. It does not tell me. I decided. I am not going to say. Not twice. She keeps them the way she keeps dates, exact.

## The Evening Road

In the evening she walked four hundred yards down the road and back, to stretch the leg. The kerb stones held the day’s warmth through her boots. A cart passed going west with empty trays, driver nodding, her nodding back. The building lit behind her, lamps one by one. The gate stood with its chain hung in a loop and the man on his stool. She thought about second years, his and hers.

His second year he decided what to do with people turned back and keeps it nine years. Her second year reading she answered because answering was the only thing she had instead of strength. Both decisions hold. His keeps a gate kind. Hers brought an Order to her door. She walks back to the gate with that measured true, neither proud nor ashamed, only exact.

## The Night Watch

She sleeps light at the gate, bar beside her, waking once to check the chain by starlight. It hangs in its loop, quiet. The man breathes slow on his stool, cup set square. She counts her breaths to sleep again, four counts in, four out, steady. His decision keeps him. Hers keeps her. Both hold through the night.

## The Rest Of The Day

She carried bundles to the third table and back for the empty string. She squared sheets with edges exact. She ate bread outside at midday with her back to the lime wash, chewing slow because bread for one day must last. She has nine coppers in her fold. The price of a page in that building is two, and she did the arithmetic once in the morning and put it aside.

The young reader worked his forty hands a month without looking at the tied sheet on the counter. The woman with the thumb worked without looking at it. Everybody knew it waited and nobody touched it. That is what a competent room does. It sets aside what it cannot read and goes on.

At the fourth hour a cart came down from the building with marked sheets tied with string and went through the gate with the chain down. The man nodded it through. She watched from the counter end, dust settling along the kerb. Four hundred and more turned back in nine years, nine hundred carts nodded through. Both are his work. The rule tells him when to put the chain across and when to lift it. The rest he decided himself in his second year and keeps.

She thought about what it costs to keep one decision nine years without saying what it is. She keeps her own: what the fine hand says about her, refused forever on purpose, never reopened, never softened, never mentioned in passing. Keeping costs. She knows. She respects a man who pays it without asking praise.

At the sixth hour she fetched her bar at the gate and checked its strap. The man sat on his stool with his cup cold in his hand. He said, “Road is clear tomorrow,” and she said, “Good,” and that was their whole talk. It was enough. Gate men and women waiting understand each other without speeches.

She thought about it for the rest of the day. His rule does not tell him what to do with the people he turns back. In nine years he has turned back four hundred and more, carts and people on foot, and the rule tells him when to put the chain across and when to lift it and nothing else.

So in his second year he decided something on his own about the rest of it, about the four hundred yards and the waiting and the word said not unkind and not as a shout, and he is not going to say what it is.

She felt anger rise once, quick, at being shut out, and then it went down. He is a gate man and he stays a gate man. He is never inside the building. He is never recruited. He keeps a turnstile at the end of a long road and he has a cup and a stool and a chain and he decided one thing for himself and he keeps it. That is more than most people on nine days of road have.

At midday he said, “You keep your bread dry.”

“I do,” she said.

“Good,” he said. “The rains come early past the kerb.”

That was the whole of their talk that day. She fetched water and carried bundles to the third table and watched the tied sheet wait on the counter. At night she lay at the gate with her hands behind her head.

“You asked well,” he said in the dark. “I am glad you did not ask twice.”

“I will not,” she said. “It is his. Let him keep it.”

She slept without writing any of them down. Four of the five were answers to questions he had not been asked. The fifth was a decision he had made once, in his second year, and had not told anybody since, including the woman walking up the road toward him at that moment with a bar on her shoulder and no question left to ask.
