# The Road With Kerb On It

She walked it on the first of the month with bread for two days in her coat and water in her skin and the bar over her left shoulder.

The road east runs eleven miles from the shed at the mill to the gate with the chain on it, and she had walked it twice now, once each way on the second to the fifth and once each way after. Her feet knew the seventh mile where the kerb starts. Her body knew the place at a mile and a half where the leg stops taking an order if she has pushed it.

Her breath ran four counts everywhere, and inside a loaded reach it ran four as well, which had not happened since a dressed stone went the length of her own arm in front of nine men.

She did not hurry. The sun stood high and the dust hung. A cart passed her going west with an empty tray and the driver nodded and she nodded back. Nobody asked her name. Nobody on that road knew there was a woman on it who could read a fine hand.

She reached the gate at the fourth hour. The man had the chain across and the cup in his hand. He lifted the chain off the post without a word and she went through. He wrote nothing down. That was the whole of the crossing. No book shut. Nothing failed.

Past the gate the road keeps going two hundred yards to the building. She had stood off it for days. Now she walked it.

The building is one storey and a hundred and forty feet front and lime-washed low and long against the ground. In daylight it looks smaller than it looks at night. The windows stand in a row and every one of them holds a lamp even in daylight, unlit, cleaned, the glass wiped.

She counted them without meaning to count them because that is what she does with lamps. There were more lamps in it than anything she had stood in, more than the counting house with forty books open at once, more than the lifting floor with its nine men.

She stopped two hundred yards off it the way she had stopped before. Her arm hung open. Her right hand held four fingers and a thumb round the strap of the bar. The aperture through her stayed cracked through and permanent and quiet.

## The Seventh Mile

At the seventh mile she stopped and set the bar butt down in the dirt and stood with her weight on her left leg and looked at the kerb.

It runs true. Each stone sits flush with the next, the joints filled, the top face worn smooth by wheels. Moss grows in the joints on the shaded side. She knelt and put her left hand flat on one stone. It was cool through the palm. It had been laid by men paid by the day, with families on that ground, and it had held four hundred years.

She knows work that holds. She has squared sheets and carried bars and read columns for two years for nothing, and she knows the difference between work done for a season and work done for a lifetime.

Her leg trembled once and held. She stood on the grip of the bar for nine seconds with her whole weight, the way she learned at a gate in a different year, and the tremble went out of it. Her breath stayed four counts. The small open place low behind her fourth rib stayed quiet.

She went on. The dust came up round her boots and settled on the hem of her coat. The sun moved a finger’s width. A second cart passed her going east with bundled paper tied with string, and the driver kept his eyes on the team and she kept hers on the road, and neither spoke, which suited them both.

## What The Lamps Cost

She counted the windows again at a hundred yards, to be sure.

Twenty-one windows on the long side, each holding a lamp, each lamp cleaned, each glass wiped. Oil, wicks, glass, hands to tend them. On nine days of road a lamp is a thing a shed does without. Here lamps stand in daylight, unlit, waiting for dusk. She worked the cost in her head without wanting to work it, the way she works every cost, and stopped halfway because the figures ran past anything she owns.

She has eleven coppers in her fold and bread for two days. The building burns more oil in a night than she has held in seven months.

That is the whole of the distance between her and that building, measured in oil. It steadied her to have it plain. She is eighteen, with four fingers and a thumb on one hand and an arm open half the time and eight of eleven holds, and she can read a hand that room cannot yet read, and she cannot pay for bread past tomorrow without counting. Both things are true. Neither cancels the other.

A woman came out of the near end with a bucket and emptied it at the side and went back in without looking up. A man carried a bundle of cut paper in through the same door. Nobody lingered. Nobody stood in the doorway talking. The place worked the way a copying floor works, steady, without wasted motion.

She went the last two hundred yards.

## The Counter She Had Not Seen

The near end holds a counter. It is plain wood, worn at the edge where hands have rested. Behind it stands a man with a brush in his hand and a sheet under it. He looked up when her shadow crossed the boards.

“Yes,” he said.

“I am here to see the room,” she said. “I was told I could ask one question in it.”

“What question.”

“Not here,” she said. “In a room with a lamp in it. About one person.”

He studied her. He took in the bar, the arm, the hand with four fingers on it, the coat with the strip folded against her ribs that she has refused forever on purpose to have read. He did not ask about any of it.

“Wait,” he said.

She waited by the counter with her weight on her left leg. Past the counter the room runs the length of the building, tables down the middle, lamps on every table, people reading. About a hundred of them. The sound was paper and breath and nothing else. No hall. Nothing called a hall. One building, one book, one rate, one rule, and she did not yet know the rule.

The air was warm the way a room with a hundred people in it is warm. It touched her face. She had not been warm like that since the shed in winter with a ring coming up a finger’s width.

A young man at the third table looked up once, glanced at her bar, and went back to his sheet. Nobody else looked up.

## What She Did Not Do

She did not put the bar down. She did not offer her name. She did not take out her coppers. She had eleven and bread for two days and she kept both shut.

## The Wait By The Counter

The man with the brush left her standing while he finished his sheet. She did not mind the wait. Waiting is a thing she knows. She stood with her weight on her left leg and watched the room work.

A reader at the second table finished a sheet, dotted it, squared it, set it on the pile at his elbow without looking up. A carrier took the pile to the far end. The man of sixty-one with the book under his arm opened it, marked something in it, shut it again. The carrier brought back empty folders. The pile grew and shrank and grew again. Paper and breath. The lamps burned steady in daylight, trimmed, the flames standing straight.

Her arm ached from the shoulder down. She shifted the ache into her back the way she shifts every ache, by changing where she stands inside it. She can still say no to pain. She just cannot make the pain take orders. That made her smile once, tired, into the warm air.

“You stand well,” the man with the brush said without looking up. “Most people lean.”

“I have stood at rails,” she said.

He nodded and went on with his line. His brush moved sure and quick. The sheet under it held an ordinary hand, plain strokes, wide spacing. He read it down, marked two lines, set it aside. Forty hands a month move through that room, and his is one pair of the hands that move them, and he treats each sheet as if it were the first of the day.

Xie Changyin came through from down the room with a sheet in her hand and stopped at the counter.

“She is here,” Xie Changyin said to the man with the brush. “She walks the road herself.”

“I see her,” the man said.

“She may ask,” Xie Changyin said. “It was offered.”

The man nodded. He set the brush down across the sheet, exact, parallel to the edge.

“On the second day,” he said to Ning Yan. “There are people ahead of you. Come back on the second day and it will be heard.”

“All right,” she said.

She turned and went back out past the windows with the lamps in them and stood in the road and looked at the building she had finally stood in. Her heart beat hard once and settled. She had wanted to run on the way in and had not run. That was enough for one day.

She went back to the gate and slept off the road that night with the bar beside her and the building lit behind her eyelids when she shut her eyes.

She had walked eleven miles in three days the first time and two days the second. This time she had walked the last two hundred yards, and it had taken her four days of standing still to do it.
