# The Four Hundred Times

She got out to the turn on the sixth at the second hour with the bar over her right shoulder and there were two carts standing at the cart stand and a third one backed in at an angle with a shaft up.

The man on the third one was about thirty-four and he had a whinny whip folded twice going past at the speed of a man doing nothing, and he had been doing nothing for a while.

She came up and she did not say anything for about a minute, because it is not a thing you ask a man out of a cart at the second hour without letting the ground say something first.

Then she said, “You have been to the end of the long road.”

“I have.”

“How long ago.”

“Two months and a bit.”

## Four Sentences About A Road

He looked at her and then at the road and then at the road again.

“There is a place at the east end of that road where strips go and they do not come back.”

“You took one out two months and a bit ago.”

“I took one out two months and a bit ago.”

“And nothing came back on you.”

He turned the whip over in his hands once.

“A journey with nothing on it is not a journey,” he said. “And I was paid for the going and not for the coming back, and there was no difference between the two of them in anybody’s book, because the second one was a cart coming back empty.”

He said all of it looking at the road. He did not turn round once.

## What She Asked Him

She asked him four things and she asked them one at a time with a space between them, because she is a person who has learned that four questions at once gets one answer.

“How much is the going.”

“Fourteen for a cart and twenty-nine for a man with nothing on him.”

“And the coming back.”

“Same. That is the part I want to say out loud for you, because I have said it to four people this year and nobody has believed it. It is the same. Fourteen out and fourteen back and the difference between the two ends is not a thing anybody is paid for.”

“And how many times have you been turned back at the end of it.”

He stopped with the whip in his hand.

“Four hundred and about forty,” he said. “I have counted them on my own fingers and I have counted them on the gate, and the gate is where a man with a chain stands and he counts too, and he has counted more than me because he has been standing there longer.”

## What A Carter Knows About Strips

He got the butt of the whip under his boot and worked at it for a minute.

“I will tell you what I know, and I will tell it to you as a carter and not as anything else, because I have never in nine years at this stand had anybody ask me anything at all about where a thing goes.”

“All right.”

“A strip is a piece of paper with a day on it and something written on it by a person who was in a room, and the room it came out of is not in this country.”

“That I know.”

“Then you know more than I do about the room and less than I do about the road,” he said. “I have taken strips to the end of that road four hundred and forty times and I have never seen one come back. Not one. And I have never asked, because it is not in the job, and there is a man at the gate who has never asked either and we are two men who have not asked each other either.”

He got his foot off the whip.

“That is not a mystery. That is a road. Things go out on it and they do not come back on it and you can live a whole life at this stand not knowing and be perfectly right about everything you do know.”

## Turning Back Is Ordinary

He got down off the cart and put his heel up on the shaft.

“Turning back is the ordinary end of that journey,” he said. “I have been paid for going and I have been paid for coming back. I have been paid for standing in a cart at the end of a road while a man with a chain works out which side of it I am on.”

“You want to know what I think of that and I will tell you what I think of it: I think nothing about it, because thinking about it is not in the job, and I have been in the job eleven years.”

“All right.”

“And I have been at this stand nine years and I have never once seen anything come back down that road from the far side of it. Not a strip, not a page, not a tray with a cord on it, not a person.”

## The Bank Behind The Stand

There were three carts standing at the cart stand and two of them were loaded and one was empty, and the loaded two belonged to a woman of twenty who was sitting on the tail board of the near one with a whinny whip folded twice.

She got up off the board when she saw Ning Yan come round the end of it and came over, and she did not say good afternoon and she did not ask anything.

“You are the one at the gate three miles east,” she said. “I have been looking at that gate for two months.”

“Yes.”

“You have not been on it.”

“No.”

“You have not been on it and I am not going to say anything about that,” Wei Ran said, “and I have four men and a bank to get off before the fourth and I would rather have told you in one sentence than have worked it out over two months.”

She put the whip under her arm.

“Twenty-nine for a man with nothing on him, and eight a day at the bank, and I do not move either of them for anybody, and I have said them to you now and you can go east or you can not and it does not make any difference to anything I am doing on the twenty-eighth.”

## The Eight Coppers

That was the sentence she came away with.

She stood at the cart stand and worked out four things while he went and got a drink of water out of a barrel and did not offer her one, and all four of them were about a road and none of them was about a shed.

Somebody keeps strips at the east end. Strips go there and do not come back. And on the twenty-eighth of a month three days before she stood in her own doorway a strip with one date on it came down a mill road on a cart that does not go east.

She had been owed eight coppers since the twenty-third of a month three months back, by a woman of twenty with a team and a rate, for nine hundred feet of cart track cut in two days.

She could not take the eight. She had tried twice on a plank bench and her hand had stopped in the air over it both times.

She could not pay anything either, because there was nothing here to pay for that she had not already given, and she was standing at a cart stand at the second hour of the sixth of a month with eighteen coppers in the fold in the inside of the door frame four miles back.

Eight was not paid. She had said the number out loud twice, in a shed, on the boards, at the second hour, three months back, and she had never once said it to the woman it was owed to.

## What He Did Not Ask

He came back with the water and drank it and did not ask her a single question.

That was what she took away from it and it was the part that made her stand there for another minute.

In seven months, four people on nine days of road had asked her a question about something she had done. Two of the four were paid by nobody either, and neither of those two had been in a cart. Eleven people at this stand go east eleven times a month and every one of them is looked at by a man with a rule in his hand or a man with a chalk, and not one of them has ever looked at the hands.

He was thirty-four and he had counted four hundred and forty turnings on his own fingers and he did not ask a woman with four fingers and a thumb where her fifth one went.

## What She Took Away

Four things, and she wrote them out at the boards at the second hour of the seventh and she did not put anything under them.

One. Strips go east and do not come back.

Two. A cart that does not go east brought one back on the twenty-eighth of a month with a date on it and nothing else on it.

Three. The date is the eighteenth of a month three months back, and that is the day a line came down this road that no hand on nine days of road can read.

Four. Nobody who walks in from the far side of the turn has ever walked out again, and a carter at a cart stand has thought about that once and has not thought about it since.

She lay there and looked at the four of them.

The first three add up. That is the whole of what she got from a man with a whip folded twice who did not want trouble and was not being evasive with her. There was nothing to tell and he had told her nothing, and what he had done was confirm three things she had already got and put a fourth one on the end of them.

The fourth one did not add up to anything at all. It is a thing a man says about a road.

She wrote it down anyway, with the other three, because it is hers now and she has never once thrown away a line on her own account. The four erased months are the only thing she has ever thrown away and she did not do that on purpose.

## The Far Side Of The Turn

She went west at the third hour with the bar over her right shoulder and she was two hundred yards back down the road when she stopped and turned round and shouted, which she does not do.

“The woman who came to my shed,” she said. “On foot. She was turned back at your gate.”

He took his time about it.

“Aye,” he said.

“Did she come back on a cart.”

“She did not come back on anything,” he said. “She came back on her own two feet. I have seen her go up the made road at the fourth hour with a lamp going in that place eleven miles east of here, and I have seen her come back down it on the ninth.”

“Nobody who walks in from there has ever walked out again, and I will tell you the honest thing about that, which is that it is not a thing anybody at this stand thinks about.”

He put the whip under his arm.

“A woman has walked up that road in three months and not one of you has asked me a single thing about the far side of the turn. That is not a complaint. It is just the shape of it.”
