# The Question He Would Not Answer

She met him on the eighteenth of the month on the made road east of the turn, going back to the gate with empty hands.

He stood nine feet off the road with the notebook shut under his arm. He was the same man she had stopped eleven feet short of by a hedge, a month back, polite, patient, buying nothing, asking nothing. The notebook has never once been open in front of her. His coat was dusty at the hem from the road.

“Ning Yan,” he said, and nodded.

“Bai Zhu,” she said. She had never called him Hunter to his face and was not going to start.

She stopped in the road. Her bar was at the gate and her hands were empty and her breath ran four counts. Her arm hung open. She had one thing she wanted to ask and she had been carrying it since the tied sheet went on the counter.

## The Dust

A cart went through the gate behind her with the chain down, wheels throwing dust up along the kerb. The dust hung in the light and settled on her boots. She waited until it settled before speaking, because she wants her questions clean.

He waited too. He is a man who waits for a living, polite, patient, genuinely good at the job. The notebook sits shut under his arm, the corners worn soft. He has carried it for years. She has asked him once before whether a thing read by another can be read back by the person it is about, and he told her yes, and that it is not usually a good idea, and that he has watched it happen twice. Twice, and not who.

The road east of the turn runs straight here, kerb on both sides, the stones flush, moss in the joints on the shaded side. She knows each stone by now. She has walked eleven miles and three days on foot and two days after, and her feet know the seventh mile the way her hands know rails.

“I have a question,” she said. “You do not have to answer it.”

“Ask,” he said.

“Has an institution that stops reading a person done something to them.”

He looked at her for a long time. Down the road a cart went through the gate with the chain down. The man with the chain nodded it through. The dust settled.

“No,” he said.

That was all for a while. One word in the ordinary voice, the way he always speaks. He never lies. He never threatens. He never touches her. He never enters her rooms. He never pays for force. He never asks her a question.

Then he shifted the notebook under his arm, exact, the way a man shifts a thing he has carried a long time.

“The other thing is this,” he said. “You asked me. A person who has been unread for four days is harder to find than a person who has been read for four years. That is the whole of it.”

She waited. He looked down the road toward the building with the lamps in it and back at her.

“This is the first question anybody has ever asked me that I would rather not answer,” he said.

He did not say who. She did not ask twice. That was the rule between them and she kept it.

“All right,” she said.


## The Notebook

She has never seen it open. The corners are worn soft, the string tie frayed. It rides under his arm as if grown there. She wonders what sits inside it, what hands he has watched, what twice means when he says he has watched reading-back happen twice.

The asking came up in her throat at the seventh mile and she held it where it was. He had given her one sentence past the one she paid for. She was not going into his coat for a second. A man who will not name a thing he has seen is telling you the shape of it, and she was clever enough to take the shape and polite enough not to press for the thing itself.

That is the rule between them and she keeps it the way she keeps every rule that lets a decent man stay decent.

They pass the seventh mile where kerb starts. She puts her hand on a kerb stone in passing, brief, cool through the palm. Four hundred years of maintenance under her fingers. He notices and says nothing. He notices everything and says little. That is his trade, watching, the way hers is reading. For a moment they are two trades walking one road, matched, neither owing the other.


## The Walk Back

She turned and walked back toward the gate with him beside the road, nine feet off, matching her pace without crowding it. Neither spoke for a while. The kerb ran on, stone after stone. A carrier passed going east with empty folders tied with string, nodded to them both, went on.

She thought about four days unread. She has been read by offices for two years, entered three times, answered because answering was the only thing she had instead of strength. The strips went east with her answers on them. The last one did not come back on a cart at all.

The floor treats her as somebody who may know something. The building has her reading on a tied sheet it cannot yet place. To be unread now would be to step off every page at once. It frightens her and it tempts her in equal parts.

“You walk well,” he said at the gate, stopping nine feet short of the chain. It was the first time he has praised anything about her body. “Your leg holds.”

“It stops at a mile and a half,” she said. “I stand on the bar grip nine seconds. It comes back.”

He nodded as if filing it where he files everything, exact, without judgement. He never lies, never threatens, never touches her, never enters her rooms, never pays for force, never asks her a question. He buys nothing. He has never bought anything.

“Thank you,” she said.

She felt cold despite the sun. She had bought her question cheap and it had cost him something. She had wanted a no and she had got a no and the no had a second half that frightened her more than any yes.

“Thank you,” she said.

He nodded once and went on down the road past the turn with the notebook shut under his arm. She watched him go. He walked the way he always walks, unhurried, nine feet off everything, belonging nowhere on that ground and knowing it.

She stood in the road a long time after he was gone. An institution that stops reading a person has done nothing to them. That was his answer and he does not lie. And a person unread four days is harder to find than a person read four years. That was the other thing and he would rather not have said it.

She thought about the tied sheet on the counter and the forty hands a month and the young reader with his brush across his sheet. She thought about what it would mean to be unread. Three entries in two years and every one of them on paper she could see and touch, and now a fifth day of nothing at all, with a man who watches for a living telling her what nothing looks like from the other end of it.

At the gate that night she lay with her hands behind her head and said nothing for an hour, which was unlike her.

“You are quiet,” he said in the present tense. “I am here.”

“I asked him,” she said. “He said no. And then he said the other thing.”

“I heard you,” he said. “I am not going to make it smaller than it is.”

She turned that over until sleep took it. Nine feet off. Notebook shut. No, and then the other thing. The second and last of his two pages here, spent, and she had asked both times and he had bought nothing either time.
