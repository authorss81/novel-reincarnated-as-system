# What A Quiet Ground Looks Like

It did not happen on one day.

That is the part she was not ready for and the part she has had the most use out of since. She had braced for a morning, the way a floor braces for a bell, and what she got was four days of nothing with a shape to it.

## The Seventh

The bench at the shed at the end of the lane was empty on the seventh at the sixth hour.

There was a stone on it. The stone was one of the four off the low bank at the west end of the cut, worn flat on one side from being carried, and she put it back on the bank where it had been the first time she ever saw it, because a stone that is put down for nothing does not stay where a person put it.

Then she carried bundles down the frame and up, because nine men were on the cut and there were six carts a day and that had not changed.

## The Eighth

The woman of fifty came down the lane at the seventh hour and stood in the doorway of the shed with her hand on the frame, and she said it in the ordinary voice before she came in.

“I am not going to bring you sheets.”

“All right.”

“I told you on the twentieth of a month that if there was ever a day when there was nothing on the bench, that would be a day I had nothing for you to do, and that I would tell you so on the day and not before.” She did not come in. “So. This is the day.”

“Why.”

“Because there is nothing coming down this lane for me to check.”

## The Order Of It

She stood at the end of the cut with a bar over her left shoulder and worked it out from the ground up, because that is the only direction she has ever been able to work anything out in, and it took about nine minutes.

A sheet arrives at the end of a lane when a cart arrives at the end of a lane. A cart arrives when there is something on it. And what is on the carts that come down that lane is what the building has read.

The building stopped reading her on the third of a month at the fourth hour, in a room, with a man of sixty-one writing one line and the date against it.

That was three days ago and she had not put it next to anything, because a refusal is a thing about her and this is a thing about carts, and she is eighteen and she has been wrong about that kind of thing before.

It took nine minutes and it came out the way the water comes off a cut.

She bought her own anonymity with her own usefulness.

That is all of it. She said it out loud on the bank at the end of the lane on the eighth of a month, and it is the only sentence she has said out loud in eight years that she would put on a wall if somebody ever built one.

And it is not a defeat and it is not a triumph. It is the most ordinary transaction in the world.

A woman of fifty has a floor with nine men on it and four carts before the middle of the day, and for two months that floor has had a person at its table who can add two columns and take one off. A floor that has that does not go and find a second person to do it. She goes and gets used to doing without.

She has done that once in eight years. She was eleven years old and she was standing in a yard behind a furnace and a man said her name was not on the list of anybody, and she spent a whole afternoon on a low wall not being angry about it, and she has had nine days of practice at not being angry about things and she got very good at it.

## The Ninth, The Tenth, The Eleventh

The man behind the counter did not put four coppers on the wood.

She went up on the ninth and stood in front of the rate board, which says two coppers a page and has said so for eleven years, and the man behind the counter had a hand flat on the wood and looked at her for four seconds and said, “Was there something.”

“There is not.”

“All right,” he said, and went back to it.

That is everything that happened at a counter that once put four coppers down without hesitating and swept them back with one practised motion when a hand with four fingers on it stopped in the air over them. Nothing had been taken away. There was nothing there. He went back to asking her about nothing.

On the tenth the floor did not ask her about carts.

On the eleventh there were thirty-one lines of chalk on the painted board at the crossing, up from six at the end of a month four months ago, and the chair under the low roof of the hut had a cloth over it and there was a light in the hut in the middle of the afternoon, and a woman of about forty-five in a grey shawl came out to the gate and shut it behind her and went back in.

The ground had not stopped. That is the thing. Six carts a day, nine men on a frame, a hundred people at tables, a hundred and forty feet of lime wash, a mill four miles west whose wheel has not turned since before she came.

The ground had gone back to being a ground.

## The Folder And The First Of A Month

The man of sixty-one found her at the counter on the tenth and did not make her come to him.

“You have a line in a book behind that counter,” he said, “and it is not a name. I am going to say the rest of it once and then I am going to go back to my shelf.”

“Then say it once and I will not ask you about it again.”

“The folder goes east on the first. It has gone on the first every month since I have had the book. It goes with what is on it and it has gone with your column on it five times, and the reason it has gone with your column is that a room read it and put it in a book and the room thought it was worth carrying.”

“And on the first?”

“On the first it goes with nothing on it,” he said, “and it will go with nothing on it, and a man at a cart will tie a string on a folder with an empty sheet in it and he will not ask why, because a man at a cart is not paid to ask why.”

“Will you put that in the book.”

“No,” the man of sixty-one said. “There is nowhere in that book to put it and I have looked, and I have been looking since the fifth of a month, which is longer than I have ever looked for anywhere in nine years at that shelf.”

He put his palm flat on the shelf for a second, the way he puts it flat on the boards when a table is not what he wants it to be, and went back down the room.

She stood at the counter and worked out that the strip had gone east on the first with nothing on it, once, before the refusal, and she had read the column down the lane from a gate two miles west and she had believed it was the room asking for her.

It was the room closing the file.

## The Young Reader At The Far End

The young reader of twenty-six was at his desk at the far end of the counter on the eleventh with forty hands a month in front of him and the person each one was about not in the room.

He did not stop her. He put his brush down flat when she came past, which he does, and he said the only thing he said all afternoon.

“Two,” he said.

She stopped.

“Two sheets this month have gone up to you at the end of a lane and gone into a floor’s book off a person who can add two columns and take one off,” he said. “That is what has stopped. It is not the reading and it is not the fine hands. It is a woman of fifty putting a number on a bench for six days and then not doing it on the seventh, because somebody in a room wrote a line in a book.”

“That is worse.”

“That is much worse and you are the only person who will say so, and I have been saying it to a room for two days and the room does not want to hear it because the room is not losing anything except a bench.” He picked his brush up. “Do not tell the man of sixty-one. He has looked twice.”

## What She Wanted On The Eleventh

She sat out under the trackside hedge at the crossing in the afternoon because there was nothing else, and she found out what she wanted, and she has not put it anywhere.

She wanted somebody to want something from her.

Not the Order. Not a sheet, not a hand, not a fine reading, not a paper with her name on the foot of it, not four coppers she cannot touch. A woman of fifty wanting her to check two columns. A boy wanting the chain lifted. A man at a gate wanting a board read out because there is a line on it she cannot see.

She wanted to be the sort of person a ground reaches for.

That is the want and she caught herself with it and did not put it down for about half an hour, and the half hour is the part of these five days she has not told anybody and never will.

It is the entire shape of eight years.

She answered because answering was the only thing she had instead of strength. She stood on a bank and read a floor sheet out loud and a man of forty wrote a heading over a second column nineteen years late because of it. She stood at a bench for four days and nobody wrote her name down. She stood in a room with a lamp in it and said yes to a thing she had said no to before, and a man read it out on a road to a stranger.

Every single one of those was a hand reaching for her. And every single one of them put her name into a book.

And she sat under a hedge on the eleventh of a month and worked out that she could have the first one without the second one, and that no arrangement on nine days of road has ever offered it to her, and that she does not know whether the reason is that nobody has thought of it or that it cannot be done.

She is not going to say that out loud again. She said it once, under a hedge, in the rain, and the rain took it.
