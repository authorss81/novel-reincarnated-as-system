# The Man With The Chain

She made the seventh mile at the third hour on the second of a month and the ground under it changed before anything else did, in the way that ground does when it has been dressed by somebody for four hundred years instead of thirty.

The made road is four miles from that shed to a mill whose wheel has not turned since before she came, and then it keeps going, and at the seventh mile there is kerb on it.

She had never seen kerb on a road on nine days of road. It is a line of dressed stone laid flat along the edge of a made thing, and it is there so that a wheel does not go into the dirt, and it costs a great deal to put in and it means somebody has been maintaining that road for longer than the mill has been standing still.

That was all the announcement there was. Nothing came up out of the ground and nothing said anything and no man came out of a doorway to meet her.

## Two Days To Eleven Miles

Eleven miles is a long walk on a body that has one arm open at her side half the time and four fingers and a thumb on the hand that is holding a bar she cannot hold at the heavy end.

She did it in two days and did it badly.

On the first she went from the sixth hour to the fourth hour of the next day with two hours off in the middle of the light, because there is no water between that shed and the seventh mile that she did not put a copper into, and she had twenty-one coppers and a bar and bread for two days.

On the second she came on to the turnstile at about the fourth hour with her right arm hanging the way it hangs and her left hand on the strap of the bar and the bar across the back of her shoulders.

## The Gate At The End Of It

It is a gate with two posts and a bar across the top of it and a chain from one post to the other, and the chain was across.

There is a man standing inside it. He is somewhere about fifty and he has been standing inside it for a long time, and there is nothing about him that is in the way of anything. He had a cup in his hand and a stool and a book.

He looked at her for four seconds.

“Turn round.”

She did not turn round.

“Not for anything you did,” he said. “I have not asked you anything and I am not going to. The chain is across and I am the one who stands inside it, and that is the whole of what is happening here.”

“Then why is it across.”

“I have got a rule and it is not a long one,” he said, “and I am not going to recite it to you in the road, and you would not be able to tell from it anyway whether it was a rule or a mood.”

He said the word.

It was one syllable and it was not unkind and it was not a shout, and it was the word a man says four hundred and forty times in nine years, and she had heard it from a carter at a cart stand two days before and had not known until that moment what it was for.

## The Man Inside The Chain

He was about fifty and he was not in a hurry and there was nothing about him that was in a hurry.

He had the chain in a loop on his arm and a cup in his other hand and a stool nine feet inside the post, and he was drinking out of the cup at a carter standing in a road at the fourth hour of the afternoon, and he had been doing that long enough that the cup had gone cold and he had not noticed.

That was the first thing she learned about him: that he was a man at a gate on a slow afternoon and not a thing set up across a road.

“You have come eleven miles,” he said.

“I have.”

“On foot.”

“Yes.”

He looked at the bar across her shoulders and at the hand on it, the way a man looks at a thing he is working out where it goes in an order.

“You have got a bad arm and you have got four fingers on that one, and the mill wheel on this ground has not turned in longer than I have been standing at this gate, which is nine years and a bit.”

“Yes.”

“I am going to say a word now and you are going to go back four hundred yards and stand in the road, and I want you to understand before I say it that I have said it to a cart at this gate about nine hundred times and it has never once been the last word between the two of us.”

“All right.”

“And if you want the whole of it afterwards, come back at the sixth hour in the morning and I will tell you all of it.”

## Four Hundred Yards

She went back four hundred yards and stood in the road.

Four hundred yards back along a road with kerb on it, in the flat light, with the sun coming down off the left shoulder and nothing on it at all, and a leg that had gone at about the eighth mile and had not come back, and a right arm that was open all the way down to the fingers and was not going to close for about another mile.

She stood there until the light went and then she went to ground with a bar over her shoulders because there is nothing at that end of a made road to go into.

She did not sleep much and that was the night.

## The Sixth Hour On The Fourth

She came back at the sixth hour in the morning on the fourth and he had the chain off the post and the bar up on the two posts and the cup in his hand and the book.

He did not ask her a question.

He did not write anything down.

There is no gate book there. The thing she had taken for a book is a list of weights and dates that people pay a copper to have read off out loud, and there was a line on the page for the fourth against a woman of sixty-one eleven days back.

And there was no line on it for a woman with a bar and four fingers on it, because no copper had been paid for one and nobody had asked her for one.

He did not say one word about the first day to anybody.

That was the whole of it and she stood on the wrong side of the chain for four seconds and then she was on the other side of it, and nothing in the world announced it, and no book shut and nothing failed.

She was on the far side of a maintained line for the first time in her life and there was no feeling attached to it that she could name afterward.

## What She Carried

There are nine things in the whole of it that she owns.

Thirty-five coppers in the fold in the inside of the door frame, less a copper at a gate on the second, and less twenty-three between the second of a month and that afternoon: bread at five miles west, water at seven, and a night’s floor at the far end of a made road, where a cart pays to stand under a wall. Which is eleven at the end.

A bar she cannot hold at the heavy end.

A strip folded against her ribs with a word at the head of it in a hand she cannot read, and has refused, forever, on purpose, to have read.

A piece of paper against the other side of her ribs that says Foundation the first stage on the front of it and has said it since a furnace took her father, and that she has still never opened.

Four fingers and a thumb on the right hand. A right arm open half the time. Eight of eleven holds. A turn gone. Short breath at four counts.

A board of clay and a box.

And a shed at a mill whose wheel has not turned since before she came.

That is the whole of what a woman of eighteen carried eleven miles east over two days in a month, and she has carried the same list every day for seven months, and she checked it standing at the seventh mile on the second in about eleven seconds the way she checks everything, and there was nothing on it she had not counted a hundred times.

## Three More Miles

The leg went properly at about a mile and a half.

It was not pain. That is the part nobody on nine days of road would understand, and she has never explained it. The leg simply stopped taking an order, the way a hand stops taking an order, and she went on for four hundred yards with the foot coming down in a place she had not chosen.

She got it back at the second hour by putting the bar into the ground on the wrong side of her and standing on the grip of it with her whole weight for nine seconds, which is a thing she learned at a gate in a different year and has never been able to teach anybody.

Then she went on, and at the second hour of the afternoon she could do four miles without touching it.

## The Building

It is a long low lime-washed building and it is one storey and it is a hundred and forty feet front and there is not one hall in it and nothing in it that is called a hall.

There is a lamp on every window.

She stood at two hundred yards off it with a bar over her shoulder that she could not hold at the heavy end, a right arm that had been open for six days, and a leg that had stopped four hours ago and had come back. She looked at it.

It was warm.

That was the whole of what she found out about it and she has thought about it more since than she expected to.

It is not a season and it is not a weather and it is not anything anybody can do about. The windows were lit and it was warm, and it was about the third hour of a day in a month with nothing in the air.

And it was warm because there were about a hundred people inside it reading by lamps, and a hundred people make a building warm the way a bar makes a furnace warm.

She had eleven coppers. She had put twenty-four of the thirty-five into water, bread and a night’s floor between the second of a month and that afternoon and she had not been hungry since the first Foundation and had not been since, and she could stand at two hundred yards off a warm building for as long as it took with nothing in her stomach and it would not touch her.

She stood at two hundred yards off a long low lime-washed building with a lamp in every window and about a hundred people in it reading, and she did not go in.

Not that day. That was not courage and it was not a decision she had made in advance.

She had a question in the road and she had said out loud in a doorway on the eighteenth of a month that she would be standing at the east end of this road on the twenty-ninth, and it was the fifth and she had four days.

And there was a woman of sixty-one who had told her that she would be at a gate at the sixth hour and did not want an answer.

And none of the hundred people inside that building had looked up.
