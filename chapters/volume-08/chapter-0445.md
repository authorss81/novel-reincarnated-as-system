# A Fine Hand In A Room

It happened on the ninth of the month in a room with a lamp in it.

Four people stood round a sheet that was not hers. The young reader of twenty-six stood with his brush down. The woman with the thumb gone at the first joint stood with her hands behind her back. The man with the brush stood with the string untied in his hand.

And a woman of twenty-two stood with her arms folded, pale, eight months gone in a way the coat could not hide, with a bad night in nine months’ time that the room does not know about and will not know until it comes.

The sheet lay on the table under the lamp. It was the tied sheet. The lower half ran close and fine, cut the way only one hand in nine days of road cuts, and four people had failed at it and the work had gone on round it for four days.

Ning Yan stood in the doorway with empty hands. She had carried bundles that morning. She had squared sheets. She had eaten bread outside. She had nine coppers in her fold and bread for one day.

The man with the brush looked up.

“Will you read it,” he said in the ordinary voice, in a room, the way people ask for a thing they need. “We cannot.”

That was all. No ceremony. No form. No sheet read aloud to the person it is about. Four people standing round a sheet that is not hers and cannot read it, asking a woman on this ground whether she will read it.

“Do not,” the young reader said.

He had not moved off the table. His brush lay flat on the wood where he had put it, and he put the flat of his hand on it as if holding it there.

“Put it in the stack with the rest of them. It goes east in the cart like everything else and somebody past the turn reads it in a month and it is done with.”

“You have had it four days,” the man with the brush said.

“I have had it four days. It is not better at five.”

“She read a floor sheet in four seconds. Four hands on it, and she did not look at the paper twice.”

“That was a floor sheet and she was stood in the open.” He looked past her at the counter, at the lime wash, at the two hundred yards of made road beyond it. “If we ask her in front of a room she is a hand in this room. After today she is a hand in this room every time a hand comes in, for the rest of her life, and she has not agreed to that. We would be agreeing it for her.”

“And somebody will write it down in a year and call it her choice.”

The man with the brush did not answer him. The woman with the thumb looked at her own hands on the table edge. It was the true thing and the room had no answer to it, because the young reader was twenty-six and had been in the room two years and had just described exactly what the rate board behind the counter does to a person.

She could have said no. She had said no once in a room with a lamp in it a hundred days ago and the no had gone up the same road every answer goes up and nothing was written and no book would not shut. She could still say no. She can always say no.

“Is it a bad hand,” the woman of twenty-two said.

It was the first thing she had said since she came into the room. Her hand stayed where it had landed, over the child, without knowing it was there.

“Will you tell me,” she said. “You have read it. I can see that you have.”

Ning Yan looked at the sheet lying flat on the table under the lamp. Answering was cheap. Answering cost a name on a roll and a fact in a room, and she had learned that price in a doorway a hundred days back and had gone without it then and would go without it now.

“I cannot tell you that,” she said.

“You could,” the woman of twenty-two said. “You did not want to.”

She was right, and Ning Yan let it stand, because the correction was free and the answer would not have been. That was the shape of the whole day, worked out in nine words by a woman of twenty-two who knew nothing about her except what she had just watched her do.

She went to the table and looked down at the sheet.

She read it.

## The Reading

The upper half ran plain first, and she gave it plain, line by line, the ground’s own wording, figures, dates, a list of work done in order. Her voice stayed level. The lamp burned. The four stood without moving.

Then the lower half, close-written, fine, three lines with a name at the foot. She read it the way Dai reads, sure, without hesitation, each stroke landing. She did not touch the sheet. Her right arm hung open. Her breath ran four counts. The room past the door worked on, paper and breath, unaware that the tied sheet is opening at last.

When she finished she stepped back from the table and put her hands behind her back to stop their shaking. “It is read,” she said. “That is what it says.”

The young reader let out his breath once, sharp. The woman with the thumb closed her eyes. The man with the brush untied his hands from the string and laid the string square on the table edge. The woman of twenty-two looked at Ning Yan with something like fear and something like thanks, both true, and held the look.

## Four Hundred And Seven

The man with the brush did not move. He looked at Xie Changyin, who stood in the doorway with her hands behind her back and her face still.

“Put it on the roll,” Xie Changyin said.

The man of sixty-one came down the room with the book shut under his arm and opened it on the table beside the sheet. He wrote the name at the foot of the fine hand into the book in the ordinary hand, with the date against it. Four hundred and six names. Now four hundred and seven.

His hand did not shake. He has written four hundred and six before this one and he knows every one of them the way she knows the seventh mile. He shut the book, put his palm flat on the boards once, brief, the way a man touches a thing he is frightened of, and took his hand away.

Nothing failed. No book would not shut. The roll took it the way a roll takes everything.

The woman of twenty-two sat down on the stool Ning Yan had left, pale, her arms folded round herself. The woman with the thumb brought her water in a cup and she drank it all. None of them asked her what ails her, and the room went on being kind to her without any of them knowing why kindness is going to matter.

“Four hundred and seven,” the young reader said, quiet. It was the first time Ning Yan had heard the number said aloud in that building. It landed the way numbers land when they are people.

“There will be more of them,” Xie Changyin said.

“I know,” Ning Yan said, and shut her mouth on the rest of it.

## The Discovery

It came to her standing there with empty hands, and it came whole.

The refusal a hundred days ago was against being told one thing. She was right to refuse it. It cost her everything she wanted. And it taught a room full of strangers that there is a woman on this ground who can read one.

Her own silence was the evidence. It was read correctly. A woman who will not have a fine hand read to her in a room is a woman who knows what a fine hand is. That is what brought the Order to her door. That is what put Xie Changyin in a shed at a mill with a folded sheet inside her coat.

She is the only person who can say she did not expect it.

She stood with that sitting in her chest. It was enormous. The best decision she ever made is sitting at the bottom of the worst thing that has happened to her. Nothing is undone. Nothing is softened. The refusal stands, and it was the right one, and being right about it is what did this.

## The Road Out

Ning Yan watched all of it with her hands behind her back. She had read Geng’s sheet aloud on a lifting floor and stopped a man in the middle of it, and that put a second column into a floor sheet. She had read sheets on a third floor for two years for nothing.

She had never read the fine hand before the ninth, because reading it is a confirmation and a confirmation is an entry. Now she has confirmed another person’s hand. The entry is not hers. It is somebody else’s name in the best book on nine days of road, put there by her mouth.

The woman of twenty-two had asked her whether the hand was a bad one and had not asked a second time. That was the part she would keep longest. She could have told her. There is no way to tell a person what a hand says without confirming that you have read it, and confirming is an entry, and she is eighteen years old and learned it in a doorway a hundred days back. So the woman of twenty-two will go on not knowing until she knows some other way.

“I did not mean this,” Ning Yan said out loud, to the room, in the ordinary voice.

“No,” Xie Changyin said. “Nobody means it. That is why it is asked in a room.”

She went out past the counter into the daylight with her hands shaking. Her breath ran four counts. Her arm hung open. She had eleven coppers once and nine now and bread for one day and a bar she cannot hold at the heavy end, and she had just done the one thing she swore never to do, and she had done it for strangers, for nothing, in the ordinary voice.

At the gate that night she lay with her hands behind her head and said nothing for a long time.

“You read it,” he said in the dark. “I am here and I heard you.”

“I did,” she said. “It goes on a roll.”

“I know,” he said. “I am not going to tell you that you are wrong.”

She slept with the lamp behind her eyelids and the four of them standing round it.

There had been no thanks in it and there would not be, because there was nothing to thank her for. They had asked a question in a room and she had answered it, and an answer in a room is not a favour. It is a use.

Past the turn there was a strip going east with her own hand on it, and a woman of sixty-one who had walked eleven miles to ask her for this same thing in a softer voice.

She had wanted to be left alone with her own name for one more month. Instead she has put four hundred and seven names in a book and made herself the hand that puts them there.

Sleep came. It did not help.
