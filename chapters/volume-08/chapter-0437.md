# Forty Hands A Month

She came back on the second day with the bar left at the gate by arrangement and her hands empty.

The man with the brush nodded her past the counter to a desk set sideways against it. Behind the desk sat a young man with ink on his second finger and a lamp pulled close to his left hand. He was twenty-six. He had a sheet in front of him and another squared beneath it and a third stacked face down.

“This is he,” Xie Changyin said. “He reads forty hands a month.”

The young man glanced up and down again. He did not offer his name and nobody gave it.

Ning Yan sat where she was shown, on a stool on the near side of the counter, with the desk between. Her right arm hung. Her breath ran four counts. The room worked round her, paper and breath, a hundred people reading, lamps on every table.

## The Morning Sheets

He worked three sheets while she watched. Each came tied in a bundle, each was untied by the carrier, squared, set before him. He read each down with his lips moving once, marked one line or two, set his brush across the sheet exact and parallel while the carrier took it away. His second finger carried ink to the first joint. He wiped it on a rag at the elbow and went on.

The lamp at his left hand burned steady. At the sixth hour a carrier trimmed every lamp down the room, his own included, with a practised turn that did not interrupt the reading. The flames dipped and stood straight again. The young man thanked the carrier without looking up, and the carrier nodded and went on.

Ning Yan watched his hands. They were young hands, quick, the nails cut short. They did not shake. They had read forty hands a month for months, and the work sat in them the way rails sit in her shoulders. She knows hands that know work. She trusts competence over people, which is her flaw and nearly finished her once, and she felt the pull of it here, clean and strong.

“You square well,” she said to the carrier when he came for the third sheet, because she had squared sheets herself and knew the work.

The carrier smiled once and went on. The young man glanced up at the sound of her voice and down again.

The young man turned his sheet over and read three lines down it with his lips moving once. He marked one line with a dot and went on.

Xie Changyin leaned an elbow on the counter. “Ask him what the work is.”

“What is the work,” Ning Yan said.

He did not look up. “Hands other floors cannot read.”

That was all for a while. A man brought him another sheet and took the dotted one away. He squared the new one and read it the same way.

Later, when the lamp needed trimming, he said without lifting his eyes, “I do it at this desk.”

Ning Yan waited. She had learned on nine days of road to let a man finish a line before asking the next thing.

“From this side of the counter,” he said.

The lamp smoked once and he trimmed it with a practised turn and went back to the sheet. Down the room somebody coughed and somebody else turned pages.

Xie Changyin said nothing. She watched Ning Yan watch him.

At the sixth hour he set his brush down and flexed his hand and looked past Ning Yan at nothing.

“The person is never in the room,” he said.

She wrote that down in her head the way she writes everything. At a desk. From the far side of a counter. The person never in the room. Three short pieces, each said an hour apart, none of them a speech, none of them meant to be one.

She asked, “Are you right.”

He smiled for the first time. It was a small tired smile.

“Every time,” he said.

He picked the brush up again. He read another hand. He dotted another line. His hand moved sure and quick. Forty hands a month, month after month, and nobody had ever asked him to go and look at the face behind one.

Near the eighth hour, while he waited for sheets, he said to the lamp more than to her, “Nobody has ever told me that is the only thing keeping me here.”

She felt that land. She had been the smallest person in every room for two years and she knew what it was to be kept for one thing and not told it. She said nothing because there was nothing useful to say to a man of twenty-six in a room full of lamps.


## The Carrier’s Child

At midday the carriers eat outside in turns. She ate with them, bread dry, chewed slow. They talked of oil and wicks and a child with a cough that will not clear. One carrier, a woman past middle age, said the cough eases when the child sleeps propped up, and another nodded.

Ordinary talk. Ning Yan listened and said little. She has no child and no cure to offer, but she knows coughs that sit in winter rooms. She told them propping helps, and the woman thanked her, and that was the whole of her part in it.

She likes these people. They carry and square and trim, steady, without wasted motion. None of them asked her name. None asked about her arm. Work first, names later, if ever. That suits her. She has been entered three times and read for two years, and a place where she is hands before she is a name feels like rest.


## The Eighth Hour

At the eighth hour the tables thinned. Readers stacked their sheets, squared their piles, wiped their brushes. The carriers tied the marked sheets into bundles again and set them on the shelf at the far end. The man of sixty-one with the book under his arm walked the length of the room once, looking at each table, saying nothing. The lamps burned on in the dusk coming down outside the windows.

The young man cleaned his brush in water, shook it, laid it across his desk exact. He flexed his reading hand twice. Ink stained the water dark. He poured it into a bucket at the end of the desk and set the cup back square.

“You waited all day,” he said to Ning Yan, surprised, as if he had forgotten her and remembered her again.

“I did,” she said.

“Most people do not.”

“I wait at gates,” she said. “I am good at it.”

He nodded, tired, and stacked his last sheets. His shoulders sat low. Forty hands a month sits in a back as well as in hands. She knows backs that carry. She carried a bar eleven miles on one arm at half and four fingers on one hand, and she knows what an ache costs at the eighth hour.

She wanted to tell him he reads well, and did not, because praise from a stranger in his own room would land wrong. She wanted to tell him the room needs him, and did not, because he had said it himself, that being right is the only thing keeping him there, and hearing it twice would not help him carry it.

So she said, “Thank you for letting me watch,” and meant it, and he nodded and picked up his bucket and went.

Xie Changyin walked her out past the counter into the daylight.

“He is good,” Ning Yan said.

“He is,” Xie Changyin said. “He has been right every time and he has never once been told that being right is the only thing keeping him there. Now you have heard him say it himself.”

Ning Yan stood in the road with her empty hands at her sides. She had come to ask one question about one person and had spent the day listening to a reader instead. That was not a waste. She understood now what a reading in that building was: a desk, a counter between, a person absent, a mark made, sheets going on. Clean. Competent. Fatal in a way she could not yet name.

“You will ask tomorrow,” Xie Changyin said.

“I will,” Ning Yan said.

She went back to the gate and fetched her bar and slept with her back to the building and her face to the road she had come down. In the night she woke once and counted her coppers by touch. Eleven. Still eleven.
