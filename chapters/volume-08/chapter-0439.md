# The Room That Reads

She learned the room from the inside over three days.

It works like this. Sheets come in through the near end in bundles tied with string. The man with the brush unties them and squares them and puts them down the tables. Readers take them in order. Each table reads one difficulty. The young do the ordinary hands in the morning. The older do the hard ones in the afternoon. At the far end a man of sixty-one keeps the book in his hand and writes nothing anyone can see from the tables.

There is a lamp on every table. The glass is wiped at the sixth hour. The oil is filled at the same hour. Nobody reads by daylight even where daylight falls across the table. That is the rule. She asked why and the man with the brush said, “So that every hand is read in the same light,” and that was the whole of the reason and it was a good one.

She sat on a stool by the counter with her bar left at the gate and watched. Her breath ran four counts. Her arm hung open half the time and nobody stared at it. A woman at the second table had a thumb gone at the first joint and nobody stared at that either. People who read hands have seen worse than an open arm.

## The Carrying

On the second day they let her carry bundles from the counter to the third table. She did it left-handed with her right hand open round the string. The bundles were light. The paper was cut clean. She liked the weight of it.

She learned the carry run quickly. Counter to third table, empty string back. Counter to fifth table, empty folders back. The boards are swept, the gaps between tables wide enough for a carrier with a load. She kept to the gaps and did not brush a lamp. At the fifth table a reader thanked her without looking up, and she nodded and went on.

At midday the carriers eat outside in turns, bread and water, standing with their backs to the lime wash. She ate with them, her bread from the store five miles west, dry, chewed slow. They talked about oil prices and wicks and a carrier’s child with a cough. Ordinary talk. She listened and said little and liked being part of it for an hour.

“You carry well,” the woman with the thumb said when she set a bundle square on the table edge.

“I have carried worse,” Ning Yan said, and the woman laughed once and went back to her sheet.

In the afternoon she squared sheets beside the man with the brush. He showed her once how he likes the edges, exact, the lower edge flush with the desk mark. She did it left-handed, slow at first, then sure. Her right hand rested open on her knee. The paper slid clean. The work steadied her the way rails steady her. Hands doing plain work in good light.

“You square well,” the man with the brush said.

“I have squared worse,” she said.

She ate bread at midday outside with her back to the lime wash. She had bread for two days and then bread for two more bought with a copper at the cart stand, which left her ten. She counted it at night by touch. Ten coppers. The price of a page in that building is two, and she did the arithmetic without wanting to do it and stopped.

## The First Thing That Goes Wrong

On the third day a sheet came in with two hands on it.

It went first to the young reader of twenty-six. He read it down, marked one line, stopped, read it again. He turned it over. He held it to the lamp. He set his brush down.

He carried it himself to the next table. The woman with the thumb read it and shook her head. It went to a third table. It went to a fourth. Four people stood round it at the end of the room with their lamps pulled close, and none of them could say which hand had written the lower half.

Ning Yan stood by the counter and watched. Her heart beat hard. She could see the lower half from nine feet off. She knew that hand. It was a fine hand, close-written, the strokes cut the way Dai’s strokes are cut on nine days of road. She said nothing. Her mouth stayed shut. She had refused that hand at ch 417 and had given up everything to do it and she was not going to pick it up in a room full of strangers.

The man with the brush tied the sheet up again with its string and set it aside on the counter.

“It will wait,” he said to the room. “The work goes on.”

And it did. The tables took the next sheets. The lamps burned. Nobody raised a voice. Nobody blamed the hand or the sheet or the man who had sent it. That was what competence looked like from the inside: a thing that cannot be read is set aside and the work goes on round it.

## The Hour After

She carried the next bundle with her hands steady and her face still. The third table took it without looking up. The fifth table took the one after. The work went on round the tied bundle on the counter the way water goes on round a stone.

But she kept seeing the lower half from nine feet off, close-written, the strokes cut the way Dai’s strokes are cut. She knows that cut. She has refused it forever on purpose. Seeing it here, waiting on a counter in a competent room, shook her more than she let show. She set a bundle down a finger’s width off square and had to square it again, and the young man noticed and thanked her all the same.

At the eighth hour she went out with the carriers and ate bread standing with her back to the lime wash. Her bread is gone by morning. She has nine coppers in her fold. She worked the figures without wanting to work them: bread at the store, water at seven miles, a night’s floor at the far end of a made road.

The arithmetic comes out the same way every time. She can stay eleven days more if she eats once a day. After that she walks west whether the work is done or not.

The woman with the thumb ate beside her and said nothing for a while. Then she said, “You saw it.”

Ning Yan did not pretend. “I did.”

“Can you read it.”

Ning Yan looked at the lime wash and the road and the gate with the chain on it. She can still say no. She just cannot make the no take, once a thing she is in has started. She has not started this. She is carrying bundles. She is squaring sheets. She has not put her hand on that sheet.

“Not today,” she said.

The woman nodded once and went back in. No pressure. No second asking. That is the room. It asks once, in the ordinary voice, and waits. She stood with that kindness sitting in her chest beside the shame, both true, neither cancelling the other.

But something had changed. The young reader sat with his brush across his sheet for an hour and did not pick it up. The woman with the thumb kept glancing at the tied bundle. At the eighth hour the young man came to the counter and stood there.

“I have never left one,” he said to the man with the brush. “Forty hands a month and I have never left one on the table.”

“You have now,” the man said, kindly. “It happens. Eat.”

Ning Yan walked out into the evening with that sitting in her chest. She had watched four people fail at a thing she could do, and she had stood still and let them fail, and she was glad and ashamed in equal parts and both feelings were true.

At the gate she said it out loud once to nobody, the way she says everything that has to be carried.

“I did not read it.”

“You did not,” he said in the dark. “I am with you and you did right to wait.”

“I know,” she said. “But they left it on the counter. And I saw it from nine feet off.”

She lay awake a long time. The building burned behind her eyelids. Four people round a sheet. A tied bundle waiting. The first thing gone wrong, handled well, and still wrong.

She had eleven days before the turn of the month and ten coppers and bread for one day, and a tied sheet on a counter with her name on it though nobody knew it.
