# What A Floor Writes

The first of the month came and the woman of fifty came down the bank and stood beside her and did not say good morning.

She had been on that floor three days by then and had heard her voice exactly twice, both times to a man, both times short.

“You are the one who squared the sheet,” the woman said.

“Yes.”

“Come and stand inside the door,” the woman said. “Not on the boards. The boards are counted.”

## The Room At The Back

The room at the back of the far bank is nine feet by eleven with a board floor and a shelf and one table, and it is the warmest place she has stood on that ground.

There is no lamp. There is a window high up and a shutter open. There is a stove at the end with a fire gone down to a grey bed and a flat stone on top of it, and the smell of that stone has followed her for the rest of the day.

There are six books on the shelf and they are all the same shape. She counted them without meaning to count them, the way she counts lamps, and they are day books, and they run back far enough that the shelf has been short once and been made longer.

The woman of fifty put the day sheet down on the table and squared it herself, and then she took her hand off it, which was an invitation.

“I did not think of the two columns,” she said.

That was all she said for a while. She stood at the table the way a man stands at a counter when he is about to say a thing he has said before.

“A floor writes the day it finds out,” she said. “That is how a floor works.”

“You have both dates on that line,” Ning Yan said.

“I rule both,” the woman said. “The right one is when it came back to me. The left one is the day it happened.”

“Which one have you written.”

“Eleven lines out of four years,” the woman said, “and not one of them by me.”

She turned the sheet round and put her finger on the ruled column on the left with its empty cells under it going all the way down.

“This one is what I have,” she said. “A man comes off a frame and he comes down a lane and he tells me and I write the day he told me. The day he came off is his business and I do not know it. I have never once asked him for it, and neither would you.”

“You ruled the other column anyway.”

“I ruled it nineteen years ago and I have never once known what to write in it,” she said. “You do not take out a rule because you have not found the thing it is for.”

## Eleven Days

Zheng came off the sill on the sixth of a month and this was the eleventh.

He came out of the shed at the fourth hour with his leg wrapped again and climbed onto the frame and worked, and the woman of fifty stood at the low door and watched him go the length of it and did not go out to him.

She did not say anything about him until he sat down.

Then she said, “Eleven days.”

“Yes,” Ning Yan said.

“He will be paid for eleven days,” the woman said. “He has never once asked me whether he will be, and if he asked I would have to go and find out, and I have not found out in eleven days because it is not the kind of thing I put on a sheet.”

That was four sentences and she said them with her arms crossed in front of her and she was not looking at Ning Yan while she said them.

“A floor like mine has had a man off his feet for eleven days before this month and a man off his feet for nineteen before that,” she said. “It is not the sill. It is the frame. Everybody on that frame is standing on the same four feet of oak and they come down off it the wrong way.”

“Do you put that on a sheet.”

“No,” the woman said. “I put the days on it.”

## The Line About A Heading

Ning Yan sat on the bench against the wall with the sheet in front of her and read the figures again, both columns, and did them twice.

“You said you did not think of the two columns,” she said.

“I did not.”

“Who did.”

The woman of fifty turned round and put her thumb on the stone on the stove for a moment, the way Xie Changyin turns a lamp base, and then took it away.

“There is a sheet west of here,” she said, “with a heading written over the second column in a hand that is not mine.”

Ning Yan did not move.

“Nineteen years old it is by the look of the pencil and nobody on that ground has ever been told why it is there,” the woman said. “And it is not in my hand and it is not in anybody’s hand out of this building. I heard about it from a carter and I have thought about it since, and that is all I have done about it.”

She picked up the day sheet and went to the door.

“You may have the sheet at the sixth hour and bring it back by the ninth,” she said. “Do not write on it. It goes into the shelf with the rest and it closes at the ninth.”

She went out. She did not say thank you and she did not say anything else.

## The Sixth Hour And The Ninth

Ning Yan sat on the bench in a nine-foot room with a grey stove and read the day's work twice.

She had been given a thing to do. She noticed that first, before anything on the sheet, and she noticed it the way she notices a stone going out of true: by the fact that she was not asked to leave and not counted.

Eleven days ago she was reading sheets out loud on a third floor four times a month for nothing, with a margin correction of four that had been four since before she came. A month ago she read a floor sheet aloud in a room about a hundred feet long and stopped a man in the middle of it, and eleven men wrote a heading over the second column of a sheet because she had done it.

And now a woman of fifty she has known two days has put a day book on a table and walked out of the room.

She did not write on it. She read it, line against line, both columns, every figure, twice. She found one thing and it was not a thing.

At the ninth hour she carried the sheet back and put it on the table square and said, “Nothing is wrong with it.”

“Is there something wrong with it,” the woman said.

“No,” Ning Yan said. “Six in, eight out, two of them by the weight, one line with no other half, and the right column is short two figures and both of them are days where the cart came after the shut. That is ordinary. That is a floor having an ordinary day.”

The woman of fifty took the sheet and looked at it and looked at her.

“You read columns,” she said.

“Yes.”

“Then read the back ones,” she said. “Nobody reads the back ones.”

## The Back Ones

The back ones are four years of them, stacked, and they are in the same hand and the same two columns, and she read six of them across the second afternoon while the stove went out and came back and the light in the window went from white to grey.

Four years of a floor, both columns, line against line.

And on every line in four years the left-hand column is empty. Not once. Not in nineteen hundred working days.

Except for eleven.

Eleven lines in four years with a date written in the column that nobody on that ground fills, and on ten of the eleven the date is not the day the work came back to her, and it is never later. It is always earlier. Once by a day. Three times by two. Five times by three, by four and by six.

It came apart eleven times in four years, and somebody wrote down both halves of it, and it was not the floor.

A floor of fifty people, nine men on a frame, six carts a day, four years, and eleven times the day it happened was not the day anybody found out.

She put the book down on the table with both hands on it and did not say anything for a long time.

It was not hers. She understood that in the room, on the bench, with the stove ticking. It was not a thing she had put into the world. It was what a floor does when the day it happened and the day anybody found out come apart, and it came apart eleven times in four years, and eleven times somebody wrote down both halves of it without knowing they were doing it, and eleven times nobody wrote the heading.

## What She Took Back

She carried the back books to the shelf on the fourth day and put them in the exact gaps she had taken them from, and she stood a moment with her hand flat on the shelf.

She has done this twice in her life with two other books and it cost her both times.

The first was a lifting floor sheet and it is in a room about a hundred feet long with nine men in it, and there is a heading over the second column that a man of about forty wrote because a man of fifty-one said one word.

The second was a book on nine days of road with forty books open on it, and a woman of forty-five read her column back to her and it contained the only refusal in the set.

And this was a third, and there was no heading, and the eleven lines had been there four years and nobody had seen them.

She had come east of the turn for the first time in her life on the twenty-sixth of a month and put her hand on a shelf that nobody in the world had asked her to touch.

She was not asked to stay. She stayed anyway, because the sixth hour came and the woman put the sheet on the table and walked out, and it had happened every morning since the twenty-seventh of a month and there was no reason on earth to think it was going to stop.

At dusk she walked out to the crossing at the end of the lane and stood four feet off the gate and looked at the board she could not read, and there were three lines of chalk on it.

There had been one on the first evening she came down this lane.

She could not read a word of any of them. She stood there and counted them, because counting is what she has, and two lines had come on in five days on a board that nobody had told her anything about.
