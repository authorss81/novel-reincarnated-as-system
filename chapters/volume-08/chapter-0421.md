# The Eighteenth, And Nothing Else

The twenty-eighth of a month beginning 1,064 was the sixth hour and the rail at the works had forty-nine lines on it and a hut at the near end with the door open, and the man with the chalk was inside it, and nothing else was happening anywhere she could see.

She read all forty-nine down the way she reads everything, which is fast and by the shape before the words, and then she read them again at the same speed and got the same thing twice, and she could take none of them.

Every line on that board is measured by the weight of what comes out of the ground or by the length of it. Two hundred and six yards of dressed stone by the load. Nine hundred feet of drain by the length, second run, low side. Forty feet of bed by the length, with a margin note against the fourteenth in a yard-book hand. Two hundred yards of hedge by the length, east of the turn, second listing of the same ground.

She had stood at that rail seven months and there was no morning she went to it that anything came of.

## Eleven Minutes

She was still standing at it when the light came off the top of the spoil heap and reached the near end of the board, and she worked out that it had been eleven minutes, and that in eleven minutes at that rail nothing had happened and nothing had been asked of her.

That was the first time she had noticed it happening.

A month ago a boy of sixteen had come up nine steps with a roll under his arm and asked her whether there had been a cart on the mill road on the eighteenth.

A man of thirty had asked her whether she had seen a dressed stone go into a cart and which way it had gone.

A woman who keeps a stove in an office end at a yard at the turn had come up nine steps she had no business on and asked her what day the season turned.

Four people, inside a month, on four different days, and not one of them had asked her anything in the seven months before that, and a man behind a counter had said in a room with a stove in it that he was going to do it four times a month and that there would be nothing written at the end of any of it.

She had worked out the calendar of it since, standing at gates and at the low side of a yard, and it is the fourth, the eleventh, the eighteenth and the twenty-fourth.

It was the twenty-eight.

## The Yard At The Turn

She was at the low side of that yard at the seventh hour of the twenty-eighth with nothing in her hands because there was nothing to do there and she goes three days in seven and does not stop going.

The office end had a pot in it and a woman in it who has never once asked her name, and the woman came out at about the eighth hour with a cup of something hot in it and put it into her hands and went back inside without stopping.

The cup was hot for four minutes.

She stood at the low side with the empty thing in it and she was eighteen years old and she could not be given anything, and a thing that a person puts into her hand goes into a column, and there are four people on a floor inside nine days of road who would ask her about it four times a month for the rest of her life.

She put it down on the edge of the bench and she watched it go cold.

Nine men were on the north end and a second course was going on over a sill that was holding, and a man checked it every morning and had never mentioned it to anybody, and not one of the nine came and asked her where she had been.

## What The Twenty-Eighth Was

She dug the run at Pei’s gate from the ninth hour to the eleventh, and he came out at the eleventh and stood at the end of it the way he stands, and she got a copper, and there were twenty-two in the fold in the inside of the door frame.

Then she walked the four miles back east to a shed at a mill whose wheel has not turned since before she came, and there was nothing at the mill but the wheel and the low sound a millrace makes when there is no water in it.

She had a hundred and six days of furnace in her and a ring of clay on the boards that comes up the width of a finger on the nights when there is something in the shed to put a hand on.

There had been nothing in that shed to put a hand on since the twelfth of a month.

## The Second Hour

She put a lump out of the box in at the low end of the ring and it came up a finger’s width and stopped, which is what it has done on about four hundred nights since before she came.

Then she lay on the boards with her hands behind her head and said the six words out loud once, into a shed with no fire in it.

“No. I will not hear it.”

She said them a second time because she wanted to hear whether they sounded any different the second time.

They did not.

She lay there for a while with the strip against her ribs and the boards under her back.

There is a word at the head of that strip in a hand that comes about nine times a year, and there is a man two miles up a made road with a lamp in his room whose whole trade is that hand, and she had wanted to have it read for every day since the seventeenth of a month six weeks back.

She had given it up on the nineteenth of a month at the seventh hour, in a room with the door shut, with no force and nothing owed in it and a condition that was the ordinary condition, and the six words had gone up the same road every answer has gone up since before the wheel on that mill was cast.

That was thirty-nine days ago. Nothing came of it and nothing was going to.

She wanted it. That was the whole of what she had on the boards at the second hour, and she was not going to argue with herself about a thing she had already argued with herself about in a room. What she had decided in that room was that she would rather have the standing arrangement — four people a month, with nothing written at the end of any of it — than have the line.

She still had that. She had it exactly as she had left it, and she had not traded one copper for it and she was not going to.

## The Calendar

She got it out on the boards the way she gets every month out, because a month has to be written out before anything else can be counted in it, and the boards took it in the ordinary hand of a woman who has been writing months on them since she was eleven.

The twenty-eighth was a day of nothing, and there was no term for that in any of it.

Four days in a month somebody was going to ask her a question and put the answer somewhere, and three hundred and sixty other days in the year nobody on nine days of road was going to ask her anything at all, and she had spent the whole of the seventh month of the first year of her being on this ground learning that half of it, and had not noticed that she had been counting on the other half until a morning when the half had not come.

She lay there and looked at it.

It was not a hardship. That was the part she could not get round and gave up trying to get round about two hours later.

She had been at a rail at the sixth hour every morning since she came past the last working point, and a board full, and nothing on it that wanted her. And a shed at the second hour, and boards that came up a finger’s width.

And four days a month, which had been the only thing in seven months that had ever come to her without being asked, and four days is not a week.

## The Seventh Hour Of The Twenty-Eighth

She was standing in the open doorway of that shed at the seventh hour of the evening with the bar over her right shoulder because it stands on end by the door the way she leaves it, and the mill road runs four hundred yards from the door to a gate and then two miles west to a counting house.

A cart came down the mill road.

She watched it come the four hundred yards and then the two miles and it did not slow at the gate, and it did not slow at the counting house, and it went on west at the speed a loaded cart goes past a woman standing in a road with a bar over her shoulder.

There was a tray on it with a cord round it. The man on it did not get out at the gate.

She stood in the doorway and watched it until it was a quarter of a mile off, and then it was under the rise, and then it was gone.

She had seen that kind of cart three times in seven months. Tray on the bed, cord round the tray, and it goes up the mill road at the sixth hour on the first with a name in it, and it comes back down on the ninth with the empty trays going up, and a man with a brush on the floor below the counting house copies whatever is on it into a book that shuts.

She had worked the rest of it out herself three months back, standing at a cart track with a bar in her hand, by asking two men about carts and getting the same answer twice.

A tray goes east and it does not come back on that cart, because that cart does not go east.

That cart had gone west, at the seventh hour, with a tray on it and a cord round the tray, and it had not stopped anywhere on four miles of road to do it with.

## Fifty-One Lines

She went down to the rail at the sixth hour on the twenty-ninth and there were fifty-one lines on it.

It had eight on it for a year and a bit, and a man of forty-one with a coat on over one arm told her standing at the hut door that he had walked the length of it in six seconds without stopping in all that time, and that he wanted her to know he knew that was the worst thing that can happen to a list.

Fifty-one. Two hundred and six yards of dressed stone by the load. Nine hundred feet of drain by the length, second run, low side. Forty feet of bed by the length with a margin note against the fourteenth. Two hundred yards of hedge by the length east of the turn.

And at the bottom, in a different ink, a cart track nine hundred feet by the load that a man with a rule in his hand had put in on the second of a month and had not been able to check off, because the ground went on him before the cart came off it.

She read all fifty-one down and got them the same twice.

Every one of them is measured by the weight of what comes out of the ground or by the length of it, and there has never been a line on that rail measured by the voice, and a man reads it out and the rail goes quiet and one of them puts his name at the end of it.

She could take none of them and she has not been able to take one since the board went full.
