# A Description, And It Is Accurate

She came down the lane at the sixth hour on the seventeenth, on foot, two miles from a building she has worked in for forty-two years, and she was sixty-one and she was carrying nothing.

She stood in the doorway of the shed at the end of the lane with her hands behind her back and looked at the boards, and at the bar against the wall, and at the four-inch stone on a shelf, and she did not come in for about nine seconds.

“You are working it out,” she said.

“I am writing it down.”

“I have watched two hundred and forty people write something down in that building and I have watched one of them write it down on a shed board and I would like to know which of the two things you are doing.”

“Both,” Ning Yan said, and moved off the bench, and Xie Changyin came in and stood at the end of it with her hands still behind her back, and there were no four sheets on it because there had not been any on it for ten days.

## The Sixth Mouth

She let it sit for a minute and then she put it down in the middle of the shed the way she puts everything down, in pieces, four or five sentences, and not once as a speech.

“I have watched you for seventy-five days before you came in here, and I have watched you for three hundred more before that, and I am going to describe what you do, and then I am going to stop talking, and I would like you to let me finish before you say one word.”

“All right.”

“You take a sheet that four people cannot take and you make it into numbers. Then you say the numbers out loud to whoever is standing in the room. You do it for nothing. You have been doing it for two years and you have been paid for it once, in four coppers, and your hand stopped.”

“That is right.”

“That is the trade.”

She put her hands flat on the table behind her.

“That is all of the trade. There is no building in that and no book in that and no rate in that and no paper in that. The building, the book, the four coppers and the room nine feet by eleven, none of it is the work.”

“The work is a person reading a column out loud in a room and being believed, and I have been trying to hire you for eleven hundred years of practice for something you have been doing for nothing since you were sixteen.”

“That is not fair.”

“It is accurate,” Xie Changyin said, “and it is accurate, and that is why it is the trap, and I am telling you it is the trap four counts before you would have worked it out on your own and used it on me.”

## The Wrong Sentence

“The one thing I have to sell anybody,” she said, “is that somebody will be standing in the room when you do it.”

“I know what that is. It is what was said to me in a shed at a mill five months ago.”

“It is what was said to you in a shed at a mill five months ago and I have never once said it in those words and I have said it forty times in forty-two years and you did not hear it until this morning because you were busy with the money.”

“You are right about the money and you were right about the finger and you were right about the arm and you were right about the layer being a fault.”

“Yes.”

“You are wrong about one thing,” Ning Yan said, “and I do not think you know it, and I am going to say it once and I am not going to argue with you about it.”

“Go on.”

“A person on a roll is not safer than a person off it,” Ning Yan said. “You have believed that for forty-two years and you have kept the dates on about forty people who said no and not one of them has had one single thing happen to them for refusing, and you think that is because they are off it.”

Xie Changyin stood at the end of a bench in a shed at the end of a lane and did not answer for eleven seconds.

“No,” she said. “I do not know that it is wrong. I am going to say the rest of what I have, and then I am going to go, and you are not going to argue it with me either, because if you argue it we will do this for two hours and I am sixty-one and there is a road behind me.”

“That is fair.”

“That is not fair either. It is the whole of what I have brought two miles to say and I am going to say the other half of it standing up, because I would rather be standing when a person of eighteen tells me I am wrong than sitting down on a bench like a woman of sixty-one at the end of a working day.”

## The One Thing She Wins

“They are tying the folder now,” Xie Changyin said. “That is the man of sixty-one with the string. It goes east on the first with nothing in it, and he has told you, and he has told you that there is nowhere in the book to put it.”

“He has.”

“So there is a sheet on a bench in that room with a fine hand on it that four people cannot read, and there is one person on nine days of road who can read it, and that person declined to be recruited in this building on the third of a month, and there is no rule anywhere in forty-two years that lets anybody ask her again.”

“They are not going to ask me.”

“They are not going to ask you and I have told the room so in the ordinary voice and the room agrees with me and the room is right, and we are all going to go on being right about it for about eleven months.”

Xie Changyin took her hands from behind her back.

“A girl of twenty-two is going to go on not knowing what a hand said about her until she knows some other way. That is what your no has bought. I am not going to thank you for it and I am not going to apologise to you for it either, because both of those would be me deciding how you feel about a thing I did to you on purpose in the best interests of a building.”

“I would have liked it bought the other way round.”

“So would I,” Xie Changyin said. “I have said that sentence to about nine people in forty-two years and I have never once said it to one of them, and I am saying it to you, and I am going now.”

## The Part She Gave Away

She was at the door when she said it and she said it without turning round, which is not a thing she has ever done in front of a person in her own hearing.

“There is a woman I recruited nine years ago who was in your position in almost every particular, including the shed,” she said. “She is alive and she reads on the far side of the counter from where you would have sat.”

“I have not seen her in six years and I do not know where she is and there is no rule that would let me find out. I have read the two sheets she wrote out in that room four hundred and six times and I have never once read one of them to her.”

“You told me that at a mill.”

“I told you that at a mill and I did not tell you the half of it, and the half of it is that I am giving you the address of the shelf they are on and the date on the string and the number of the room, and I am doing it because you are the only person on nine days of road who has just been entered four times and is going to go and look.”

“That is a favour.”

“No,” Xie Changyin said. “That is an ordinary consequence. You are a person who reads things, I am a person who has shelves, and you have a line in a book that is not a name, and I am sixty-one and I am going up a road in the morning. Take the address or leave it.”

She turned round at the door.

“You read those two sheets and you will find that a woman wrote them and not that a woman signed them. And then you will know exactly what it is that you have just refused and what it is that you have just paid for, and I am not going to help you with either, because you will do it in about nine seconds and I would rather be eleven miles away.”

She went down the lane at the ordinary speed of a woman who is sixty-one and who has two miles to do.

## What Ning Yan Did With It

She stood in a shed with a door that does not shut properly and she did not go after her and she did not write the address on the board.

She wrote the address on the board.

She wrote it with the point out of the bar, in her own hand, in the ordinary way, and she could not write a false line and she did not want to, and it went into the wood of a shed four miles east of a mill whose wheel has not turned since before she came, and she has never once done that with a thing anybody gave her.

That is a column. That is a request. She worked that out on the twenty-seventh of a month four months ago with the point of a bar on a board in this same shed and she has not forgotten it and she did it anyway.

She looked at it for a minute and then she scraped it off, all of it, down to the wood. It took a while and she got the dust up with the side of her boot, and the board went back to being a board.

And then she got out the bar, and squared the blade, and put the bar against the wall where it goes, and she stood there with her hand on it and worked out that she had done the second thing she came into this shed knowing.

The first was the refusal, which is in a book at the far end of nine days of road and cannot be taken out.

The second was this: she has decided to leave a trade without knowing what it costs, and she has decided to be somebody nobody on nine days of road is looking for, and she has done both of them in eleven days, and there is not one person on that ground she could go and tell either of them to.
