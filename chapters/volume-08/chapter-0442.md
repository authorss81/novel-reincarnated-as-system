# Turned Back At The Chain

She was turned back once before she crossed it, and not for anything she did.

It happened on the twenty-seventh. She came to the gate at the sixth hour in the morning with the bar over her shoulder to fetch water at seven miles, going west, and the chain was across and the man stood inside it with the cup in his hand and his stool set square.

“Turn round,” he said.

She stopped. She had been through that gate four times now without a question and without a mark. Her breath ran four counts. Her arm hung open.

“Not for anything you did,” he said. “I have not asked you anything and I am not going to. The chain is across and I am the one who stands inside it, and that is the whole of what is happening here.”

She knew those words. He had said them to her on the second of the month eleven miles west of nothing, word for word. She had thought they were for the first time only. They were not. They were what he says when the chain is across.

“All right,” she said. “How long.”

“Until the eighth hour,” he said. “A team is coming through with a load that takes the whole road. You would be under it. Go back four hundred yards and stand in the road.”

She went back four hundred yards and stood in the road. She had done this before, on the second, in the flat light, with a leg gone and an arm open. This time her leg held. This time the sun was morning sun. She stood and watched the road stay empty for two hours.

## Four Hundred Yards

Four hundred yards back the road dips a little, enough that the gate sits half-hidden behind the rise. The kerb runs on, stone after stone. She stood where people who are waiting stand, off to the side where a cart can pass, with the bar butt down in the dirt and her hands resting on it. Her breath ran four counts. Her arm hung open.

She watched the light move. She counted her coppers by touch without taking them out: nine, fold stitched shut. She checked the bar strap for wear at the shoulder point, found it thinning, made a note to rebind it with coat lining. She drank nothing. She ate nothing. Waiting is a thing she knows. She waited at Pei’s gate for a copper a day. She waited at rails. She waited eleven days once for a date she said out loud. Two hours for timber is nothing.

A bird worked the ditch beside the road, quick, head down. She watched it because watching a living thing passes time without spending it. The small place low behind her fourth rib stayed quiet. The aperture through her stayed cracked through and permanent and quiet. She is eighteen and carries all of it the way she carries her bar, over the shoulder, everywhere.

At the seventh hour the road trembled faintly under her boots, timber coming, heavy, taking the whole width. She stood clear and watched it pass with the driver walking at the head, hand up, eyes on the team. The balks rode steady on the long cart, bound with rope. Good rope. Good knots. She knows knots. She approved of them without meaning to approve, the way she approves of square sheets and wiped lamps.

At the eighth hour a team came through from the west with balks of timber on a long cart, taking the whole width between the kerbs, the driver walking at the head with a hand up. The man at the gate stood inside his chain and watched it pass and nodded to the driver. It went on toward the building and turned in at the side where timber goes.

Then he lifted the chain off the post and nodded to her.

She crossed. He did not ask her a question. He did not write anything down. He did not say one word about the morning to anybody. The list of weights and dates lay on his stool with a line on it for the twenty-seventh against nobody she knew.

She fetched her water at seven miles with a copper and carried it back with the skin slung over her left shoulder and the bar in her right hand held at the light end. She had ten coppers now, less one, which left her nine, and bread for one day.

## The Evening Count

That night she counted her coppers by touch in the dark, fold stitched shut, nine coins cool under her fingers. Bread gone by morning. Water in her skin half gone. Bar strap thinning at the shoulder point. She made a list in her head the way she makes every list: bread at the store five miles west, twine if any seller has it, water at seven miles, floor nowhere, she sleeps at the gate free.

The arithmetic comes out. She can stay eleven days more eating once a day. After that she walks west whether the reading is done or not.

She checked the bar strap by feel, found the thin place, planned the rebind with coat lining. She does such mending with her left hand and her teeth. Her right hangs open, four fingers and a thumb, eight of eleven holds. It holds light ends. It holds string. It does not hold heavy ends. That is the arrangement since the middle-bank method went, and she works inside it.

## The Water Mile

The stand at seven miles is a low wall with a trough and a dipper on a chain. The keeper charges a copper and watches the road while you drink. She paid, drank deep, filled her skin, stoppered it. The water was cold. It eased the dust in her throat. She thanked the keeper and shouldered the skin and the bar together, balancing them the way she balances everything, left side taking the load her right cannot.

Coming back the leg stopped at a mile and a half, the way it does when she pushes. No pain. The foot simply comes down where she did not choose. She put the bar into the ground on the wrong side of her and stood on the grip with her whole weight for nine seconds, and it came back. She went on and at the second hour of the afternoon she could do four miles without touching it.

At the gate the man nodded her through without a word. The chain hung in a loop off his arm. The cup sat on the stool. The list of weights and dates lay beside it with lines for the twenty-seventh against names she does not know. No line for her. No copper paid for one. Nobody asked her for one. That suited her. She cannot be given anything and she cannot be paid, and a gate that asks nothing of her is a gate she can use.

At the gate that night she cleaned the skin and stoppered it and set it square.

“You were turned back,” he said in the dark. “I am here and I saw it happen.”

“I was,” she said. “It was not about me. A load takes the whole road. That is all.”

“You waited well.”

“I have had practice,” she said. “Four hundred yards is nothing. I have waited nine years for worse.”

She lay with her hands behind her head. She had been turned back twice now, once on the second and once on the twenty-seventh, and both times the chain had gone up again and both times nobody had written anything down. That was what a gate is. It opens and shuts and the people it turns back are not marked by it.

But she had learned something standing four hundred yards back in the morning sun. When the chain was across she had wanted to argue, not from anger but from habit, from two years of answering because answering was the only thing she had instead of strength. She had opened her mouth and shut it again without a word coming out. The habit was still there. The mouth shutting was new.

She slept with that. The building burned behind her eyelids. The chain hung. The man sat with his cup cold in his hand, nine years at that gate, counting four hundred and more, asking nothing.
