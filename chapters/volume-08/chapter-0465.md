# The Lane Asking For Nothing

The bench had four sheets on it and they had not been there on the eighteenth.

She found them at the sixth hour on the twentieth, laid square and squared at the corners the way the woman of fifty squares everything, and there was a stone on the top one to keep the wind off, and the stone was one of the four off the low bank at the west end of the cut, worn flat on one side from being carried.

There was one of them from the crossing, which she recognised by the chalk dust in the fold. There was one from the frame, which had a wet corner. There were two she had never seen and could not place, and both of them were figures and neither of them had a single word she could read.

She stood in the doorway of the shed and looked at them for a while, and the thing she felt was not gratitude.

It was the feeling of a road that has had a sign put up on it.

## The Woman Of Fifty Comes Down The Lane

She came at the seventh hour with her coat open at the neck and a bar over her shoulder that she put down against the wall of the shed as if she had put it down a thousand times, which she had, in a shed two hundred yards back with nine men waiting on a frame.

“Five came down the lane yesterday,” she said. “I have six on the crossing sheet.”

“The sixth came after the shut.”

“How do you know that.”

“I cannot read a word of either of them,” Ning Yan said. “I have never been able to read a word of either of them since I was eleven years old. What I can do is add the two columns and take one off, and the sixth one has no hour against it and the fifth one is a quarter short of the frame, and a quarter short of the frame on the last line of a day is always a cart that came in after the numbers were written.”

The woman of fifty stood in the shed and did not say anything for a count of four.

“That is the whole of it,” she said. “That is the whole thing I have been doing wrong for nine days.”

“You have been doing it wrong for nine days because you have been counting carts.”

“I have been counting carts for nineteen years.”

“And you have not had anybody who could add two columns and take one off,” Ning Yan said. “That is all I am good for on this ground and it has taken you nineteen years to find it out and eleven days to say so.”

The woman of fifty put her hand flat on the door frame the way she had put it flat on the frame nineteen days ago when she told her that a girl came down the lane with a bundle of paper and stood at that table for four days and said nothing to anybody.

“You have not asked me a single question since then,” she said.

“No.”

“I have been waiting eleven days for you to ask me one.”

## The Question In The Room

It was not a big question. That was the thing Ning Yan had not been ready for. She had spent four days getting ready for a woman to come down a lane and ask her something large, and the woman came down a lane and asked her something small.

“Did you put the dates in my column.”

“No,” Ning Yan said.

“That is all of it?”

“That is all of it.”

The woman of fifty stood in the doorway with her hand on the frame and she did not go, which she had not done in eleven days, which had been the entire shape of the last eleven days from Ning Yan’s side of the lane.

“Then who does.”

## The Silence That Is A Third Silence In Four Days

And there it was, and she had it coming, and it went exactly the way the other two had gone.

There is a thing a person can say that is true and that is not an answer, and she had learned it at a counter over four coppers and she had learned it again in a room with a lamp in it, and she stood in a shed at the end of a lane on the twentieth of a month and learned it a third time in four days.

She could have said: “A voice I cannot describe put them there and I made him stop and I have not been able to make the no take.”

She could have said: “There is a ground underneath this lane that makes a list of work and does not know the difference between the day a thing happened and the day anybody found out, and the whole of what I have got is a column with a pen under it a hundred years.”

She could have said a dozen words and the woman of fifty would have believed her, because the woman of fifty had already decided to believe her on the strength of six carts and a quarter short of the frame.

She said nothing.

She looked at the four sheets on the bench, and the woman of fifty watched her not say it, and understood every part of what was happening, because a woman who runs a floor for nineteen years has watched four hundred people not say a thing to her and knows exactly what each of them looked like doing it.

“All right,” the woman of fifty said.

She picked her bar up off the wall and put it over her shoulder.

“I am not going to ask you again,” she said, “and I want you to hear the shape of that, because I have been not asking for eleven days and that is a different thing from not asking now. Now it is because I have worked out that you are going to answer when you answer and not one day before, and if I ask twice you will find a way to tell me something true that is not an answer, and I have had eleven days of watching you manage that.”

“That is fair,” Ning Yan said.

“It is not fair. It is arithmetic.” She got to the door. “The sheets will be on the bench in the morning. There is a sixth one on the third. If there is ever a day when there is nothing on the bench, that is a day I have nothing for you to do, and I will tell you so on the day and not before.”

## Four Days

They were the four days in the middle of a month that nothing happened in, and she spent them the way she had spent two hundred of them.

She carried bundles down the frame and up. She stood on the bank at the ninth hour and watched Zheng go the length of the cut thirty times without leaning on the near rail, and she did not tell him anything, and that had been a decision for four days now and not a failure.

She read the figures off four sheets she could not read and matched them against each other. On two of the four there was nothing at all wrong, and on one there was a half, and on one there was a six where a five belonged.

She said the half out loud to the woman of fifty at the sixth hour on the twenty-second and the woman wrote it in the back of the day’s sheet in the ordinary hand, in the right-hand column, and did not write a date in the left one, and both of them looked at the left-hand column while she did it and neither of them said a word about it.

Nobody thanked her for the half.

She noticed that on the twenty-third and she noticed it for four or five days afterwards, and the noticing was not gratitude and it was not the want for it she would have admitted to. It was a piece of arithmetic. A floor that has four sheets a day going into a nine-foot room has acquired, this month, a person who checks the figures in them. That is not a kindness. That is a hand on a wheel.

And on the twenty-fourth she worked out that the last time a floor put her name anywhere in two months she had been standing in the open at the sixth hour reading a man’s day out loud on a bank, and that a woman of fifty had heard it and had gone on running the floor, and that everything the lane had to say to her about it was a stone on a sheet.

## What She Wants

She worked it out on the boards on the twenty-fifth, with the bar against the wall and the light going.

She had a hundred and eleven days of not getting stronger and no wages and no season to get stronger in. She had asked one person in her life for a second pair of hands and it took eleven days to arrive, and the woman who arrived had walked eleven miles to do it, and the stage took six days and two hours of a woman’s hands that were doing something else at the time.

And there was nothing on nine days of road to ask.

So the thing she wanted was not a stage and it was not hands. It was a figure. There was one number in seven years that had ever been put in front of her about herself that was not a price for something, and she had it, and it had been in her head since the fifth of a month, and she had carried it down two miles of lane and up to a bench with four sheets on it without once having anything to set it against.

Five days and two hours.

That was what five days and two hours was worth, and she had spent seven weeks turning it over, and the reason she had not been able to put it down anywhere was that it was a figure and she was a person who reads figures, and a figure with nothing to go on is not a fact. It is a shape somebody has made up.

She wanted the other one. She wanted the one that went round the outside of it.

## The Twenty-Fifth, At The Ninth Hour

She shut the door, because she had learned that at a shed at a mill four months ago, and the boards were cold under her feet and the rain was not coming off the roof yet.

“Yuan.”

Nothing. She waited the way she had waited eleven times in seven years, four counts in and four counts out, and she got to about two hundred and then she stopped counting because counting was the only instrument she had and she was not going to use it on a silence.

“I want the whole of it,” she said. “Every day. From the beginning, not from the fifth of this month. In one number.”

The shed was quiet in the way a shed is quiet, which is a roof and a bench and a bucket and a man who is not there.

She stood with her back against the door and her arm open at her side and she waited for the length of a night, and nothing came back, and at some point in it she understood that this was not a man deciding whether to answer. She had heard that silence. It took four counts and then it turned into an argument, and she had heard it twice.

This one had nothing behind it at all.

He had said “I know” once, in the middle of all of it, and he had said “that is further than anybody has got” once, and he had put a fourth thing down on the thirteenth and told her so, and then he had gone quiet, and she had thought at the time that he was being punished and had not once considered that there was nothing in him to punish.

She slept on the boards with her boots on because she was too tired to take them off, and she had stopped counting how many nights that was some time in the second year.

At the fourth hour she woke and the shed was the same shed and the four sheets were on the bench with the stone on the top one and the door did not shut properly and the wind was coming under it.

She lay there and looked at the boards and she found that she was not angry. That was new and she did not trust it. She had spent two years getting good at being angry at a thing she could not see, and she had got very good at it, and there was nothing in the room to be angry at, because a man who has nothing to spend is not a man who has chosen not to spend one.

At the sixth hour she got up and carried the bundles down and the lane asked her for nothing at all.

And at the seventh hour of the same day, standing at the east end of the cut with four sheets on a bench behind her and a bar over her left shoulder, she worked out the thing that the night had left her with.

She had asked him once in seven years. She had never in her life asked anybody a question twice, not a man at a gate and not a woman of fifty and not a man in her own head, and the reason was not pride. The reason was that a second question is a different question, and a person who asks one twice has told the person they asked that the first one was not the one they meant.

So she could not ask him again.

Which meant that if a man with nothing to spend says nothing at all for a whole night and a whole morning after being asked the only question she had ever wanted answered, then that is the answer, and she has to carry it up two miles of lane to a building with a hundred people in it and be the sort of person who has an answer and does not know it.

She went and did the bundles.
