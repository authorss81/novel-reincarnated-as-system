# A Rate And A Room And A Roll

She came back up the four miles on the tenth at the fourth hour and she was at the shed at the seventh hour on four days running, which is four days more than anybody has stood in that shed since it was a mill.

The folded sheet was still on the boards on the threshold where the woman had put it, and it had been rained on twice and it was not open.

“You have not opened it,” Xie Changyin said.

“No.”

“I am going to tell you what I would have done if I had been you, and then I am going to tell you the rest of it, and the two are not the same thing.”

## The Building

“There is a building eleven miles east of here at the east end of the long road, past the gate with the chain on it.”

“Yes.”

“It is a long low lime-washed building and it is one storey and it is a hundred and forty feet front and there is no hall in it and there is nothing in it that is called a hall. I have been in it for forty-two years and there is no hall in it.”

“All right.”

“There is a counter in it at the near end and about a hundred people at tables down the length of it, and there is a lamp on every table, and every one of them is reading.”

She said the whole of that at the same speed she said everything else, which was the speed of a woman reading a figure off a board.

“In the morning they read the ordinary hands. In the afternoon they read the ones nobody else can. There is a man there who is sixty-one and reads a page in four seconds and it is me, and there are eleven other people there who are better at it than me, and I am going to tell you the ordinary fact about that, which is that eleven of them have read in that building for less time than I have.”

## The Rate

“There is a rate per page. There is a second rate by the difficulty of the hand and there is a third rate for a hand that nobody in the building can read, which is two of the first rate and the third rate is not for me and I have not had one in nine years.”

“Two coppers a page.”

“Two coppers a page,” she said. “And that is why I can tell you the whole of it in a doorway.”

“There is a board two miles up that road and every line on it is priced by the weight of what comes out of the ground or by the length of it, and there is not one line on it for a person who reads, and I have been in front of that board three times in three days on my way to you and I have read all of it.”

Ning Yan said nothing.

“You have told me what a page is worth in a book on nine days of road and it is four coppers. I do not know what it costs where I come from and I have not asked.”

## The Licence

The woman put her hands behind her back.

“There is a licence. It is not a piece of paper that says what you are. It is a piece of paper that says that somebody who has read a hand in that room, with another person in the room who could check it, is a person whose reading can be relied on inside a set of walls, and it does not go out of those walls and it does not have to.”

“Then it is worth nothing.”

“It is worth a great deal,” Xie Changyin said. “You said that faster than I expected you to and I am going to tell you why you are wrong, and I am going to do it without one word of flattery in it, because you are the only person I have offered this to in nine years and I am not going to be coy with you.”

“All right.”

“You cannot be paid.”

“No.”

“You have told me that before the end of your second hour,” Xie Changyin said. “You said it on the second of a month in your own doorway and I did not know what it meant then and I have not stopped thinking about it since, and it took me three days to work out that it is the reason for everything about you, including the four coppers and the four fingers and the strip you carry against your ribs.”

Ning Yan did not move.

“I am not asking you about the strip.”

“No.”

## The Room And The Standing

“There is a room. It is nine feet by eleven and it has a board floor and a lamp and a shelf, and there are four of them in that building and three of them have been empty for eleven years.”

“And standing.”

“Standing means that when you are in a room and somebody cannot read a thing, you are a person they can call, and it is not a favour. It is the only arrangement in eleven hundred years of practice by which a person who has been read against their own word ever had a second reading, and it is not cheap and it is not free and it is not kind. It is a structure.”

She turned her head about a quarter of the way.

“You would be the strongest person in every room you walked into. I am telling you that as a fact and not as a compliment, and you have been the smallest person in every room for two years, and I would like you to sit with how different those two sentences are before you say anything at all.”

## The Roll

“And there is a book.”

“Yes.”

“It is one book and it is kept in a hand and it has four hundred and six names on it at the moment I left, and it is the only thing in that building that I have ever been frightened of, and I have been frightened of it for nine years and I have never got over it and I have never stopped signing it.”

“Sign it?”

“A name on it is a person who cannot be un-recruited. That is the whole of it. You do not strike a name off. There is no hand in that building that takes one off and there has never been one and I have looked, twice, for my own.”

Ning Yan stood in the doorway with her weight on her left leg.

“The trade is called the Order of the Recruited,” Xie Changyin said. “That is the name of it and there is no other name for it and I have never heard of anybody putting a better one.”

## What She Said And It Was Not Wrong

“You have been a reader for two years,” she said, “and you have done it out of a shed for nothing, and it has already taken a finger and half an arm out of you.”

“All right.”

“I am not going to tell you that it will not take anything else, because it will, and I have seen what it takes.”

“Then why are you here.”

“Because you cannot argue with it,” Xie Changyin said. “You can argue with a feeling for as long as you like and I have watched you do it.”

“Be one properly and I will put the list of what it costs in front of you in a column, with the days and the hours against each line. Then you can argue with a number instead of with a feeling.”

She stopped.

“That is the whole of what I have come to say. It is not wrong. I have never once in forty-two years heard it put as well as I have just put it, and I have been saying it badly for nine years to people who had the wrong hands.”

## What Nine Years Is

“There is one more thing and I am going to say it because you have not asked me and I would rather you had it than not.”

“Nine years ago I offered that to a woman who was in your position and she took it, and she was in your position in almost every particular, including the shed, and she is alive and she reads on the far side of the building from the counter.”

Ning Yan did not say anything.

“I have not seen her in six years and I do not know where she is and there is no rule that would let me find out, and that is the part of it that I have never been able to get round, and I have read the two sheets she wrote out in that room four hundred and six times.”

“That sounds like it was a good thing.”

“It was a good thing for six years,” Xie Changyin said. “I have not had a conversation with that woman since the sixth year and I do not know one single thing about her life, and I am going to be honest with you, which is that that is the arrangement and not an accident in it.”

## What She Offered And What She Did Not

“I am not asking you for anything today,” she said. “I have told you a building and a rate and a room and a book and that is all of it, and I have not asked you to read anything and I have not asked you to say anything.”

“No.”

“And I have not asked you about the strip.”

“No.”

“What I will do is this. You may ask me one question. One. And it can be about one person and it can be about nothing on this ground at all, and it will be answered in a room with a lamp in it, and I will answer it in the ordinary voice, and it goes nowhere and nothing is written down and you will be the only person who knows what I said.”

“Any question.”

“Any question,” Xie Changyin said, “except one that would put a third person in a room who is not in it.”

She picked up the folded sheet off the boards, shook the rain out of it, and put it inside her coat.

“I am going back on the fourteenth and I am coming back on the eighteenth, and if you are here on the eighteenth I will be here on the eighteenth, and if you are not I will not come up the four miles again for nothing, because I have done that twice this month and I am sixty-one and this is the last ground I am going to walk across for a while.”

## What Happens To A Person Who Says No

She was at the shed door when she said it, with the bar against the wall and the boards showing behind her.

“What happens to a person who says no.”

“Nothing,” Xie Changyin said.

She said it in the ordinary voice and she did not add anything to it and she stood in the four miles of light with her hand on the strap of the folded sheet.

“That is the answer and I have not given you a soft version of it. Nothing happens. Nobody comes to a shed. Nobody stops a cart. There is no second letter and there is no man with a rule in his hand and there is no week where something is held back from you.”

“All right.”

“In forty-two years I have watched two hundred people be recruited and I have watched about forty of them say no, and not one of those forty has had one single thing happen to them for refusing, and I have kept the dates, because that is what I do, and I am telling you the figure instead of the story.”

She went four feet down the made road and stopped.

“And that is the first honest thing anybody has said to me about a trade in two years, and I have been a reader for two years, and I know what that makes me.”
