# Two Coppers A Page

The price is painted on the board behind the counter where everybody can see it.

Two coppers a page. A second rate by the difficulty of the hand. A third rate at twice the first for a hand nobody in the building can read. Ning Yan had read the board twice on the fifth. On the seventeenth she stood in front of it a third time with nine coppers in her fold and bread for one day and did the arithmetic out loud the way she does everything that has to be carried.

“I read a page yesterday,” she said to the man with the brush. “A fine hand. Nobody in the building could read it. That is the third rate. Four coppers.”

The man set his brush down exact, parallel to the edge of the sheet.

“Yes,” he said.

“Pay me.”

He looked at her. He took in the arm open at her side, the hand with four fingers on it, the coat, the bar left at the gate. He reached under the counter and counted out four coppers onto the wood between them, clean, the way a man pays who has paid forty hands a month for years.

She put her hand out. Her hand stopped in the air over the money the way it stopped over four coppers on a plank bench at a yard three months back and the way it stopped over eight coppers on Wei Ran’s book. It did not shake. It simply stopped, as if the air had gone solid.

She could not take it. She cannot be paid and never could, and the price of a page is two coppers, and she has nine, and none of it matters because her hand will not close on money given for reading.

She put her hand down at her side.

“I cannot,” she said.

He did not move the coppers. “Take them.”

“I cannot,” she said again. “Not because I will not. Because I cannot. My hand stops. It has stopped three times. It will stop every time.”

He studied her for four seconds. Then he swept the four coppers back under the counter with one motion, practised, without reproach. He has been behind that counter for years and he has seen hands stop for worse reasons.

“The rate stands,” he said. “The book stands. You read a page and the page is on the roll. Nothing about the money changes that.”

## The Morning After

She woke at the first hour with her hand open on the boards beside her, empty, the shape of four coins still behind her eyelids. For a moment in the half-dark she thought she could still feel the cool wood where they lay. Then the moment passed. Her breath ran four counts. Her arm hung open. The building stood lit down the road, lamps coming up one by one in the windows.

She ate nothing, having nothing. She drank from her skin, sparing, half left. She shouldered the bar and went to the gate and stood where people who are waiting stand. The man nodded to her. The chain hung. The road ran. The arithmetic of staying sits plain before her: nine coppers, water at seven miles, bread at the store, eleven days eating once a day.

After that she walks west. The rate board stands behind the counter, two coppers a page, fair and true, and her hand will not close on it in any weather. That is the price of the reading, which she cannot take, and never could.

## The Board At Dusk

At dusk she stood before the rate board once more, reading it a fourth time to be sure. Two coppers a page. Second rate by difficulty. Third at twice the first for a hand nobody can read. The paint is fresh where hands have not touched, worn where carriers brush past. Someone repaints it when it fades. Someone wipes the lamps.

Someone ties the folders. The building holds because people hold it, steady, without wasted motion. She respects every hand in it. She cannot join them. Her hand stops in the air over coin for reading, and never will close, in any weather, in any room. She turns away with that plain and carries the next bundle with edges exact.

## The Coppers Under The Counter

She stood at the counter a while after he swept them away, looking at the wood where they had lain. Four coppers. Clean. Counted out without hesitation. The man pays forty hands a month and pays them the same way, brush down, coin out, no speeches. It is a good counter.

It is a fair counter. Pei at the gate, the carter at the stand, Geng on the lifting floor, Rao on the third floor, the man with the brush, the man behind the counter — all correct, none of them made wrong in order to have a problem.

She holds that plain. The best offer any of them has put in front of her came from a woman of sixty-one who keeps a written count of the times she has been right and still does not know which one she got wrong, and the counter is part of why the offer tempts.

She put her hand flat on the wood where the coins had been, briefly, to prove to herself that her hand can touch wood. It can. The wood is cool. The edge worn where hands have rested. Her hand rests there fine. It is money given for reading that stops it, nothing else. Bread bought with gate coppers eats fine. Water at seven miles drinks fine. Four coppers for a fine hand will not come into her hand in any weather.

She lifted her hand away. “Keep the rate true,” she said.

“We do,” he said.

She went out and sat with her back to the lime wash and ate the last of her bread. Xie Changyin came and sat beside her without asking.

She stood there with her empty hand at her side and felt the whole of it land. The Order’s rate is the whole reason the offer is a seduction rather than a job, and she cannot touch it. The ground she stands on has never paid anybody for reading and is not going to start, and the one building that would pay her cannot pay her because of what she is.

She has nine coppers out of twenty-one, spent on bread and water at seven miles and a night’s floor at the far end of a made road, and she will spend them down to nothing and there will be no more coming in for reading, ever, in any weather.

“He paid you,” Xie Changyin said. It was not a question.

“He put four coppers on the wood,” Ning Yan said. “My hand stopped. You have seen it stop.”

“I have,” Xie Changyin said. “On the second of a month in your doorway. I did not know what it meant then.”

“It means I cannot be paid,” Ning Yan said. “Not here. Not on nine days of road. Nowhere. And never could. That is the whole of it.”

She felt grief, plain and heavy, not for the four coppers but for the arithmetic that will never come out. Two coppers a page. Forty hands a month. A room, nine feet by eleven, with a board floor and a shelf. Standing. All of it real. All of it reasonable. And fatal to her anyway, because the one term she cannot meet is the term the whole trade is built on.

That night at the gate she counted her coppers by touch. Nine. Still nine. Bread gone by morning.

“The price is two coppers,” she said out loud. “I have nine. And I cannot take four.”

“I know,” he said in the dark. “I am here and I am not going to tell you it is fair.”

“It is fair,” she said, angry, reckless, tears standing without falling. “That is the worst of it. It is fair and it is good and it is competent and I cannot touch it.”
