# Four Days Apart

She was on the bank at the sixth hour on the thirteenth and the woman of fifty put the day sheet down on the table and walked out of the room, and at the eighth hour she came and stood in the doorway and did not come in.

A man was at the table.

She did not know him. He was fifty and thick through the middle and he had his own hand on the sheet and he was reading the line the woman of fifty had not finished writing, and he was saying something about the low end being laid and the chain being oiled and that being all of it.

He said it once and did not say it twice. He is not a man who repeats himself. Then he put his thumb on the line and looked at the woman of fifty in the doorway and she said, “The thirteenth,” and he said, “The thirteenth,” and she wrote it.

She wrote it in the right-hand column.

## The Cell That Was Not Empty

Ning Yan was standing inside by the shelf with her back to the table and she was looking at the shelf, and she did not move for some time.

Then she came round the table and looked down at the line.

The line was a length of sill at the crossing, forty yards by the length, the low end, laid on the ninth of a month — and it was in the right-hand column, because the day the man came down the lane and told the woman of fifty was the thirteenth.

The left-hand column had a date in it.

She had been in this room six times and had looked at that column every time.

## The Ninth

She got down off the edge of the table on to one knee on the boards, which she has not done in front of anybody since she was at a third floor four times a month, and she put her face down to the level of the sheet, and she read the cell again with her eyes four inches off the paper.

The ninth.

Not the thirteenth. The ninth.

She has never in her life been able to read a hand. Three entries, two years, four hundred and seven names on a roll, and she still cannot get one word out of a page of writing in front of her.

She can read a column. She can read two columns against each other, and she has been doing it for two years for nothing on nine days of road, and nobody nine days of road west ever paid her a copper for it and nobody was going to.

She read two columns against each other on a board floor in a nine-foot room at the end of a lane east of the turn and there were two dates on one line and they were four days apart, and the earlier one was four days before anybody in this building knew anything about the work at all.

She stood up off her knee and put her hand flat on the edge of the table.

## Four Days

She did the arithmetic standing up and she did it twice and she did it the way she does everything she has to carry.

He finished the sill at the last of the light on the ninth. She was on the cart track. She held four wedges in a ring in the dark for two hours.

He did not come down the lane. She walked the mile behind him that night and watched him go past the shed and past the low door in the far bank and out past the crossing and away west, and she stood in the lane and thought it was ordinary that a man finishes a sill and goes home.

He came down the lane on the thirteenth, four days later, and said so in a room with nine feet of it, and the woman of fifty wrote the thirteenth down in the column she always writes in.

And the ninth was already in the other column. In a hand she cannot read. Put there four days before, by something that was not the woman of fifty and not the man at the table and not the floor.

## What She Cannot Do With It

She stood at the table with her back to the bench behind her and could not get one step further.

That is the honest part and she went over it four times while she stood there, the way she goes over a sum.

Her instrument is columns. She reads a column and she reads two columns against each other, and what a column tells her is that two numbers are not the same. It does not tell her what a number means. She has known that since she was eleven and it is the whole shape of what she is good at and she has never once pretended otherwise.

Two dates that are not the same can be eleven things. A man who forgot. A cart that came late. A gate that was shut for rain. A floor that is behind and says so at the ninth hour. And a woman of fifty who has ruled a column for nineteen years and has never known what to put in it.

Eleven lines in four years on the back books, and every one of them earlier than the day the floor found out, and a woman who has had the book open four years and has never once queried it.

Nothing she owns can read that and say “this is wrong”.

If she had the fine hand — Dai's fine hand, the one that cut close and has a name at the foot, the one four people in a building could not read and she read on the ninth of a month, five days back — then the left-hand cell might carry a name and a floor and a figure and she would know what she was looking at.

She does not have the fine hand. She has given that up on purpose and she will never get it back, and it was a denial and a denial is an entry, and she has not reopened it once in all the time since.

So she has two columns and two dates and nothing else on nine days of road that will tell her what a date four days early means.

She looked at the man at the table. He was reading the sill line with his thumb on it, satisfied, the way a man is satisfied with a thing he has done and reported.

She could have asked him. She had a question and it was a good one and it was the only instrument she has ever had.

She did not ask it, because the answer he would give her is “the ninth”, and she already has the ninth, and she has had the ninth since the eighth of a month when she watched a man take a bar off a wall.

She put her hand flat on the table for a moment and took it away.

## The Man At The Table

She went and got the back books off the shelf herself, all four of them, and put them on the bench in order on the same day, and she was not asked to and she did not ask.

She read the left-hand column of every line in four years again, all of them, twice, and there were still eleven dates in it.

Then she found the twelfth.

It was on a sheet dated the thirteenth of a month and it had a length of sill against it and a day in the right-hand column and a day four days earlier in the left one, and it was the line the woman of fifty had finished writing an hour before she came out of the room.

She wrote nothing on it. She squared it and turned it round and put it back in its gap on the shelf with the other three, and she stood with her hand on the shelf.

That is the second time in her life. The first was a floor sheet in a room about a hundred feet long with nine men in it, and a heading over a second column, and it cost her nothing at all and it has been the most useful thing she has ever done.

This one she did not write on. It is not her column. She found it and she left it and she is going to have to stand in this room again tomorrow and read it again and not be able to do one single thing with it.

## The Thing She Is Going To Have To Do Tomorrow

Here is what she worked out on the walk back and it took the whole of the lane and it was not comfortable.

She is going to be standing in that room at the sixth hour tomorrow with a sheet that has two dates in it four days apart, and she is going to read it again, and it is going to be there again, and nothing on this ground is going to change because she has looked at it.

A man of fifty wrote a heading over a second column once because a woman read his sheet out loud in a room with nine men in it and stopped him in the middle of it.

That is how that happened. It took a voice, in a room, in front of witnesses, out loud, to a man who runs a floor, and it took nineteen years of a sheet that had a gap in it before anybody noticed.

She is standing in a room with one woman in it at the sixth hour on a lane nobody comes down, and the woman is not a fool, and the woman has been looking at that column for four years without asking.

Ning Yan went up the lane at the fifth hour and put her hand on the gate post at the crossing and left it there for a while.

She could say it. A floor sheet, once, out loud, in a room about a hundred feet long with nine men in it, and it stopped a man in the middle of a line, and there is a heading over a second column in a room nine days of road west because she did it.

She could say four words to a woman of fifty in a nine-foot room and put a question to the only reader of her that this ground has ever had.

She did not do it. She has a rule she has kept for four days, which is that she does not ask anybody to do anything, and she keeps it because the last time she asked anybody to do anything she said a date out loud on a road and could not take it back eleven days later.

So she went back to the shed and did not say it, and the gap in her is going to be in that column tomorrow morning and on the morning after that and on the morning after that.

## The Walk Down The Lane

At the ninth hour she carried the day sheet back to the table and put it down square and said, “Nothing is wrong with it.”

“Is there something wrong with it,” the woman said.

“No,” Ning Yan said. “Six carts in and eight lines out and two of those are one cart twice and the sill has both its dates on it now, and the sill is correct.”

The woman of fifty took the sheet and looked at it and looked at her and did not ask a single question about the left-hand column, and Ning Yan understood that the woman had not noticed, and had never noticed, and would never notice, and that this had been true for four years.

She went out past the frame and up the lane to the crossing and stood four feet off the gate in the last of the light and looked at the board on the near post.

There were four lines of chalk on it. There had been five on the eighth.

She could not read a word of any of them and she had been within forty feet of the man who could, and she had stood at the gate on the sixth and the seventh and the eighth and counted the lines, and she had watched him go north on the eighth of a month with a bar on his shoulder and she had not thought to learn a single one of the words.

She went back the mile to the shed with the lane smell in her clothes and got the boards and lay down on them in the dark with the low place behind her fourth rib quiet and her breath at four counts.
