# What A Form Is

She worked it out on the twenty-ninth at the sixth hour, on the back of a cart sheet at the end of the lane, with the sun on it and a woman of fifty counting carts a mile away and nobody within nine feet of her except the water coming off the cut.

A form is a page with a place on it for a person to put themselves.

That is all it is. It is not a contract and it is not a test and it is not a threat.

It is a piece of paper that a room has made so that a person can stand in front of it and leave a mark. Every office on nine days of road has one, and she has signed eleven of them in eight years, and every single one of them was a piece of paper with a place on it for a person to put themselves.

She had got that far by the second hour and she had known it since she was thirteen years old, and it took her four more hours to get to the part that was actually doing the work.

## The Part That Was Doing The Work

The sheet is read to you.

Not shown to you. Not handed to you. Read to you, out loud, by a person who is qualified to read it, in a room, in daylight, on the condition that you confirm or deny.

And then, on the twenty-ninth, at about the seventh hour, she found the hinge of it, and she had to put the cart sheet down on the ground to get it out:

You cannot read it yourself.

That is why. That is not ceremony. That is mechanics.

The paper is in a hand. The hand is the fine one, the one that comes about nine times a year down the mill road, and a woman of twenty-two has just watched four people fail at one.

A person who is about to be put into a book for the rest of their life is not handed the paper and told to get on with it. A paper can be folded into four and taken home. A paper can be shown to a mother. A paper can be read out of a room by somebody who has been paid two coppers a page to be interested in the wrong direction.

So it is read to you. And that is not an insult. On this ground, with these hands, that is the only way it can be done.

And the reason it is read to you is not fairness.

Ning Yan sat on the bank at the end of the lane with the sun going and said the sentence out loud, because she says everything out loud exactly once.

It is read to you so that there is no copy.

## What It Actually Is, Said By Her And Not By Anybody Else

She stood up off the bank at about the seventh hour on the twenty-ninth and said all of it to the water coming off the cut, and here it is, and it has taken her eight years and four months and twenty-nine chapters and it is not in any book on nine days of road:

Recruitment is a reading performed on a person, by somebody who is qualified to read it, in a room, on the condition that the person confirms or denies.

Not a job. Not an offer. A reading.

A woman of sixty-one is going to hold a sheet in her own hand, in a room, and read out what it says about you, and you are going to say yes or no, and the yes goes in a book that has four hundred and seven names on it, and the sheet is read to you rather than given to you so that there is never one word of it anywhere except in the room and in the person who read it.

That is the trade. That is what she has been carrying eleven miles for five months.

And it is the same act as every other one that has ever been done to her.

A man of forty-three in a room with a lamp in it turned a sheet round four months ago and said he was going to say four things, and the first thing on it had her name at the head of it, and she said no, and the no went up the same road every answer goes up.

She refused to be read. That is all a refusal in a room with a lamp in it was, four months back, and it cost her everything she wanted.

A woman of sixty-one is going to read a different sheet to her, about the trade and not about her, and ask her to say yes.

Both of them are the same shape. One of them had her name at the head of the paper. The other one will have her name at the foot of the book.

## The Maximum

She went up on the first at the sixth hour and Xie Changyin had the sheet in her hand, and it was not one piece of paper.

It was four.

The first was the terms. The second was the room, nine feet by eleven, board floor and a shelf and a lamp, and there are four of them in that building and three of them have been empty for eleven years.

The third was standing, so that when somebody in a room cannot read a thing she is a person they can call. That is not a favour and it is not cheap and it is not free and it is a structure.

The fourth was her own line in the book, with the date against it in the ordinary hand, by a man of sixty-one who has written four hundred and seven before this one and knows every one of them.

And the rate was not the rate on the board.

“There is one rate,” Xie Changyin said. “Four coppers for every page, whatever the hand, and there is no second rate and no third rate and there is nothing anywhere in this building that says a page is harder than another page. The board behind the counter is wrong and I have had it wrong for nine years and I am not going to have it repainted, because I would rather it stood there and be wrong than be right and cost more than it should.”

“That is worse for you.”

“It is worse for me and it is better for you and you have worked out in about nine seconds which of those two things is why I am doing it,” Xie Changyin said. “That is the whole of the maximum and there is nothing behind it. I have no more terms. There are no more terms.”

## The Ordinary Condition

She put the four sheets square on the table and sat down, which nobody in that building had seen her do, and she put her right arm out of the way of the table edge because it gets in the way.

“Here is the part you have not been told yet,” she said, “and I am going to tell it to you exactly the way it has been told to about two hundred people in forty-two years, because you would not believe a version of it that I had improved for you.”

She put her hand flat on the top sheet.

“This is not a paper I am giving you. This is a paper I am going to read to you. And when I have read it you are going to say one word, and either word is the right answer, and I am going to write down whichever one you say, and I am going to read it back to you before it goes in.”

“Then why is that a good thing.”

“Because in forty-two years I have watched two hundred people recruited and about forty of them say no,” Xie Changyin said, “and not one of those forty has had one single thing happen to them for refusing, and I have kept the dates, because that is what I do, and I am telling you the figure instead of the story.”

“You told me that in a doorway five months ago.”

“I told you that in a doorway five months ago and you held a silence in daylight for a minute and I walked away up the road and I did not come back up those eleven miles for a while,” Xie Changyin said. “Nothing has happened to you. You are standing in the room. That is the whole of what I have to promise and I am not going to improve it.”

## What She Wanted Out Of The Room

She looked at the four sheets on the table and she worked out what she wanted and it was not the rate.

“I want it read to me,” she said.

“That is what it is for.”

“Not on the first,” Ning Yan said. “On the second, at the fourth hour, with the man who keeps the book in the room, and I want the room to know it is going to happen.”

Xie Changyin looked at her for four counts.

“You have said a date out loud to a stranger in a room,” she said, “and you have done it twice, and both times it took you eleven miles and you went anyway. I am not going to argue with a habit of yours when it is doing the thing I want it to do.”

“It will cost me something.”

“It will cost you about an hour of the best light in the day and it is the only hour in the day when I have ever been able to say anything to anybody at all, and that is not a joke, because a lamp in this building is worth nothing at the fourth hour in a month and everything in the world at the seventh.”

She stood up and squared the four sheets and put them in a string.

“I will be here on the second at the fourth hour,” she said. “And I am going to read it to you the way it has been read to two hundred people, and you are going to listen to it as a document and not as a decision, because it is a document. The decision is one word and it is on the last line and it is the shortest line on the paper.”

## The Two Miles Back

She walked the two miles down the lane at the seventh hour with the light going and her arm open at her side and the bar over her left shoulder, and she went the whole way without saying one word out loud to the water or the hedges or anybody.

Here is why, and she gave it to herself at the half-way stone in the ordinary voice:

She has spent eight years being the person a document is done to. She has been read. She has been entered, three times, and every one of the three was a page somebody else held. She has refused one and it is still on a floor with nothing at the other end of the rule. She has answered one and a name went into the best book on nine days of road.

In one day and four hours she is going to sit in a room and let somebody qualified hold her own terms in her own hand and read them out loud to her, and then she is going to say one word.

That is not a job interview. That is not a ceremony. That is the exact shape of every single thing that has ever been done to her, done to her on purpose, by the best of them, in daylight, on the ordinary condition.

And she is going to sit there and let it happen.

That is the want.

She is not going into that room to say a word. She is going into it because there is not one person on nine days of road who will tell her what is written on a sheet she cannot read, and she is the only person on nine days of road who can read it, and the last time that was true a woman of sixty-one walked eleven miles into a shed at a mill and said two lines and a date to a stranger.
