# The Woman Who Came On Foot

The cart was standing in the mill yard with the shafts on the ground and one of them under the near wheel.

She went the four miles east at the ninth hour on the twenty-ninth with the bar over her right shoulder, past the low sound the millrace makes when there is no water in it, and she came into that yard and the cart was there and the wheel was not turning and it had not turned since before she came.

The driver was asleep sitting up on the seat board with his hat over his face and his feet on the shaft.

There was a strip of paper on the seat board beside him, folded once, and a stone on it to hold it against the wind.

## What Was On It

She stood about nine feet off it for a while and then she came the four feet and read it.

There was a date on it.

That was the whole of what was on it. One line, one date, at the top of it, in the ordinary hand of the counting house, which is a hand that comes about nine times a year and which she has been reading off boards and rails and stacks of strips for seven months without once having to stop and think about it.

The eighteenth.

She did not know which eighteenth and she stood in that yard and made herself do it properly, the way she does everything, which is to take it apart and put it back and take it apart again.

There was a hand in this country that writes about nine times a year and it is not the hand that date is in. A date in an ordinary hand is the ninth of a month or the first of a month and it is put at the top of a strip so that a man with a brush can put it into a book in the right place.

Nothing on nine days of road has ever come down that road with a date on it and nothing else on it.

## The Arithmetic

She did not wake him.

A tray goes up the mill road at the sixth hour on the first. There is a cord round it. There is a name in it. It comes back down on the ninth with the empty trays going up and a man with a brush copies whatever is on it into a book that shuts.

The cart that takes the empty trays up is a cart that goes to the mill and stops at the mill. It does not go east. She worked that out herself three months back at a cart track by asking two men about carts.

So a strip that goes east does not come back on that cart, because that cart does not go east.

And a strip that does not go east comes back on the ninth.

And she was standing in a yard on the twenty-ninth of a month with a piece of paper on a cart seat with a stone on it and one date on it, and the date was the eighteenth, and she had watched that cart go down the mill road the evening before at the seventh hour past the gate and past the counting house and off west.

## She Did Not Take It

She read it four times, standing at the near wheel, and she did not put a hand out for it, and there was nothing in the yard within nine feet of her that could tell her afterwards that she had.

There was a man asleep eight feet away with a hat over his face.

If she had put it in her coat it would have gone to Lowwater with her and it would have gone to the second floor of that building on the first of the month in a bundle with a cord round it, and a man with a brush on the floor below would have copied one date into a book that shuts, and there would have been nothing on the page about where it had been between the two.

She left it on the seat board under the stone and she went back the four miles and dug the run at the gate and got a copper, and there were twenty-three in the fold in the inside of the door frame.

## What She Did Not Do About It

There were three places inside nine days of road where a thing she did not understand could have been put in front of somebody who was paid to look at it.

There was a post on the mill road with a board on it and a man on that board with a paste pot and a rag and a woman at the post and a boy of about twelve. He had fetched his strips from that counting house on the first of every month for nine years, because that is the only morning anything comes down that road.

There was a third floor with forty books open on a table the length of nine men and a woman of forty-five on it who has never once said a thing to her that was not correct.

And there was a room two miles up a made road with a lamp in it and a man of forty-three in it whose whole trade is a hand she cannot read.

She walked past the post at the fourth hour on the first and did not stop at it, and she worked out while she was walking why, which is that a man with a paste pot would take the strip and read the word at the head of it out loud in a doorway in exchange for nothing at all.

So she did not go. And she did not go up the hill either, and she said both of those out loud at the boards that night, which is a thing she does not do about most of the decisions she makes.

## The Thirty-Third Hour Of A Month

There was no thirty-third hour. She wrote the month out on the boards that night anyway, which is a thing she has done since she was eleven, and she put a mark against the thirtieth and a mark against the twenty-ninth and she did not put a third mark anywhere.

She lay on the boards and said it out loud, four times, because the second hour is for it.

“A cart came down this road at the seventh hour yesterday with a tray on it and a cord round it and it did not stop.”

“A strip that goes east does not come back on the cart that takes the empty trays, because that cart does not go east.”

“A strip came back on a cart that does not go east.”

“And it had one date on it and it was the eighteenth and it was the only thing on it.”

She lay there.

“Or the cart goes east now. And I cannot tell which of those from a date, because I have never seen the two lines and nobody has ever put them in front of me at the same time.”

## The Second Hour Of The First

She said it four times that night because the second hour is for it, and she got the same answer four times, and she had said those words before in a made road with a mill on the left of it and she had got the same answer four times then as well.

The answer was that somebody knew the day she had been asked a question on, and had written it down, and had put it in a place where it would come back to her, and there is no office inside nine days of road that has ever sent anybody anything.

Every strip that has ever come down that road came down because somebody at the far side of a room had put a pen to paper at a desk. That is what a strip is. A strip is a person writing something down about a thing that has already happened, in a building, and it going down a road afterwards.

A strip is not a thing anybody sends to anybody.

She had known that since she was seventeen and it had never once come up.

## The Cart Stand And The Wheel

The cart had been standing three days and the near wheel had sunk into the yard about four inches where the ground is soft, which is a thing that happens to a cart that stands still and a thing that does not happen to a cart that is being unloaded.

She had walked the yard on the twenty-ninth and the thirtieth and had not asked anything about it, because the driver had been asleep both times and there was no reason on nine days of road to wake a man asleep in a cart about a wheel.

She worked it out standing at the low side on the thirtieth instead, which is what she does. A cart that stands three days in a yard with a shaft up has either been paid for or it has not. This one had no load on it. A cart with no load standing three days in a yard at a mill whose wheel has not turned since before she came is a cart that is waiting, and there is nothing in this ground to wait for at a mill.

And it came from the east, because the wheel was on the right-hand side going down and the right-hand side going down is the side that comes down, and the shaft was not broken and the near wheel was under it.

So it had gone out eleven miles and come back, and it had stopped at the mill and not at the counting house, and the man on it had gone to sleep.

## The Mill Road In Daylight

She went west at the fifth hour on the first with twenty-five in the fold and came back at the eleventh with four loaves and seventeen in the fold and bread for five days, and the tray went up the mill road at the sixth hour with a cord round it while she was two miles off, and she stood a hundred yards short of the counting house and watched it go for the fourth time in four months.

She was at the shed at the sixth hour on the first and she went out to the door because the horse was making a noise she had not heard on that ground before.

The cart came down the mill road at the seventh hour.

It came from the east, past the mill, the way it had come on the twenty-eighth, and it turned in at the mill yard instead of going on west, and it stopped with the shafts down and the near wheel where it had been four days before, and a man got off it and put his hand out to help somebody down.

Nobody got off it.

The woman got off it herself, one hand on the side board, and she stood in that yard and looked west up four miles of road at a place she had never seen and she could not have told anybody how she knew to look west.

She was sixty-one. She was in a plain coat with nothing in her hands, and her boots were mud to the ankle, and she had come on foot the last four miles because the driver had not offered and she had not asked.

She came up the four miles. She came past the flat ground and past the dry millrace and she came up to a shed at a mill whose wheel has not turned since before she came, and there was a woman standing in the open doorway of it with a bar over her right shoulder.

The woman in the yard looked at her for four seconds.

“Who keeps this shed,” she said.

“Nobody.”

“Then I want the woman of eighteen who reads.” She put one hand on the door frame. “I am going to stand here either way. I would rather stand here than be told to go and find out.”
