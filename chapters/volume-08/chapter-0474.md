# Her Name In The Best Book

The third of a month, at the fourth hour, with the shutters up.

She had said the date out loud in that room three days before and it had cost her three days of walking once, and this time it cost her nothing, and she had known it would cost nothing, and she had still said it, because a date out loud is a date.

The man of sixty-one was at the end of the table with the book shut under his arm. Xie Changyin had the string in her hand. The man with the brush was at his desk and came over and stood four feet off. The young reader of twenty-six was at the far end with his brush down flat on the wood, where he puts it when he wants a room to know he is in it.

Thirty people at tables, give or take a handful. A lamp on every table, worth nothing at that hour.

“I am going to do this first,” Ning Yan said.

“Do what,” Xie Changyin said.

“Read it to you.”

## Read It To Them First

They looked at her.

“There are four people at this table and none of you can read the sheet and all of you know that, and you have all known it for forty-two years, and nobody in this building has ever said so out loud in a room.”

“You have said so out loud,” Xie Changyin said.

“Yes,” Ning Yan said. “On the twelfth of a month, and I said it to four people and I did not tell them why, and I have never told anybody why, and I am going to tell you now because I am going to do something in this room in about a minute and I will not do it in front of four people who think I am the reason.”

She put her right arm out of the way of the table.

“There is a hand in this world that none of you can read. It is the one on that paper and it is the one that comes about nine times a year down the mill road.”

She put her left hand flat on the table edge.

“I have been able to read it for eight years. I did not learn it anywhere. I was not taught it. It is the only thing I have ever been able to do that I cannot account for, and I have said no to having one read to me, out loud, in a room, on the eighteenth of a month four months ago, and my no went up the same road every answer goes up.”

Nobody in the room said anything. The thirty of them went on reading.

“That is why you cannot read it in this room,” Ning Yan said, “and it is not because of the hand.”

“There are nine of you who have been reading for eleven hundred years and four of you failed at a sheet in a month and two hundred years of the practice of a trade has not produced anybody else. It is because I am in the room, and I am the only one, and a woman of twenty-two has been standing at a bench for five months waiting for me to open my mouth about her and I have not done it once.”

Xie Changyin had not moved.

“Why now,” she said.

“Because you are about to read a document to me in this room and I am going to say a word, and the word is going in a book that has four hundred and seven names on it.”

She looked at the man of sixty-one with the book shut under his arm.

“If I am the reason that happens, then in about four years somebody is going to stand in a doorway and ask you how it is that a woman who read a fine hand at a counter on the ninth of a month is the reason a trade of eleven hundred years put a sheet it could not read into a man’s cart. And you will not have an answer. And neither will I, because I cannot say one word about this room in a way that anybody would take.”

She looked at the sheet on the table under the hand of a woman of sixty-one.

“I am not going to do that. So I am going to read it to you first.”

## She Read It

She read it the way Dai reads, sure, without a hesitation anywhere in it, three lines at the fine hand and a name at the foot and then the rest.

It took four counts.

She did not touch the paper. She stood with her arm open at her side and her breath at four counts and she read a document out loud in a room in daylight, and the thirty people at the tables stopped reading for the length of it, which does not happen in that building, and which Xie Changyin noticed and did not comment on.

“The first line is a paper that says a paper,” Ning Yan said, in the ordinary voice, after. “A rate of four coppers for every page of any hand. A room nine feet by eleven with a board floor and a shelf. Standing, so that somebody in a room who cannot read a thing has a person they can call, twice a day at most. A name in a book that does not come off, with the date against it in the ordinary hand.”

She stopped.

“And on the last line, in the same paper, in the same room, by the same person who has read this to two hundred people in forty-two years: this document has been read to you, do you agree to it.”

She put her hands behind her back.

“That is all that is written on it,” she said. “There is no cost on it. There is no finger on it and no arm on it and no turn and no order. I have been reading other people’s sheets since I was sixteen and I have read that paper the whole way through and I want it on the page that I read it and that none of it is a lie and that none of it is a trade either.”

“No,” Xie Changyin said. “It is not a trade. It is a reading.”

“It is a reading,” Ning Yan said. “In a room. In daylight. On the ordinary condition.”

## No

“I am not going to agree to it,” she said.

That was all. She did not put a reason first. She had one ready and she had had it ready for three days and she did not use it, and she has never in eight years been able to work out whether that was pride or a habit she built to survive a yard.

She said the four words and she stopped talking, and the room did not fail. No book would not shut. No door opened. Nothing went up a road. A man of sixty-one did not put his head in his hands and a man with a brush did not drop anything and the young reader of twenty-six did not look up.

Xie Changyin took the sheet, and turned it over, and wrote on the back of it in her own hand, and she wrote for eleven seconds.

And then she did the thing.

## Read Back Before It Goes In

She turned the sheet round and pushed it two inches across the table and she said, in the ordinary voice, at four feet off:

“Ning Yan. Present, at the fourth hour, in the room, on the third of this month, brought in by the terms of her own asking. The terms have been read to her at the fourth hour in the room with the man who keeps the book at the end of the table. She has read the paper aloud to the room. She has declined it. Nothing has been asked of her twice.”

She stopped there and she did not read any further, and she did not add anything, and she waited.

That is the shape of it. That is exactly the shape of it, and it has been the shape of this trade since before there was a building on this ground: the person it is about hears it first.

Nobody in that trade has ever read the entry back to the person before it goes in. She said so in a shed at a mill five months back and she said it again in a room at a gate and she said it a third time in a road in daylight, and Ning Yan had taken all three of those as kindnesses, or as the ordinary care of an institution that is run by a careful person.

It is not a kindness. It is the ceremony turned the right way round for once in eleven hundred years, by a woman who read four hundred and six names and never read one of them back.

“Stop,” Ning Yan said.

Xie Changyin stopped.

“Do not put that in the book.”

“Why not.”

“Because it is the only thing in this that is mine and I am not giving it to you.”

Xie Changyin took the pen off the back of the sheet and put it down on the table square, parallel to the edge, the way a man with a brush puts a brush down.

“Then it will go in as it is,” she said, “and you will read it back to me.”

“No.”

“You will read it back to me,” Xie Changyin said, “or I will not put it in, and a thing that is not put in is not a refusal, it is a person who did not turn up, and in forty-two years I have never once been able to make that distinction and I am not going to start with your name on it.”

## She Read It Back To Her

So Ning Yan read her own refusal back to a woman of sixty-one in a long low room with the shutters up, at the fourth hour of the third of a month, and her voice did the thing her voice does when she is not lying.

She read it in the same words, in the same order, at the same speed, and then she said: “Ning Yan. Present, at the fourth hour, in the room, on the third of this month, brought in by the terms of her own asking. The terms have been read to her at the fourth hour in the room with the man who keeps the book at the end of the table. She has read the paper aloud to the room. She has declined it. Nothing has been asked of her twice.”

She stopped there.

“That is me saying I do not agree to be in that book, and it is going in a book that has four hundred and seven names on it, in the ordinary hand, by a man who has written all four hundred and seven, and there is no hand in that building that takes one off.”

“There is not,” Xie Changyin said.

He came down the room and opened the book on the table beside the sheet and wrote one line and the date against it, and she watched him write it, and it took about nine seconds and it is the rest of this.

It went in.

It is in there now. It is in the best book on nine days of road, in a hand, with the third of a month against it.

## What She Felt

She stood in a room where she had said no for the third time in eight years and she felt something come off her that she had not been carrying on purpose.

It was not relief and she found that out nine seconds later, standing by the counter with nothing in her hands.

It was the third time.

Four months ago in a room with a lamp, to a man of forty-three, over a paper that had her name at the head of it. Five months ago in daylight on a road, to a woman of sixty-one, in silence. And now on the third of a month at the fourth hour in a room with the shutters up, over a paper about the trade, to the same woman, spoken.

Three refusals in eight years, and every one of them went into a book anyway, and this is the fourth page any of them has put in there, and it is in the best book there is on nine days of road and it cannot be taken out.

She put both hands flat on the wood of the counter and left them there for the length of a minute.

“You have been entered four times in eight years,” Xie Changyin said from four feet off, “and this is the first one you chose and it is the one you will not get out of.”

She put her hands flat on the wood of the counter behind her.

“I am not going to congratulate you and I am not going to tell you that you were right. On the first one you gave me I told you you would ask me for the column. You asked. I gave it to you. You read it and you came back the next day, and you do not owe me a word about that.”

“I am not going to say anything about it.”

“You are not going to say anything about it,” Xie Changyin said, “and that is correct, and I would have been worried by a speech.”

The man of sixty-one shut the book and put his palm flat on the boards once, brief, the way a man touches a thing he is frightened of, and took his hand away.

She stood there and let him go down the room.

Four times entered in eight years and every one of the four was a page somebody else held. A finding at seventeen. A second one, carried up out of its own office in a hand she has never seen. A reading in a room with a lamp in it, on the ninth of a month, that put a name in a book because four people could not get a job done.

This is the first one she went at herself, in daylight, at the fourth hour, with the shutters up, and it is the only one of the four that nobody can take out, and she cannot un-enter it.

She is eighteen years old. She has a name in the best book there is and it is the word no, and it is going to be there when she is forty, and it is the first thing she has ever done on purpose that has cost her the whole of what it cost.

That is what she has bought and she has not got a figure for it and there is nobody on nine days of road she could ask for one without the asking being entered as well.
