# One Question About One Person

She chose it on the walk up in the morning.

She had carried three questions for nine days. The first was about the strip against her ribs and what the fine hand says at the head of it, and that one was spent at ch 417 and never gets back and she did not touch it. The second was about the man she cannot see and what he is, and that one would put a third person in a room who is not in it and Xie Changyin had barred it in plain words.

The third was about the woman walking beside her.

She asked the third.

The room was small with a lamp in it and a table and two stools. It was not the long room with a hundred people. It stood off the counter end with a shut door and a window high up. Xie Changyin set the lamp between them and sat down.

“Ask,” she said.

“How were you recruited,” Ning Yan said. “You. At nineteen. Out of a village.”

## The Village

Xie Changyin leaned back on her stool and looked at the lamp flame a moment before speaking. The room was small and shut and warm. Paper rustled past the door, distant, the long room working on without them.

“It sat in a valley with one well,” Xie Changyin said. “Twenty houses. My mother kept a garden. I read hands in winter because there was nothing else to do, and I was right, and people came from two villages over with sheets. I thought it was a game. At nineteen I still thought it was a game.”

Ning Yan listened the way she listens to columns, for the figures under the words. Twenty houses. One well. Three hands right in a winter. A woman with a sheet in her coat.

“The woman stayed three days,” Xie Changyin said. “She watched me read twice without saying who she was. On the third day she asked for a room with a lamp in it. She read the sheet aloud to me in it. It was about me and I confirmed it. I said yes. That was all.”

“What did your mother say.”

“She said a table by a lamp is better than a field in winter,” Xie Changyin said. “She was right. She died while I was in my second year here. I went back for the burial and came back on foot. Eleven miles is nothing. That walk was thirty.”

Ning Yan sat with that. Thirty miles on foot at twenty-one for a burial, and back again to a table. She knows walks taken for the dead. She has carried her brother’s name for years the way she carries her bar, over the shoulder, everywhere.

Xie Changyin said in the ordinary voice.

Xie Changyin nodded once. She had offered one question about one person and not about Ning Yan herself, and this was one person and it was the person in the room, and no third person was needed to answer it.

“I was nineteen,” Xie Changyin said in the ordinary voice. “A woman came to the village with a sheet in her coat. She read it aloud to me in a room with a lamp in it. It was about me and I confirmed it. I said yes. That was all.”

“What did it say.”

“That I could read a hand nobody else in the village could read. That I had read three hands right that winter. That the work would be at a table by a lamp for an institution that would vouch for what the reading says.”

“Did you know what you were saying yes to.”

“No,” Xie Changyin said. “I knew the room and the lamp and the voice. I did not know the book. A name on it is a person who cannot be un-recruited. Nobody told me that part until after.”

The lamp burned between them. Ning Yan watched the flame stand straight up.

“Do you regret it,” Ning Yan said.

“That is a second question,” Xie Changyin said, without reproach. “I will answer it anyway because it goes nowhere. No. I regret nothing in it except the part I told you, that I have not seen the woman I brought in six years and there is no rule that would let me find out.”

Ning Yan sat with that. She had expected the answer to open something. It opened nothing. A village, a sheet read aloud, a yes at nineteen, forty-two years at a table. It was the same offer on the table now, moved back forty-two years.

“It goes nowhere,” Xie Changyin said. “You see that.”

“I do,” Ning Yan said.

She felt angry for a moment, sudden and clean, at herself for having hoped the question would do work. She had spent two years answering because answering was the only thing she had instead of strength, and she had thought asking would be different. It was not different. It was a room with a lamp in it and an answer in the ordinary voice and nothing written down.

She stood up. Her arm hung open. Her breath ran four counts.

“Thank you,” she said, and meant it.

## The Walk Back

Xie Changyin walked her to the counter end and left her there with a hand raised, no more. Ning Yan went out past the windows alone. The daylight hit her face hard after the small room. She stood in the road with her hands empty and worked the answer over the way she works a column, line by line.

Village. Woman with a sheet. Room with a lamp. Yes at nineteen. Table for forty-two years. Mother buried in the second year. One woman brought in nine years ago, unseen six years, no rule to find her. No regret except that one.

It goes nowhere. That was the condition and the answer kept it. It opened no door. It named no price she can pay. It put no tool in her hand. She had spent her one question, offered freely, on another person’s life, and the life is whole and closed and tells her nothing about what to do with her own.

She felt grief rise once, for the village girl at nineteen who said yes without knowing the book, and for herself at eighteen who keeps saying nothing in order not to say yes. Both girls are true. Neither helps the other.

She fetched her bar at the gate and checked its strap for wear, the way she checks everything, and found the strap thinning at the shoulder point. She will need to rebind it before the rains. She has nine coppers and no twine. She will unpick the hem of her coat lining and use that, the way she has mended before.

Xie Changyin stood too. She was sixty-one and she moved like a woman who has sat at tables for forty-two years and walked eleven miles without thinking about it.

“You asked well,” she said. “Most people ask about themselves first. You did not.”

Ning Yan went out past the counter with that sitting in her chest. She had used the one thing she had been offered and it had bought her nothing she could spend. That was the condition and she had known the condition.

At the gate that night she lay with her hands behind her head and said the answer over to herself once to make sure she had it right. Village. Sheet read aloud. Yes. Forty-two years. No regret except one woman unseen six years.

“You asked it,” he said in the dark. “I am hearing you and I am glad you asked it yourself.”

“I did,” she said. “It goes nowhere.”

“Most true things do at first,” he said. “I am here and I am not leaving.”

She slept. The question was spent and the building stood lit and nothing had changed, and that was what going nowhere felt like from the inside.
