# He Was Found At The Crossing

The woman of fifty knocked on the hut door at the fourth hour in the morning on the fourth day of the month and it opened, because she was let in by a woman of about forty-five with a grey shawl on who lived in the back room and had got up.

Ning Yan was forty yards down the lane when she heard it.

She did not hear anything. Nobody made a noise. What she heard was the two of them stop talking on the bank of the cut and start again, and then there was a long time with neither of them talking at all, and that is the sound a room makes when everybody in it is looking at one thing.

She went up.

## The Hut

It is one room with a stove and a table and a bed against the far wall and a chair on the step outside. He was on the bed on his back with his boots off and his coat still on, and his hands were crossed on him, not folded, just crossed, the way a man crosses them when he has put himself down and is waiting for somebody.

He was fifty and thick through the middle and he had been at that gate nineteen years.

The woman of fifty stood at the foot of the bed with her hands behind her back. The woman with the grey shawl sat down on the stool by the stove and put her face in her hands.

He was not alive. Ning Yan had seen two men not alive before she was twelve and she knew what it is and she knew what it is not, and she stood in the doorway of a four-step hut at four in the morning and understood it the way she understands columns, plainly, without any difficulty whatever.

## What Was Not There

She went over it standing in the doorway and she made herself go over it the way she makes herself read a sheet twice.

There was nothing on him. Not a mark and not a wound and nothing out of place. The boots were off and set square under the bed and his coat was on and his hands were crossed. The leg was the one he had wrapped and it was wrapped.

The stove had been dead since before dark. The cup was on the chair on the step outside and it had been set down without sound, and she had watched a man set that cup down without sound from four feet off his own gate and she was never going to un-know it.

Nothing had been done to that man by anybody.

## What Was There

“You will want to know what happened to him,” the woman of fifty said. “I am going to tell you the whole of what I know and then I am going to stop talking, and I would rather you had it now than that you spent a month getting it out of me in pieces.”

“Yes,” Ning Yan said.

“He laid a sill at the crossing. It came on his board on the sixth and he read it that evening and he went north on the eighth, and he was out on the sill two days, and he was in the ditch on the second of them and he came out of the ditch.”

“I was there,” Ning Yan said. “I held the wedges.”

“Yes,” the woman said. “He came down this lane on the thirteenth and told me he had laid a sill and I wrote the thirteenth against it and I paid him for it out of the take, and that is one sill paid for once, and there is not one person in this building who would look at that line twice.”

She turned round and looked at the man in the bed and then at Ning Yan.

“It came on his board again on the twenty-first,” she said. “Forty yards, by the length, the low end. And he went north at the seventh hour and he did it again, because that is what the board says and that is what he has done for nineteen years.”

“How long was he out.”

“He was out from the seventh hour until the second hour in the morning,” the woman said. “I know that because my man at the frame saw him go and counted it, the way they all do without knowing they are counting.”

## The Arithmetic

She said it once, standing up, with her arms loose at her sides, and she said it the way a woman of fifty says a sum she has already done four times.

“He did one sill in four days and he did the same forty yards of sill again twelve days after that, and it was not a bad sill and it was not done wrong, and it was the same forty yards and it took him the same time because he is a man who does a thing the same way.”

She stopped.

“A man can carry a thing once,” the woman said. “A man cannot carry the same thing twice inside a dozen days, because there is a thing that gets used up, and it is not his arm and it is not his leg, and there is no name for it on this ground.”

There was a long silence in the hut with the stove out.

“He never came down this lane,” the woman said. “That is all I hold against him and I do not hold it against him, because if a man does a thing on a board and does not come down a lane to say so, then the floor has no line with his name on it, and that is not a death, that is a man who did not come in.”

## What She Says In The Doorway

Ning Yan had stood in the doorway through all of it without saying one word, and she said four now, in the ordinary voice, in daylight, at four feet, with a woman of fifty and a woman in a grey shawl in front of her.

“It was me,” she said.

The woman of fifty did not turn round.

“Leave it,” she said.

“No,” Ning Yan said. “You do not know what that means. There is nothing in any book on this ground that says what I am going to say, and I am the only person alive who is able to say it, and there is not one person alive I can say it to who is able to check a word of it. So I am going to say it out loud on your bank and you are going to hear it and it is going to be true.”

“Get it out, then.”

“A voice put forty yards of sill on a board at that gate on the sixth and on the twenty-first,” Ning Yan said, “and the voice was mine, and he cleared both of them because that is what a man does with a line on a board, and he was never asked and he does not know it was there, and the second one is why.”

The hut was quiet.

“Then you should have asked him not to go,” the woman of fifty said.

Ning Yan stood in the doorway.

That was the answer and it was the only answer there was and she had known it for two days and she had not said it out loud because saying it out loud is worse than knowing it.

She could not ask him not to go. She was not on that floor and she was not in that lane. Five days and two hours, said out loud in a shed four nights back to a man who was not allowed to answer her, and nobody has ever drawn up a way for a woman who has never met a man to ask him not to carry a sill.

She can still say no. She just cannot make the no take.

That had been true for five months and it was standing in a doorway at four in the morning for the first time in her life where somebody else was going to have to live in the same room as it.

She said it out loud.

“No,” Ning Yan said. “I could not ask him, and I did not ask him, and it was me.”

## What Was Written Down

The woman of fifty went out to the bank at the fifth hour and stood in the rain that had started about an hour before and did not go in again for some time.

When she came back into the room she went to the table and squared it and took the pen off the shelf.

“In the book,” she said, “there is going to be a line that says he has not come down the lane since the thirteenth. That is the whole of it and that is what I am going to write.”

“Someone should know he did the sill twice.”

“He did the sill twice,” the woman of fifty said, “and I have known that since the twenty-second day of this month and not one person has put it to me and I have not said it out loud, and that is on me and not on him.”

She wrote the line. She squared the sheet and turned it round and put it at the head of the day and closed the book on it with her palm flat on the boards for a moment, brief, the way a man touches a thing he is frightened of.

Then she shut the room and went out to the frame and did the day's work and did not come back to the low door for two hours.

## The Want

Ning Yan went down the lane to the shed at the seventh hour in the morning and sat on the boards with the bar against the wall and did not go in past the shed and did not lie down.

The rain came off the roof at the low end into a bucket that had been there for years. The cut was full to the top of its steps with the rain running brown across it toward the branch. The water smell was stronger than usual. Her breath ran four counts. Her arm hung open. Eighteen years old.

She had said it out loud to two women and a man in a grey shawl, and it had been true, and not one person in that room could do a single thing about it, and she could not take it back, and no one was ever going to come up this lane and tell her she had been wrong.

That was all that saying it bought her.

Then she got up off the boards and went and found the day book on the shelf and stood and looked at the sill line, the one she had never written on and had never once been able to do anything with.

There was one of them.

She wanted to know how many there were.
