# Four Sentences At The Gate

She came back to the gate before the second hour on the thirtieth with the bar over her left shoulder and stood nine feet short of the chain and did not touch it.

The light had not cleared off the road. The kerb held a thin line of damp along its inner edge and the posts threw long marks across the dirt. The man inside had the cup in his hand and the stool set square against the post and the chain hung in a loop off his arm. He had not moved the stool since the fourth of the month. She knew where its feet sat because she had looked at them for two days.

For two days she had done the same thing. She had come at the sixth hour in the morning and stood where people who are waiting stand, which is off to the side where a cart can pass. She had watched the chain go up and down for carts she did not know.

She had answered when spoken to and had not spoken first. She had not gone the two hundred yards to the building. She had not put her hand on the counter she had not seen. She had not asked anybody inside it for anything.

She had eaten bread in the morning and bread at night. She had drunk water out of her own skin at seven miles and had not paid a copper for it since the fourth. She had eleven coppers in the fold in the inside of the door frame four miles west and the fold was shut with a stitch she had put in herself. Her right arm hung open at her side the way it hangs. Her breath ran four counts and held there.

## The Cup

“You are early,” the man said.

“I am,” she said.

He set the cup down on the stool. He had a way of setting it that put no sound into it. His hands were short and square and the nails cut straight across. He was somewhere about fifty and he had been at that gate nine years and there was nothing hurried in him.

She looked at the cup because looking at a man’s hands tells her where she stands. The cup is plain, glazed inside, chipped at the rim. He drinks from it through the day and sets it down without sound and picks it up again. Nine years of cups. She has drunk from her own skin for seven months and eaten bread dry.

She envies him the cup for a moment, clean and simple, and then puts the envy aside. She cannot be given anything. A cup offered would stop her hand in the air. Better he never offers.

“I have been here two days,” she said. “I have watched the chain. I have not asked you for anything.”

“No,” he said. “You have not.”

She shifted the bar a finger’s width on her shoulder. The strap cut where it always cuts. The left side low behind the fourth rib held the small open place that had come open on the twenty-first and it did not hurt and it did not move and she had stopped waiting for it to do either.

“I have one question,” she said. “It is not about yesterday and it is not about tomorrow.”

“Ask it,” he said.

“Do people who stand here wait, or do they go up to the building.”

He picked the cup up again and put it down again. He looked at her for four seconds the way he looks at everybody, which is the way a man looks at a thing he is working out where it goes in an order.

He said, “Some stand.”

He said, “Some go up.”

He said, “The chain does not tell them which.”

He said, “I do not call after them either way.”

That was all. Four sentences in the ordinary voice, with pauses between them where he drank nothing and looked at the road. She counted them because she counts everything men at gates say. They were not unkind and they were not a rule recited. They told her nothing about what to do.

“All right,” she said.

She went and stood off to the side where people who are waiting stand. The sun came up over the building and put light along the lime wash and the windows went from lamp colour to day colour without anybody putting a lamp out that she could see. About a hundred people were inside it reading. Not one of them had looked up in two days.

She thought about the four sentences for an hour. Some stand. That was true; she was standing. Some go up. That was true; carts went up and men went up and a woman of sixty-one went up and down it as if the road were hers. The chain does not tell them which. That was true; the chain was a chain. She had expected a fifth sentence and there was none.

At the fourth hour a cart came down from the building with two men on the seat board and went through with the chain down and the man nodding it through. The wheels threw dust up along the kerb. She watched the dust settle. Her leg held. Her arm stayed open. Her breath stayed four counts.

“You are still here,” he said at midday, not as a question.

“I am,” she said.

“You have bread.”

“I have,” she said.

He nodded once and went back to his stool. He did not offer her water and she did not want him to offer it. She could not be given anything and he did not know that and she was not going to tell him. She drank out of her own skin when he was looking at the road.

In the afternoon Xie Changyin came down the road on foot with a folded sheet inside her coat and stopped at the gate long enough to say her name.

“Ning Yan.”

“I am here,” Ning Yan said.

“The building is there when you want it,” Xie Changyin said. “I am not asking you for today.”

“I know.”

Xie Changyin looked at her once, the way she looks at hands, and went on down the road toward the mill. She was sixty-one and she walked as if eleven miles were nothing. Ning Yan watched her go until the turn took her.

She stayed at the gate until the light went flat. She had done nothing for two days except stand and watch and keep her bread dry and her coppers shut. She had not entered. She had not signed. She had not offered a hand. She had not read a sheet to anybody.

That was the whole of it. She had wanted to be wanted for two years and now somebody wanted her and she was standing nine feet short of a chain because she did not know what her own feet would do if she went the two hundred yards.

At the sixth hour she went to ground off the road with the bar beside her and lay with her hands behind her head and looked at the building with the lamps coming up in it one by one.

“You have been quiet,” he said in the dark, in the present tense, the way he always speaks when nobody else can hear. “I am here and I am not going anywhere.”

“I know,” she said out loud, to nobody, into the dark.

“You counted his sentences.”

“I did,” she said. “Four.”

“What do you want them to mean.”

“Nothing,” she said. “They mean what they say. That is why I am still here.”

She lay there and listened to the building make no sound at all. A hundred people reading make less noise than nine men on a lifting floor. The lamps burned steady. The chain hung. The man sat on his stool with his cup cold in his hand.

She had one question left that she was allowed to ask in a room with a lamp in it, about one person, and she had not asked it. She turned that over until sleep took the edge off it.

She did not dream. She woke at the first hour with her breath at four counts and her arm open and her coppers shut and the gate still there.
