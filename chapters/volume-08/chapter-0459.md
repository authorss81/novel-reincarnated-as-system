# The Chain Across An Empty Road

He was not in the chair on the thirtieth.

Ning Yan went out to the crossing at the sixth hour with her bar over her shoulder, the way she has gone out every evening since the twenty-sixth of a month, and the chair on the step of the hut was empty and the bar was not against the wall and the gate was shut with the chain across it.

That is not wrong. A gate is shut when nobody is standing at it.

She stood four feet off the post and looked at the board. There were five lines of chalk on it and she could not read a word of any of them and she counted them because counting is what she has, and she counted them three times and it was five.

She went on down the lane and slept.

## The First

The first of a month came and she went out at the sixth hour and the chair was empty and the chain was across.

At the eighth hour a cart came up the lane from the west with bundles of paper tied with string on it, the ordinary bundle going into the building behind her, and a boy of about fifteen came out of the floor and lifted the chain and nodded the cart through and went back in without one word to anybody.

That is the part she turned over on the way back.

The chain is lifted by the man in the chair. She has seen it lifted four times an hour for as long as she has been on that lane. A boy lifted it once this morning and put it back and did not go and find out where the man was, because no one had asked him to and because that is not a boy's job and because a gate is a gate.

She stood off the near post for a quarter of an hour after that and could not make herself walk up the four steps and knock, and she has never knocked on a stranger's door in her life. She did not know what she would have said if she had.

## The Second

On the second she asked the woman of fifty, in the room at the back, with the day sheet on the table and the stove ticking.

“Has he been down the lane.”

“Not since the thirteenth,” the woman said.

“He did the sill again on the twenty-first.”

“He told you he did.”

“He told me he went north with his bar at the seventh hour,” Ning Yan said. “And he was not in the chair at the sixth on the thirtieth and he was not in it yesterday and he was not in it this morning, and a boy lifted your chain through a cart at the eighth.”

The woman of fifty stood at the table for a while and did not sit down, which is a thing she does not do in her own room.

“Then I will go out to him,” she said.

“Why not today.”

“Because I have nine men on a frame,” the woman said, “and four carts before the middle of the day, and because he has been at that gate nineteen years and has never once not been in that chair.”

That is a correct answer and it is the reason nobody went out on the first and it is the reason nobody goes out on the second, and Ning Yan went back to the shed at the end of the lane and lay on the boards and did not sleep much.

## What She Could Have Done

She went over it until the light went, and she went over it the way she goes over a sum, which is line by line and without mercy on herself.

There were nine things she could have done and she had nine days of not having done any of them.

She could have gone up four steps and knocked. She worked out what she would have said and the answer was “I am here because a chair is empty”, which means nothing to anybody who has not spent four days counting lines of chalk.

The woman of fifty would have sent a boy if she had asked her for one, and a boy would have got exactly nothing out of a man on a step.

She could have gone north up the cart track on the thirtieth and the first and the second and looked for him at the crossing four hundred yards in, and she had four fingers and a thumb and a bar and she could have done four hundred yards in twenty minutes on a body that has stopped a dressed stone the length of its own arm.

She did not do that either, and that is the one she cannot put down, because there was nothing in it that anybody would have said anything about. Nobody at a crossing two miles east of a turn has any idea a woman with a bar is standing at his gate. Nobody in a lane that ends at a gate has ever been asked to look for a man by a stranger who cannot read a board and has not been on that ground before a month ago.

She has been the strongest person in every room she has walked into for two years and it has not been worth a halfpenny to her once.

## Carrying

She carried bundles on the second and on the third because there were bundles and because there is always work at that floor for two people who will do it.

She squared edges and carried them down the frame and up and she did not spill one, and nobody thanked her, and not one person on that floor put a question to her in three hours.

At the ninth hour on the second she went out to the crossing and looked at the chair again and stood there in the dark for a while.

At the sixth on the third she went out and did not go up the four steps to the hut.

That is the whole of what she did about it on the third, and she has gone over it since and she cannot make it any other way.

She has read a hand that a hundred people in a building cannot read, and she did not use one word of it on four steps of stone at the end of a lane. She had a want for two days and she never once said it out loud where anybody could hear it, and she has learned the hard way that wanting a thing and saying it are two different things and only one of them is any use.

At the sixth hour on the third she had a bar over her shoulder that she could have carried to the far side of nine days of road and she stood in the rain with the chain in front of her and watched it.

## The Third

The woman of fifty went out at the fourth hour in the afternoon on the third, and she said she would be back before the shut.

Ning Yan was at the shed when she passed the door and stopped, and the two of them looked at each other in the middle of a lane in the flat light with the water smell coming up off the cut.

“Stay where you are,” the woman said.

“I will come with you.”

“No,” the woman said. “You are not on this floor and I am not having you stood in that hut.”

She went up the lane at her own pace with her hands behind her back, and Ning Yan stood at the shed door and watched her go and did not follow, because she had been told not to and because a woman who says no in a lane is a woman who has said something.

## Not Back

She was not back by the shut and she was not back by the ninth hour.

The books closed at the ninth hour. The woman of fifty ran that floor the way she has run it for nineteen years and she did not leave nine men on a frame at the end of a day because a stranger had not been in his chair.

Ning Yan waited at the shed until the light went flat and then she went down the lane to the crossing anyway, at the ninth hour, because there was nothing else to do with her legs and because a chain across a made road at night with the far side of it empty is not a thing a person can lie down beside.

The chain was across and the gate was shut and the chair was empty and the hut had a light in it.

A light in it.

She stood four feet off the post in the dark and looked at that light and did not go up the four steps.
