# Forty Yards Of Sill

He said it on the fifth of the month, in the dark, in a shed at the end of a lane east of the turn, and he said it before he did it.

She was on the boards with her hands behind her head and the bar along her side and the light coming grey under the door, and she said out loud, to nobody, the thing she had been carrying since the twenty-ninth of a month five months back.

“The sill is loose at the low end.”

It is the ordinary thing she thinks about. It is the last line of a day sheet she has read twice and it is not hers and it belongs to a crossing she has not been through.

“I am here,” he said in the present tense. “I am listening.”

She did not answer that. She lies on boards and says things out loud and he is in the inside of her head and he has never once pretended otherwise, and four days of a silence she held on a road in daylight is sitting in her chest like a swallowed stone.

“What are you going to do.”

He did not say that he was going to do nothing, and that was the answer.

## Five Days And Two Hours

“Something is going to go out of the ground under you tonight,” he said, “and it is not coming back.”

Her hand stopped where it was on the boards.

“I have felt that,” she said. “Once. Five months back, on a nineteenth, and the floor went cold under me and I put it down to a sill line at the south end of a yard.”

“That was me,” he said. “This is the same thing.”

She lay still. The grey under the door did not move. Her breath ran four counts and came back to four.

“How much.”

“Five days and two hours of it,” he said. “And you will not be able to tell it from a floor going cold in the wet season.”

He told her the number the way he always tells her the number, exactly, once, and did not round it, and then he was quiet for a while, the way a man is quiet after he has said a thing he has not said before.

“It is not for you,” he said.

“No.”

“It is not for me either,” he said. “I am putting it where somebody's hand already is. That is all I am going to tell you.”

She waited four counts, in and out, twice, which is how long she gives anything she has asked for.

“Whose hand.”

“I am not going to say,” he said.

She put both hands behind her head again and lay in the dark and felt the floor of the shed come up cold in one place near the door and then go back to ordinary, and it took her a moment to be sure she had felt it at all.

## She Does Not Ask Twice

She did not ask again that night and she did not ask again the next day.

That is not a rule she agreed to. She has never agreed to a rule with him about anything, because he is not a man at a counter and there is no chain and no cup and no form.

She does not ask twice because the first time he says no, the second time he would say no again. Two years of being entered by offices that never needed a bound have taught her the one thing worth knowing about a boundary, which is that it can be put down once and it holds.

She got the price. Five days and two hours, exact, before.

She did not get what it buys.

That is what she carried for two days: a number, a direction, and a sentence with the subject taken out of it. “It is not for you. It is not for me. I am putting it where somebody's hand already is.”

She had carried a number from him before, in a shed at a mill, and she had watched the floor go cold and had not been able to put it on anything, and she had decided at the time that a thing she cannot put on anything is a thing she has to hold and not a thing she has to believe.

She held it. She did not believe it and she did not let go of it.

## Why She Did Not Sleep

Here is why she did not sleep, and it was not the number.

She has felt a floor go cold exactly once. It was a board floor in a shed four miles east of a mill, at the second hour of the nineteenth of a month, and she had put it down at the time to a sill line at the south end of a yard because that was a thing she could put it on.

That was a lie she told herself and she told it because the alternative was that something in a building with two shut doors had reached out and taken something out of the ground under her feet.

She has never gone back and looked at that. She has been carrying the date of it for five months.

Now there was a second one and this time she had been told the number before it went, and this time it was out of a shed at the end of a lane and not out of a mill, and this time she was two miles from a building full of lamps and eleven days from the far side of a turnstile.

She lay on the boards and worked out that he had said “tonight” and that it had not gone at night.

It went in the morning.

That was the only thing about it she could turn over, and she turned it over for an hour and it did not improve, and then she stopped turning it over because she has learned that there are things a woman on nine days of road cannot find out by standing in the dark being clever, and the number of such things has gone up in five months and not down.

## Three Lines On A Board

On the sixth she walked out to the crossing at dusk the way she has walked out every evening since the thirtieth, and the board on the near post had four lines of chalk on it.

She stood four feet off and could not read one of them, and she counted them, and four was one more than the three that were there on the first.

On the seventh there were four. On the eighth there were five, and the fifth was the sill.

She stood there on the eighth at the fourth hour, in the flat light, with the bar butt down in the dirt and her weight on her left leg, and she watched the crossing the way she has watched a chain for two days, which is to say she watched it and asked nothing of it.

The gate stood open on its two posts. The chain hung slack in the middle of it and had not been lifted since the morning. The track going north into country she had not known was there ran flat and rutted and had carts on it four times a day.

## The Man Who Came To It

He came out of a low hut set back off the north side at about the sixth hour, and he was about fifty and thick through the middle, with a hut that was his and a chair on the step of it.

He was not a gate man the way the man with the chain at the end of the long road was a gate man. That man had a stool and a cup and nine years and a rule. This man had a hut, a chain, a board, and his hands, and he crossed the lane to the post the way a man crosses a room to a table he has been working at.

He stood in front of the board and looked at it and did not touch it.

She watched him from four feet off and he did not look at her, because there was nothing about her to look at. She is a woman with a bar and an arm hanging open, sitting on the dirt at the end of a lane, and gate men do not count women sitting down.

He read the board. He read it the way a man reads anything on a board he has read a hundred times, quickly, without going over any of it twice.

Then he read it again.

He put his thumb under the fourth line and stood with it and did not say anything for a while. Then he went into the hut and came out with a bar that had a stone on the end of it, and he shouldered it, and he went north up the track without shutting the gate behind him.

It was the seventh hour. The light had gone flat and long.

Ning Yan stood there with the bar butt in the dirt and her heart going hard once and settling.

He had not asked where the work was. He had not asked who wrote it. He had not asked whether it was going to be paid.

He had read a line of chalk on a board at a gate he stands at every day of his life, and he had gone and taken his bar with him, and that is what a board at a gate is for.

She did not follow him. She went back the mile to the shed with the smell of standing water in her clothes and five days and two hours sitting in her chest that she could not get out of it.
