# The Board Behind The Counter

She had not been in that building in seventy-five days and she found out that from a cart.

The cart came down the lane on the fourth of a month at the seventh hour with four bundles of paper under a tarpaulin and a boy on the front of it, and the boy said good morning to her as he went past, which he had not done in four months and which he had no reason to do now, and she stood four feet off the post and listened to it go.

Seventy-five days. She worked it out against the road in nine seconds and had it before the cart was out of sight.

And nobody had come for her.

That was the other half of it, and she had not put the two halves together in seventy-five days. She had been at the end of a lane with a woman of fifty and four sheets a day and a board of eighty-four marks in it, and she had not once thought about the fact that the last four things anybody on nine days of road had wanted from her had all come down the same road.

## What Seventy-Five Days Looks Like

Nothing had stopped. That was the first thing and she had to sit down on the low wall at the gate to take it.

The counter was where it had been and the hundred people were at the tables down the length of the room with a lamp on every one of them, in daylight, with the shutters up. The man with the brush was squaring sheets. The man behind the counter had a hand flat on the wood.

The young reader of twenty-six was at his desk on the far side of the counter, reading at a desk from the far side of a counter, about a person who is never in the room. It is the only thing he has ever been good at.

The whole building had gone on for seventy-five days without asking her one single question.

That is not a complaint and she did not make it one. She had held a silence in that road once already, in daylight, to a woman of sixty-one, for a full minute, and it had bought her one clear evening with everything counted true, and she had been proud of it.

This was different and she took a while to work out how.

A silence she keeps is a thing she has chosen. Seventy-five days of a room not asking is a thing that has been decided somewhere else, in a building she cannot see, by people who have never met her, about a woman they have a file on.

## The Rate Board

The paint was fresh where hands had not touched and worn where carriers brushed past, exactly as it had been on the fifth of a month four months back and on the seventeenth and at dusk on the same day, four times, and she had read it four times.

Two coppers a page.

A second rate by the difficulty of the hand.

A third rate at twice the first, for a hand nobody in the building can read.

She stood in front of it with her hands behind her back for the better part of a minute. A man repaints it when it fades. A man wipes the lamps. Somebody ties the folders. The building holds because people hold it, and it is a good counter, and a fair counter, and she has not been able to put her hand on four coppers laid on that same wood since a man counted them out without hesitation and swept them back with one practised motion.

She put her own hand flat on the wood of the counter, just her hand, empty, for two seconds, and took it away.

That is the whole of what she is at eighteen years old in the best trade on nine days of road.

## Wei Ran At The Gate

There were four men unloading at the long wall and a woman of twenty standing with a team and a tally board, and Ning Yan had not seen her since the twenty-second of a month five months back, when she had been owed eight coppers for a journey east and had not taken them.

Wei Ran saw her first and did the thing she always did, which was look at a person from the feet up for four counts and say a number.

“You have got thinner,” she said.

“I have not been paid in five months,” Ning Yan said.

“That is not what I said and you know it is not.” She put the tally board down on the wall. “I have carried east four times since the second and I have carried back twice. Two of those four were full going up. None of the four came back with anything in it, and I am telling you that as a trade and not as a favour, because you will want to hear it as a favour and it is worth nothing.”

“All right.”

“Eight coppers is on my book,” Wei Ran said. “It will be on my book when the twelfth of next month comes and it will be on my book the month after that. I have never chased it and I am not going to start now, because chasing a woman for eight coppers is a thing you do once and then you have a rate problem for a year.”

“I cannot take it.”

“I know you cannot take it. I have watched you not take it.”

She picked the tally board up and turned round to her team, and then she stopped, and she turned back, and she said it in a road with four men unloading within nine feet of her and a boy on a cart within twenty.

“Ning Yan,” she said. “That is your name and it is on that door in four counties and it is not a secret and it has not been a secret since the second of a month, and I would say it out loud in a room if there was a room.”

Then she went back to her team.

## What Wei Ran Did

Ning Yan stood at the gate and let it land.

She had said her name out loud on that ground three times in two years and every one of them had been on a road with four feet between her and the person she was saying it to. She had said it at a yard and she had said it at a door on a made road and she had said it at a gate to a man with a chain, and none of those had gone anywhere except into the person she said it to.

Wei Ran had said it in a road, out loud, at twenty, in front of four men and a boy, for no reason at all except that she was going to unload a cart and had come out of her way by six feet.

That is what it looks like. That is the whole mechanism of the ground she has been standing on for two years, and it has nothing to do with offices at all: a person says a name in a road and four men hear it, and every one of them will hear it again, and that is the entire distance between a woman nobody looks at twice and a woman the ground knows about.

And it did not require a room, or a book, or a strip going east on the first of a month, or a woman of sixty-one walking eleven miles.

It required one woman of twenty to say a name out loud at the wrong end of a cart.

## The Young Reader

She went in at the seventh hour and stayed until the lamps came up. The young reader of twenty-six did not argue about her.

She had been braced for it since the fourth hour of a month four months back, because he had been right then and being right is a thing a man of twenty-six does not stop doing.

He put his brush down flat when she came past and he said, “I am not going to ask you to read anything.”

“Then I have nothing to do here.”

“I said a thing in that room about you and the room did it anyway,” he said, “and I have had four months to work out what it cost me to be right, and the answer is nothing.”

He put his thumb on the flat of the brush.

“That is the first time in two years that being right has been worth nothing to me. So I am not going to do it again and I am not going to do it with a better reason. The next one that comes in with a hand on it goes on the stack with a string on it.”

“That is correct.”

“It is correct and I am not going to thank you for noticing,” he said, and picked his brush up, and that was all of it, and it went into a book she will never see as four sentences in an ordinary voice about a sheet she was not asked to read.

## The Woman Of Sixty-One

Xie Changyin was at the far end of the room with a sheet in front of her and her hands behind her back, and she was sixty-one and she had not walked eleven miles for anybody in seventy-five days.

She came down the room at the ninth hour when the lamps were lit and she stopped at four feet, the way she stops.

“You have not come about the offer,” she said.

“No.”

“Seventy-five days. I have not taken it back once and I am not going to, and I am not going to ask you about it again this month.” She put her head on one side by about a quarter of an inch. “You want something else.”

“Yes.”

“Then say it and I will tell you the price of it and you may go and think about the price where I cannot hear you.”

Ning Yan stood in the middle of a long low room with a lamp on every table and a hundred people reading and four hundred and seven names in a book under a man’s arm, and she had come up two miles of lane with a bar over her left shoulder to ask a woman of sixty-one a question she had built for four days on a board.

She did not say it.

She stood there for the length of a breath and a half and then she said, “The column is going east on the first and there is nothing on it.”

“There is nothing on it,” Xie Changyin said.

“That is all.”

“That is all,” Xie Changyin said, and looked at her for four seconds longer than she needed to, and did not ask the question that was sitting in the middle of the room where both of them could see it.
