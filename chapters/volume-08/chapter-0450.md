# A Man Off His Feet

It held four days, which is longer than she expected of it.

She had said nothing at all on the twenty-second, in daylight, on the road, to a woman of sixty-one with a folded sheet inside her coat, and she had gone to the gate and sat off to the side where people who are waiting stand. She expected to wake on the twenty-third into the whole of it arranged around her, a term settled and closed.

It did not come. Nothing came down the road at the sixth hour on the twenty-third, or the twenty-fourth, or the twenty-fifth. Xie Changyin did not come. The man with the cup did not look over. Two hundred yards off, the building went on being lit window by window with a hundred people in it who had not asked her for anything since a man with a brush said “you square well” and meant it.

That was the part she had not braced for. She had braced for the opposite.

The roll held four hundred and seven names and the seventh of them was there because of her mouth on the ninth of a month, and the room had gone back to its work the way a room does, and the tied folder waited on its shelf with its date on the string, and the young reader came down the length of it with his brush across his sheets and did not look at her at all.

She carried bundles to the third table and back. She squared edges. She ate bread outside with her back to the lime wash.

On the fourth day she counted what she had in the fold and did the sum she does.

Nine coppers. No bread. Two yeses she had said without meaning and one silence she had held on purpose, and a bar she cannot hold at the heavy end with a strap thinning at the shoulder point, and eleven days of eating once a day if she does not walk west.

So she walked west.

## Two Days West

She left at the sixth hour on the twenty-fourth with the bar over her left shoulder and nothing else, and she went eleven miles in two days, the way she has gone it twice. Her feet know the seventh mile where the kerb starts. Her leg stopped at a mile and a half and she stood on the grip of the bar for nine seconds with her whole weight and it came back, and she went on.

The shed was there. Four miles east of a mill whose wheel has not turned since before she came, boards on the floor, the ring with clay in it cold under a thin skin of dust, the bar standing on end by the door where she had left it eleven days back with dust a finger's width deep on the head of it.

She swept the boards. She checked the roof cord and it had held. She slept on the boards that night with her back to the door and the strip folded against her ribs where it has been for seven months and has never been read and never will be.

On the twenty-sixth she walked five miles west to the store with an empty bag and came back with two loaves in it and four coppers less than she had.

The storekeeper had not asked her anything and had not given her a different price. Two coppers a loaf, the same as every loaf she has bought there. He put them in the bag and looked at the bar on her shoulder and looked away again.

## Water Without A Copper

She drank from her own skin going back and filled it at the seventh mile from the trough without paying, because the keeper charges a copper and she had four coppers and she counted what four coppers was, and a copper is a quarter of a loaf and she was going to walk to the floor.

That is a trade she makes nine times in ten and it is not theft and nobody at the trough knows she has done it and the water is cold and it does the work.

The seventh mile came and went. The kerb ran on, stone after stone, joints filled, moss on the shaded side. The eleventh mile put the building in sight at the top of the rise, low and long and lime-washed, every window holding a lamp, and she went past it.

That is the part she had not done before. In two months of standing at the gate and walking two hundred yards to a counter she had never once gone past the near end of that building, because everything she had wanted in the world for two years had been inside it and she had gone in.

Beyond it the lane runs on east. She knew it existed because carts came up it with tied sheets in bundles and went back down it empty, and she had watched them from the gate for two months without ever once walking it.

## The Lane

The lane is metalled for a mile and then it is not. It narrows between a hedge on the north and a low wall on the south, and it smells of standing water the whole way, which means there is a cut somewhere ahead of her holding water in it.

At the end of the mile the ground drops two feet and the cut comes up on the right, twelve feet across, four feet deep at the near side and shallow at the far, with cut stone steps going down at three places and a plank laid across at two. The water at the bottom is brown and does not move.

Along the near bank runs a frame. Oak, four feet off the ground on posts, a sill of dressed stone laid the whole length of it flush with the boards, and a chain hung from a post at each end of it. Men walk loads along the frame and down the steps and up again on the far side, and the loads go over the cut and come up on the other bank on a bar and two men, four at the heavy end.

She stood in the lane and watched for a while before she went any further. She knows work measured by the length. She has carried a bar eleven miles on one arm at half and four fingers on one hand. The frame was the length of a lifting floor and shorter, and there were nine men on it, and the cut was a thing she had never worked over and would not have to work over.

Then she saw the man on the boards.

## The Man Off His Feet

He was on the boards at the south end with his back to the frame and his leg out straight in front of him, wrapped from the knee to the ankle in something grey that had been white once. He was about forty. There was a man of thirty beside him holding an umbrella over both of them although there was no rain and no sun worth the name, and they were not talking.

She had walked down the lane and she had not asked anybody for anything and nobody had asked her anything, and she had been on that ground four minutes.

It was the first time in two months.

She stopped. She stood where a person stops when she does not want to be counted in a place. Nine men on a frame, a woman coming down a lane with a bar, and one man on the boards — that is three things and the frame counts them, and a floor counts everybody who comes onto it.

She sat down on the low wall on the south side instead and put the bar butt down and drank nothing and waited to be asked.

Four men went past her without turning their heads.

At the end of an hour a man came out of a low door in the far bank and walked the length of the frame looking at the sill, and he came to the two on the boards and stood over them and said four sentences she could not hear at the distance.

Then he came along the bank toward her, past the steps, and stopped nine feet off.

“You are not on the list,” he said.

“No,” she said.

“Right,” he said. “Then you are not standing in the way either. Sit if you want to sit. The bank is not counted, the boards are.”

He went on along the bank and she sat on the low wall with the cut in front of her and the nine men going over it, and the water smell sat in her chest, and nobody came and asked her one thing for three hours.

## Three Hours

She stayed until the light went flat.

She learned a floor in three hours the way she has learned every floor for two years, by watching and by not being asked.

The loads come off the carts and go on the frame in the order they came. The sill is dressed stone and the joints are tight, and the edge is worn round in one place where something heavy has been set down twice and nobody has bothered to re-lay it. The chain is for the loads they will not lift by hand, and it is lifted four times an hour and put back hung, not dropped.

She learned the man on the boards is called Zheng and he came off the sill on the sixth day of a month and the grey wrap is a shift-sock and it is not medicine.

She learned that nobody on that floor has ever heard of the building behind her, and that the paper bundles the carts bring up the lane go into the low building and come back empty, and that the people who carry them do not know what is in them and would not ask.

That last one landed in her chest and stayed there. A place that carries paper east and sends nothing back, and the carriers do not ask what is in it. She has driven that road with a woman who has a team.

At dusk the man who had told her she was not on the list came back along the bank and looked at her bar, and at her arm hanging open, and at her right hand where four fingers and a thumb were round the strap.

“You can read,” he said. It was not a question and it was not a request. “Can you read.”

“I can read columns,” she said. “And I cannot read a hand.”

He nodded once at that, as if it were a particular and useful piece of news, and went on up the bank.

She stood up off the wall and shouldered the bar and looked back down the lane at the cut with the brown water in it and the frame along the near bank and the nine men who had not asked her for anything.

She had two loaves in a bag and five coppers and no wages, and a silence on the road behind her that had lasted four days, and she had come four minutes onto a floor and been left alone on it, and it was the first peace she has had in two months.

Then she slept in the shed at the floor with the bar beside her and the bag of bread under her coat.

She did not think about it that night. She thought about the row of joints in the sill and the place where the edge is worn round, and about a man under an umbrella in weather that did not need one, and about how many floors she has stood in that would have let a stranger sit on a wall for three hours.
