# The Date She Said Out Loud

Between the eleventh and the eighteenth she went down to the rail at the sixth hour and dug the run at the gate and read whatever was put in front of her, and she did not say one word to a living soul about a trade eleven miles east of here.

On the fourteenth a woman of sixty-one came up the four miles, looked at the boards on the threshold where her own sheet had lain for nine days and been rained on twice, and went back down them again without opening it.

On the sixteenth there were forty-three lines on the rail and she read them and got them the same twice and could take none of them.

On the seventeenth she dug the run at the gate and got a copper and there were twenty-one in the fold in the inside of the door frame.

## The Seventh Hour Of The Eighteenth

The woman came up the four miles at the seventh hour in daylight and she stood in the yard the way she stood in it every time, with her boots mud to the ankle, and she did not come into the shed.

“There is a question you are allowed to ask me,” she said. “One. About one person. I have not forgotten it and I am not going to open with it.”

“I am not going to ask it.”

“All right.” She put her hand on the door frame. “Then say the other thing and I will answer it in the ordinary voice and there is nothing in the room.”

“What other thing.”

“Whatever it is you have been not saying for seven days,” Xie Changyin said. “You have not said a word about it and I have not asked you about it and I am not going to keep standing in this yard for another month being polite about it.”

## What Was On The Boards In Those Seven Days

She got the month out on the boards on the eleventh, because she always gets a month out before anything else can be counted in it, and she wrote the days down in the ordinary hand and put a mark against the tenth, the fourteenth, the sixteenth, the seventeenth and the eighteenth.

Against the eighteenth she wrote one word and then put the pencil down flat and left it there.

On the nineteenth she came up the four miles at the fourth hour and had thirty-one lines on the rail in her head before she got to it and forty-three when she got there.

On the twentieth she dug the run at Pei’s gate and he came out at the ninth and stood at the end of it the way he stands and did not say one word, and she went back four miles and put a lump out of the box at the low end of the ring and it did not come up at all.

On the twenty-first she did not go anywhere at all.

She has never once, in two years, decided a thing and not said it out loud to him at the boards, and she had been eleven times to those boards and argued with him twice, and on the seventeenth of a month she lay there in the dark and found that she had nothing to argue with, which is a thing that has happened to her exactly once before in two years.

## Three Times Before

She has done it three times.

She said her name at a trestle on a bank at the sixth hour of a month and a man wrote it down and it was the second time that name had gone into a book on nine days of road and she could not get it out of either of them.

She said yes at a gate at the eighth hour on a step in daylight, in one word, and it went into the only book on nine days of road that shuts.

And she said no in a room with a lamp in it on the nineteenth of a month at the seventh hour, six words, with nothing owed and no force anywhere in the world in it, and a denial is an entry, and it went up the same road every answer has gone up.

Three times. Every one of them cost her something she had not known she was going to pay until after it was paid, and all three of them had been said in daylight in the ordinary voice to somebody who was being kind about it in the way that people are kind.

She had not said anything to anybody for seven days and she had not wanted to and she had also not been able to stop standing in that shed at the seventh hour.

## What She Said

“I will be at the east end of the long road on the twenty-ninth.”

The woman did not move.

“At the sixth hour,” Ning Yan said. “At the gate.”

“That is eleven days.”

“Yes.”

“Why.”

“Because I have walked there and back once and I have not gone inside the building and I am not going to stand outside a building for three days to look at it,” she said. “And because you said you would be at a gate on the twenty-ninth and I would rather be there when you are than not be there.”

Xie Changyin took her hands out of her sleeves.

“Write it down,” Ning Yan said.

“That is mine.”

“Pleasure,” she said. “Write it down, because I have been entered three times in two years and every single one of the three was because I said a thing out loud in the ordinary voice in daylight and somebody wrote it down.”

## What She Did

The woman took the folded sheet out of her coat, and she opened it, and she turned it over so that the bottom of it was up, and she took a small piece of chalk out of a fold and she wrote a place and a day and an hour on the bottom of her own sheet in the ordinary hand.

She did not read it out.

She said nothing at all while she did it, which was not hers, because she had talked without stopping for a quarter of an hour at the near end of a bench a week before and she had told Ning Yan a number twice on purpose.

Then she folded the sheet along the fold it already had and put it inside her coat and shut the front of it with her hand flat on the outside for about two seconds.

“There,” she said. “That is done and it is in a hand and it is in a book eleven miles east of here, and it is on the lowest sheet of a book and it is dated, and I am not going to tell you which sheet, and you are not going to ask me.”

## What Cannot Be Un-Agreed

She stood in the yard with the bar against the wall behind her and worked it out standing up, in about nine seconds, and she got the same answer nine times.

Nobody has to un-do anything. Nothing has failed and nothing has been refused and no book will not shut and no door is closed on a date, and a date and a place and an hour are on the bottom of a sheet in the ordinary hand of a woman of sixty-one and it is in a building with a counter in it and a lamp on every table.

There is nobody she can go to and ask to take it out.

She has asked once, in a room with forty books open on it, for a name to be taken out of two books on nine days of road, and a woman of forty-five told her in four sentences that there is no hand in that building that can do it and she was right about all four, and she went out of that room and did not sit on the bench outside because she had not been outside, she had been inside.

And there is one difference between that and this and she went and found it standing up, because it is the only thing left to find.

Her name is not in it.

A date is in it, and a place, and an hour, and a woman said a date out loud in daylight to somebody who will use it.

## Eighty-Nine Days

She gave up knowing what the fine hand says about her on the nineteenth of a month at the seventh hour in a room with a lamp in it, so that she would never be entered again, and she said six words, and she meant them, and they cost her everything she had wanted and nothing anybody could take off her.

It was the nineteenth of the month beginning 1,034. It is the eighteenth of a month beginning 1,124.

Eighty-nine days.

Nothing caused the second one.

That is the part she could not get round in a yard in the flat light of the seventh hour, with a woman of sixty-one standing three feet away with her hand still flat on the front of her coat. Nothing that she did. Nothing that anybody did to her. Nothing in either of those two rooms.

Four days of walking, and a chain, and a gate, and a hedge, and a number that came out of the ground under her own feet in a shed at two in the morning.

The second one came because a woman of sixty-one had said a thing in a doorway and a young man with a lamp had stood at a bench, and one of those two was on the nineteenth of a month three months back and the other was last week.

She was entered again and she was not asked and she had not been told and nothing was wrong.

## The Second Hour

She lay on the boards at the second hour of the eighteenth with the strip against her ribs and the bar under her right hand, and she said the thing out loud into a shed with no fire in it, because the second hour is for it and because she has never once decided a thing and not said it out loud to him at the boards.

“The twenty-ninth.”

She lay there.

“The sixth hour, at the gate, at the east end of the long road, and it is written on the bottom of her own sheet and she will be there and she does not want an answer and she does not need one.”

He was there.

He is always there and that has not changed in seven months and she had said out loud to an empty room in a shed, on the boards, at the second hour, on the eighteenth of a month, that she was going to tell him, and she had told him.
