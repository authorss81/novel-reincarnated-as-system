# One Payment In The Ordinary Hand

She went out to the crossing on the twenty-first at the sixth hour and asked a man of fifty to read a board to her.

She had thought about it for a day and a half and she had no better way of doing it, and she had got as far as the fourth foot of standing in front of the post before she opened her mouth, which is the distance she has closed on every other hard thing she knows.

He was in the chair on the step with his bar against the wall. He looked at her for a while. He had been in that chair on the sixth, the seventh, the eighth and the ninth and he had not spoken to her on any of them and he did not speak now.

“I cannot read it,” she said.

“All right,” he said.

That was all he said for a moment, and then he got up off the step, because he is a man who does a thing when a thing needs doing, and came over to the post and stood in front of the board with his shoulder against it.

## The Board Read Aloud

He read it the way he had read it on the eighth. Quickly, without going over any of it twice.

“Two carts off the country road before the middle, by the load. Water in the ditch, cut side, the branch end, fill it. Chain stiff on the gate, oil it. Sill at the crossing, forty yards by the length, the low end, take it up and lay it true.”

Then he stopped.

He said, “That is the whole of it.”

“There were five lines on it the other day,” Ning Yan said.

“There are five lines on it,” he said. “Four of them I have read. The fifth is under my thumb.”

She did not say anything about that. He had put his thumb up over the fifth line before he started and she had watched him do it.

“Forty yards of sill,” she said. “Is that new.”

“The sill is new,” he said. “The crossing is thirty years old by the look of the stone. A sill goes forty years and then somebody has to do it again.”

“Have you had one done before.”

“Not that one. The one behind it, when I came.”

## The Fifth Line

He took his thumb off and showed her the bottom line for about two seconds and put his thumb back on it.

She got six words of it, which was six more than she had.

“Twenty-one,” he said, when she asked what day it was for. “It goes today. That is why it is chalked today and not the day before.”

She stood and looked at the board and could not read a word of it and now knew that the man who could had carried the sill off this same gate twice in twelve days, in the same words, off the same painted board, and had not thought about it at all.

“You do not think it is strange,” she said.

“No,” he said.

## Why He Went

He took the bar off the wall at the seventh hour and shouldered it and went north up the track without shutting the gate, the way he had gone on the eighth.

She did not go with him. She had held four wedges in the dark on the ninth and she knew now what a wedge was for, and she also knew that a man going to do a forty-yard sill at the end of a working day does not want a woman he has met four times following him up a cart track to watch, and that is not modesty. That is the shape of the trade.

She sat down at the foot of the near post with the bar across her knees and the chain in front of her and she waited, and it came on to rain about an hour later, thin, from the west, and she got up and stood under the trackside hedge with her back to the gate.

He came back at the second hour in the dark with the bar over his shoulder and went past her and lifted the chain and shut the gate and went into his hut, and the light went out.

He had been out four hours.

## What The Floor Paid

She asked the woman of fifty in the room at the back on the twenty-third, with the day sheet on the table and the books on the shelf and the stove ticking.

“Forty yards of sill at the crossing,” she said. “When is it paid.”

“His rate is at the head of the book,” the woman said. “Sill is by the length and a yard is what it is a yard.”

“How much.”

“He has been at the gate nineteen years,” the woman said, “and his rate has gone up four times and it is in the book in his hand and I could tell you, and I am not going to tell you, because if I tell you what a man at the crossing is paid you will be back in here every ninth hour with a question, and you are not on this floor and you do not work here.”

“That is fair,” Ning Yan said.

“It is,” the woman said. “You are welcome at the sixth hour and the ninth hour and you may read every book on that shelf and you may not have the rate.”

“What I want to know is one thing and it is a yes or a no,” Ning Yan said. “Is the sill paid once or twice.”

The woman of fifty stood at the far side of the table for a while and looked at the line she had written.

“He came down the lane on the thirteenth and told me he had done a sill, and I wrote it against the thirteenth, and I paid him for one sill, and that is the whole of what has happened,” she said. “He has not been down this lane since and I do not know what he has been doing on the days when a board says go.”

She put her hand flat on the day sheet for a moment.

“You are asking because there are two dates in the left column,” she said.

“Yes,” Ning Yan said.

“I did not write the second one,” the woman said. “I have not written a date in that column in four years and neither has anybody in this building and you can look at the hand if you want to, which you cannot, and that is the first honest thing either of us has said this morning.”

## The Gate Man

She went back out to the crossing on the twenty-fourth and the twenty-fifth and he was in the chair both times and he let her stand there, which was more than most men do.

On the second afternoon she asked him how long he had been at the gate.

“Nineteen,” he said. “Eleven before that at the pits on the water, and nine before that on a roof.”

“You have done three trades.”

“I have done four,” he said. “And I have been bad at two of them.”

## His Mouth, In Five Pieces

She sat down against the post at some point without meaning to, because the ground was there and she was tired, and she was the only woman in this ground who is eighteen and she has learned to put her weight where it stops the leg.

“Is it a good trade.”

“It is a gate,” he said.

She waited.

“I have a wife,” he said. “She does not come to the gate. She has not been past it in four years and that is her choice and I am not going to talk you into it.”

Nobody had to talk her into anything. She sat at the foot of a post and listened.

“A yard of sill is a yard of sill,” he said. “That is my rate and it has been the same since I was forty-one and it goes in the book at the head of the shelf in a hand that is not mine.”

She did not say that she cannot read the hand. He knows she cannot. He knew it on the twenty-first.

“I have done that sill twice in twelve days,” he said, “and it has been paid for once, and that is because I have been down this lane once.”

He picked up his cup and looked at it.

“Nobody has come to take it back,” he said.

“Take what back.”

“The other one,” he said. “I have not asked for it and I am not going to ask for it. If it comes it comes and if it does not come then I did it twice for nothing and I will do it twice again, because that is what the board says and I have never in nineteen years written anything on a board that was not there.”

She sat with that for a while.

That is a man who is correct. A man with a rate in a book and a wife who does not come to the gate and eleven years on a roof and four trades and a chain he lifts four times an hour.

He is not doing her a favour and he does not know there is a question in it, and he will not ask her a single question. She cannot be given anything and cannot be paid, and she has been out in the rain at the foot of his post for two afternoons because there is nowhere else on this ground she can stand.

The low place behind her fourth rib was quiet. Her breath ran four counts. Her arm hung open at her side with four fingers and a thumb on it.

She thought about two years of columns she has read for nothing, and a woman of forty-five who read one of them back to her and stopped a whole set on it, and a man of fifty-one who put a heading over a second column because she read his sheet out loud and stopped him in the middle of it.

Nine days of road west. That is what two years of reading comes to. That is all a woman who reads a fine hand has ever been offered by anybody who has ever heard of it.

A woman of fifty in a nine-foot room will not tell a stranger what a man at a gate is paid, and the woman of fifty is right, and she said so out loud, and then she went and put the day sheet on the shelf with the rest of them.
