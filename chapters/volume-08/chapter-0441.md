# Eleven Miles On Foot

She walked it west on the twenty-second because her bread was gone and her coppers were down to ten and the shed needed its roof tied before the rains.

She did it in three days. Eleven miles and three days on foot, the way the outline of her life has always gone, slow, with stops. On the first day she went four miles to the mill with the bar over her left shoulder and tied the roof cord and slept on the boards with the ring cold under her back.

On the second she walked west five miles for bread, four loaves for eight coppers, which left her two, and carried them back. On the third she walked east again with bread for two days in her coat.

## The Shed

The shed needed her. The roof cord had worked loose in wind, the ring of clay sat cold on the boards, the bar standing on end by the door had gathered dust. She tied the cord with her left hand and her teeth, her right hung open. She swept the boards. She checked the fold in the inside of the door frame, counted two coppers by touch, stitched the fold shut again with a stitch she has used since she was eleven.

She slept on the boards that night with the strip folded against her ribs and the paper that says Foundation the first stage against the other side of them. The Clay Plate rested worn against her ribs and she did not describe it even to herself. Short breath four counts. Arm open. Eight of eleven holds. The small place low behind her fourth rib quiet.

In the morning she stood in the yard and looked at the mill whose wheel has not turned since before she came. Four miles east the road runs to kerb and gate and building. Four miles west the store holds bread. She stands between them with a bar she cannot hold at the heavy end and does the arithmetic every morning: bread, water, floor, coppers. It comes out the same way.

She can stay. She can walk. She cannot be given anything, so no material arrives. She cannot be paid, so no coin comes for reading. The furnace will not light itself, so every stage needs hands she has to ask for. She asked once. She got Xie Changyin for two hours of six days. That debt sits in her chest beside the rest.

She shouldered the bar and walked west for bread.

It was on the second day, coming back from the store with the loaves wrapped, that she met Wei Ran on the made road with a team and a cart.

Wei Ran was twenty, mortal-born, with one team and a trade and a rate and an opinion, and nothing else on this road. The team stood patient in the road. The cart held bundled paper tied with string.

“Ning Yan,” Wei Ran said. “You walk.”

“I do,” Ning Yan said.

“The fare east is eight coppers for a person with no load,” Wei Ran said. “That is the rate and it has not changed. Your eight are on my book and your hand would not take them. So you have a credit against a fare and not a coin, and I am saying the number out loud so neither of us pretends otherwise.”

“All right,” Ning Yan said.

“I will take you east for the eight and not for the two in your fold,” Wei Ran said. “That is the trade. The team walks anyway. You would ride the seat board and save your leg the mile and a half where it stops.”

Ning Yan shifted the loaves in her arms. Her leg had stopped that morning at the mile and a half and she had stood on the grip of the bar for nine seconds to get it back. Her arm hung open. Her breath ran four counts.

“No,” she said. “I am walking it.”

Wei Ran nodded as if she had expected it. “Then my opinion, since you have never refused it. That building is a good trade and a genuine competence. The rate is two coppers a page and it is paid. The lamps are cleaned. The sheets are squared. Nobody there will cheat you. And it will take you all the same.”

“How do you know.”

“I drive the road,” Wei Ran said. “I have taken strips east for three months and come back with nothing, and I was paid for the going and not for the coming back. A place that keeps strips and sends nothing back is a place that keeps people the same way. That is not a judgement. It is a rate and a road.”

Ning Yan stood in the road with the bread in her arms and felt love for the plainness of it, which was not romance and had nothing of romance in it. A trade, a team, a rate and an opinion. Eight coppers said out loud.

“Thank you,” she said.

“Eat before the kerb,” Wei Ran said, and clucked to the team and went on west.

## The Kerb Mile

She ate before the kerb, standing off the road with the loaves in her lap, chewing slow. The team’s dust settled. The road ran on, kerb stones flush, joints filled. She checked her leg the way she checks everything, bending it twice, standing her weight on it. It held. It would stop at a mile and a half if she pushed. She would stand on the bar grip nine seconds when it does. That is the arrangement and not an accident in it.

She walked the kerb mile with the bread in her coat and the bar over her shoulder. The sun stood high. Water at seven miles cost her a copper, drunk from her own skin with the carter watching the road. She paid it without haggling. She has two coppers and bread for two days and a credit of eight on another woman’s book that her hand will not take.

Neither woman pretends otherwise. Neither says one word about what it means that the balance of that fare can never be paid in coin.

At the seventh mile she stopped and put her left hand flat on a kerb stone, cool through the palm, and thought about Wei Ran’s opinion, given plain because she has never refused it. A good trade and a genuine competence. Lamps cleaned. Sheets squared. Nobody will cheat her.

And it will take her all the same. That is not a judgement. It is a rate and a road. She trusts competence over people, which is her flaw, and here is competence warning her off itself, which is a new thing.

She reached the gate at the fourth hour on the twenty-sixth with dust to the knees and her coppers shut and her credit on another woman’s book and her feet sore in a way she liked, because sore was a thing she understood and a thing that would be back tomorrow if she went and asked for it. Eleven miles in three days, for bread and a roof cord, and every one of the miles paid for in something she can count.

The man with the chain nodded her through. The building stood lit. The tied sheet waited on the counter. She had walked eleven miles in three days to buy bread and tie a roof cord, and the walk had given her the one thing the building could not give her: a number said out loud by a woman with a team who wanted nothing from her but a fare she could never pay in coin.

That night she lay at the gate with her hands behind her head and her bread dry beside her.

“You walked when you could have ridden,” he said. “I am watching you and I am proud of the walk.”

“I know,” she said. “It cost me nothing but feet. That is the cheapest thing I have.”
