# She Says His Name Once

She went back to the room at the back on the seventeenth and the eighteenth and the nineteenth and the twentieth, and she read the sheet at the sixth hour and gave it back at the ninth, and nothing on it changed.

That was the honest result and she took it standing up.

She had spent four days with two dates on one line and had hoped — she did not use the word hope, she used the sort of hoping a person does when the sum does not come out the first time — that there would be a thirteenth, or a ninth, or something moving on the sheet in the four days while she slept on it.

Nothing moved. The floor does not write down things nobody has told it.

## The Eleven Lines Again

She went and got the four back books again on the seventeenth and put them on the bench and read all of them both ways and got the same answer she had got the first time, which is that it is not an answer.

She tried it by work. She copied out the twelve lines onto the back of a day sheet with no figures on it — the floor's paper, which she was about to put back in its gap and which she did not write on, because she had found the line the way she had found it at the lifting floor, by reading it out loud to a man, and she was not going to do it a third time in a month in a building she did not work in.

She tried it by numbers. The gap on the eleven was a day, and two days, and two days, and three, and three, and four, and four, and six, and one, and two, and — on the twelfth — four.

She tried it by what a floor knows. A floor knows the day a man came down the lane. A floor does not know the day he came off a frame, or the day he finished a sill in the last of the light with his leg favouring, or the day a load came back heavier than it went.

That is the ordinary shape of it and it was true of all twelve, and if she wrote it out for the woman of fifty it would have been the finest explanation of a ruled column anybody on nine days of road has ever given.

And it explained nothing at all.

Because a floor that ruled a column nineteen years ago and filled it in eleven times without knowing who filled it in is not a floor that has a reason.

## The Seventeenth And The Eighteenth

She had read them twice already and the sum did not come out, so on the second morning she did something she has never done on nine days of road in two years.

She sorted them.

She took the four books of four years and the two behind them and laid out every line that had anything in the left-hand column in a row on the bench in order of date.

There were thirteen of them, and she wrote out beside each one what the work had been and how long it had taken and who had done it. She can put a name to a length of chain and a bed at the low end and a cart that came after the shut, because she has been on that floor four days and she has watched nine men go over a cut.

Twelve of the thirteen she could account for.

The eleventh was a chain. That one was done on the third of a month and the floor heard about it on the fourth, and the chain on that gate had been stiff since the day before she came down the lane, which she had seen with her own eyes off the board.

The seventh was a load. The eighth was a load. A man came off the frame with a leg on the tenth, and water stood in a ditch on the fifth.

Every one of them was ordinary work, and every one of them was work that nobody on that floor had any way of knowing about on the day it was done.

That is what a ruling is. She came to it on the evening of the seventeenth with thirteen lines spread out on a bench in a nine-foot room.

A column gets ruled for a thing somebody expects to have to write in it, and it stays ruled, and it stays empty, and once in a while somebody has a day he was not ready for and something arrives in the column that was made for a different kind of day.

The first time it happened it would have been the man who ruled the column, four years ago, on a floor at the end of a lane, and he would have looked at it and gone and said nothing, and then the column would have had a second one in it a year later, and then a third.

That is how a column fills up without anybody writing a heading over it.

She looked at the bench for a long time.

And it explained the column. It did not explain the fourth day.

## The Thing She Has Never Had

Here is the part she came to on the second day and had to lie still with.

She has been in a document for two years.

Entered as good at the seventeenth in one year. Entered again in the next for materials. Copied up out of the office that keeps it, in a hand she has never seen, by somebody she will never meet. And a name written into a book in the ordinary hand at the far end of a long room because she opened her mouth in it.

She has never once in twenty years of reading columns stood on a page.

That is what this stretch of days has given her and it arrived sideways, on a board floor at the end of a lane east of the turn, on a knee, with her face four inches off the paper.

She was on that cart track on the ninth. She held four wedges in a ring for two hours in the dark. She watched a man go down into a ditch and come out of it. She walked the mile behind him and watched him go past the shed and out past the crossing and away west, and she thought it was ordinary that a man finishes a sill and goes home.

Somebody wrote the ninth into a ruled column four days before the woman of fifty knew there was a sill.

She could not read who. She could not read a word of the hand. She could read that a cell that has been empty for nineteen years on this floor had a day in it, and the day was hers, and it had been there four days.

And she had said “it comes out of the ground under you” to a woman of sixty-one on a road in daylight, and the ground keeps a book, and the book had her day on it before it had anybody's.

## The Boards

She got in on the twentieth at the ninth hour and sat on the boards with the lamp out because there was no lamp, in the dark, in a shed at the end of a lane with a bar against the wall and a ring with clay in it under the boards.

She said his name out loud.

It is the only name she has ever said out loud into an empty room that nobody in the room answered for seven years, and she has never said it with any hope attached to it, and she said it this time the way you put a hand down on a thing to see whether it is warm.

“Yuan.”

## What Came Back

Something came back. It came back in a quarter of a second, and it was the first time in as long as she can remember.

“I know,” he said in the present tense.

She lay on the boards with her arms folded up and her heart going hard once and settling, and she waited four counts in and four counts out, twice, which is how long she gives anything she has asked for.

“What do you know.”

He did not tell her.

She lay there in the dark for a while with that and understood, the way she understands every cost she has ever paid, that she had walked thirteen miles round to ask a question and had got the same thing back that she gets from a gate, which is a man who is not going to say what he has decided.

He said it out of the quiet, after a while, without being asked a second time, and he said it the way a man says a thing he has checked and does not expect to be thanked for.

“You have got to the column,” he said. “That is further than anybody has got.”

## The Thing He Said That Was Worse

She lay there and let that land.

Nobody on nine days of road has said a good word to her in two years and nobody had ever had occasion to. Rao read her column back to her and stopped the whole set on it. Geng put a heading over a second column and never once mentioned it again. The man behind a counter wrote nine words on a strip and swept four coppers back under the wood without reproach.

And a voice out of the inside of her own head has just told her that she is the furthest anyone has ever got, in his own voice, in the present tense, as a fact.

She felt it go through her the way the drain had gone through the floor five months back.

He is proud of her. He is proud of her for getting to the column, and there is no way on this earth to tell the difference between that and a man watching a thing he started work on come out right, and she has never in her life had to make that distinction and she did not know she could.

“I did not ask you that,” she said.

“No,” he said. “I did not wait to be asked. That is what it is and I would rather you had it from me than work it out at three in the morning some month from now and be angry about it.”

## What She Said Back

She said the whole of it out loud to an empty shed on the twentieth of a month, which she has never done about anything, and she said it in the ordinary voice, sitting up on the boards with her hands on her knees.

“A column I ruled nineteen years ago has been filled in twelve times in four years. Eleven of them are between one day and six days and the twelfth is four days and the twelfth is mine.

I cannot read the hand. I have never been able to and I gave that up on purpose and I am not going to take it back. So I have two dates and a floor and a man at a gate, and no way on this earth of telling any of it from a man who forgot to come down a lane for four days.”

She stopped.

He said nothing at all.

That was all the answer she got from either of them, and she lay back down on the boards and did not put her hands anywhere in particular.

## The Want

By the twentieth she wanted one word off the board at the crossing.

That is the whole of what she wanted on the twentieth of a month at the end of a day in a shed four miles east of a mill whose wheel has not turned since before she came, and it is a small want, and she has wanted much larger things and had them refused.

She wanted one word off a painted board on a post at a gate, and there are perhaps six people within two days of that gate who could put it into her hand, and three of them would not do it and two of them would not talk to her and one of them has never seen her face.

She has been on nine days of road for two years as the woman in the room with a lamp in it. Nobody on this ground has handed her a board.

She lay there a long time with it, and the lane smelled of standing water through the wall, and the low place behind her fourth rib stayed quiet.
