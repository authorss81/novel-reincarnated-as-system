# The Signing Hand

Six days in at the quarry the ice found the place where the water ran off the cut and put it across the grade, and the grade is where the sledges go, and on the seventh day the sledge with no wheel on the near side came down three loads of core-stone on a sheet of ice about a foot wide.

The first one went through.

The second one she saw coming, in the half-second before, the way she had seen a load roll for nine months — the near runner climbing and the far runner still tracking and the whole of eight hundredweight going over on the high side.

She had a bar under it before it went and it did not go.

The third one came down behind the hoist and she did not see it at all, and she heard Bo shout, and she went for the handle.

## Eleven Seconds

A sledge handle is ash and it is nine hours old by the fourth hour of the afternoon and it is wet and it has grit worked into the grain and it is the handle of a thing that a man pushes down a grade with his shoulder, which is the only reason nobody wears gloves at it.

She got her left hand round it and she took the weight of the near side to lift it off a man who was on his knees on the ice with one leg under the runner, and the stone came off him and went onto the flat and stayed there.

Then she put the sledge down and stood up and it was about four seconds later that she noticed her hand.

Not the pain. The pain was late and it was dull and it came up out of the forearm about a quarter of an hour afterwards the way the cold does.

The skin was gone from the heel of her left hand at the base of the thumb, in one strip, about the width of two fingers, and it had gone with the first layer under it, and the underneath was the colour of the inside of a lip.

## What She Did Not Do

She did not stop.

She did not go to the foreman and she did not go to the quarter and she did not take a day, and the reason she did not go to the foreman is that a foreman who has eleven men will hear about a hand and will find a way to put a woman with a hand in a lighter place, and the lighter place pays eleven coppers.

So she went down the grade and put in four more loads before the light went, and she was slower, and nobody said anything about it, and on the sixth day after that she took six loads and on the seventh day she took five.

## A Third Less

It is not pain that takes work away. She had carried loads on that shoulder for eleven months with the right arm open a third of the time and had lost about a third of what a two-armed load pays, and she had built a whole working day out of arranging that.

The hand took a different third.

The heel of the hand is what turns. It is the joint under the thumb that brings the fingers round and down and puts a stroke on a page or a grip on a handle.

What she had under it on the seventh day was a wound that closed over the top in nine days and stayed closed over the bottom for a month, and a hand that closes over the bottom is a hand that cannot turn.

She could hold.

She could hold a sledge handle for nine hours and she could hold a bar behind her shoulder for nine hours and she could get a stone off a grade.

What she could not do was the small tight rotation at the end of a stroke that ends one and starts the next without a pause in it. A pause in it is not a smaller number. It is a different number, and the different number was about a third, and eighteen coppers a load goes to twelve and does not go back.

She was paid at twelve for five weeks.

## The Pace That Is Not There

What would have saved her nine seconds was a thing she had built out of five weeks on an escort road in the summer, when a train on it taught her the point at which a wheel begins to lean a quarter of a mile out.

It was gone. She had spent it in the summer at the fourth bridge, out of her own foundation, told before it was spent, and what had gone was not the knowledge.

It was the order.

She still knew what a leaning sledge looks like. She knew where the third one was going in the half-second before the ice took it, in the ordinary way a woman who has carried stone for a year knows things, and there was nothing at all to be said between a thing she knew and a thing that could have told her sooner.

She turned the second one with a bar and her left hand was not on the bar.

It had been on the bar all morning.

That was the sentence she sat with for a week afterwards, and there was nobody in the world she could say it to, because saying it would have meant saying that a voice in her head had once been able to buy her a sequence out of her own foundation, and she was not going to give that to a quarry.

## The Quarters

The crew came in at the ninth hour and she was at the bottom of the third flight with her left hand wrapped in a strip off the lining of an old shift, and nine men looked at it, and one of them said the thing that all of them were thinking.

“That is frost.”

“It is not frost,” said Ning Yan.

“It is not frost.”

“No,” she said, “and I am not going to be careful about it in front of you, so it is a handle that has been wet for nine hours and a thing that came down a grade. That is all it is and I will have it in a week.”

Which was true, and which was also, she understood about nine minutes later on the third flight, the last thing she was going to say to nine men.

It was not frost because frost is what happens to a body that has stopped being able to make heat, and hers had not stopped. It was not frost because a hand does not come off on a sledge handle in the middle of an afternoon with three quarters of a load still behind her.

It was not frost because she could not say the true thing about it, and the true thing was that she had been faster than any woman with one arm has any business being at that quarry for nine hours because a voice in her head had once had a look and had told her the thing she was going to need, and that look does not come back.

## Writing

She went at it that night on the bench in the loft with a slate, because a slate does not need a wrist to turn, only a wrist to rest on.

It took her until the second hour.

The county hour is in person and in her own hand, and there is no version of that which anybody else may do for her, and there is no version of that which a clerk will accept from a member who cannot hold a brush in the ordinary way.

She could make the figures. That had never been the problem; she could hold a brush at a fixed angle and pull down and make a figure, and she had been making them in her left hand since the first month of the year.

What she could not do was the turn at the end, and without the turn every figure runs into the one after it and about one in nine comes out wrong, and one in nine wrong in a column of hours is a column with a hole in it.

So she wrote them with the flat of the finger.

Not held. Not gripped. The brush laid into the flat of the second finger past the knuckle, held there by the two fingers above it, and every figure finished by a drag out to the right instead of a turn in.

It is slower. It is legible. It was hers.

It took her until the second hour of the night to find it and it took her about nine days to be able to do it without stopping, and in the nine days she lost two hours and four loads, and afterwards she did a county hour in about eleven minutes longer than she used to and nobody in nine counties has ever known that.

## The Ugly Luck of It

The thing she could not put down, in the days after, was how lucky it was.

There is one hand that signs and one hand that carries and she had spent four months of a year building her whole life around the fact that the hand that carries is the one that is open a third of the time and the hand that signs is the one that has never failed her since the first month of the year.

And now the one that signs had been taken off a sledge by four hundredweight of stone, and the one that carries was open, and could not be closed about a page at all.

A county does not want a hand to be beautiful. A county wants a hand to be hers.

She had gone to a clerk at a counter in the spring and had said *I am the licensed one and my hand is my own* and had put four hundred hours of a tithe on a piece of paper in front of him, and she had never once in her life been in an office where somebody looked at her writing and did not ask to have it done properly.

Nobody in nine counties was going to ask her to have it done properly.

“Nobody is going to say anything about your hand,” said Yuan.

“I know.”

“You were going to be frightened and instead you have got a way of writing that will last you the winter.”

“I know that as well,” said Ning Yan. “Say the other thing.”

“I have not said anything.”

“You have not said it and you have not been going to say it,” she said, “and I have been listening to you not say it for about nine days, and it is the loudest thing anybody has done to me this year.”

He did not answer that.

He had been watching a right arm go for nine days and a left hand come off at the heel and he had said four words about it in total, all of them about the schedule, and he was not going to say the other thing either, because the other thing was that he had been in her head since the first year and had been worth four and a half weeks and one sequence and had not been worth this.
