# Loaded and Watched

She was four hours short and there were about sixteen days of road left in the season.

She did it on the lane at the ninth night hour of the hundred and fourth day with the slate frame on the boards in front of her and a piece of charcoal worn flat, because she had stopped using chalk on a slate in the cold and had started using a burnt stick, which made it worse and which she had decided not to improve on.

Fifty-eight hours, asked, in her own left hand, over a man who had put his own number under hers and signed it without looking up, in the middle of the season, in the spring.

Fifty-four.

The gap was not the road.

The gap was the seven weeks after the thirty-first that had gone into a lane behind a well at twelve coppers a load, and into a county hour four times a week, and into four miles twice a day on cleared days, and into the ordinary waste of a body that does not stop being tired at the end of a shift.

Forty-one days of the irregular certification had gone up four miles to a man with a stamp at the second hour. What had not gone up was everything she had worked and had not written in the frame, because she had stopped writing in the frame when the road started, because the road started at the ninth hour and the frame was written at the ninth night hour and on a road day those were four hours apart and one of them was in the dark.

A gap is a gap. She had heard a man who does not hurry say that in a room with four tables and had written it in a frame and had not needed it explained since.

“Four hours,” said Yuan.

“Four hours.”

“You could write them down tonight and you would be level and it would be true for about nine seconds.”

“No,” said Ning Yan. “A column is a record of where I was. It is not a shape I put a number into and the number goes up the stair to a man who is copying my hours into a report for the rest of his life.”

She put the burnt stick down on the boards.

“If I write four hours tonight that I did not work, then in about five months there is a number in a report that does not match a body, and there is no way to explain that to a man with a fair hand who cannot lie.”

“You are going to work four hours.”

“I am going to work four hours,” said Ning Yan, “and I am going to do it on the road because that is where they are, and the season's road shuts when the weather shuts and not before, and I have got about sixteen days.”

## What She Was Building

The stage had been building itself out of load and watching since the eighth week and she had not asked it to and she had not been able to see it clearly until she was sitting on a lane in the cold doing a subtraction.

It was not what she had built the last one out of.

The last one had been built on knowing. Six weeks at a water channel, six loads a day, and the thing that had gone into her was a feel for a load arriving — the point at which a stone stops being carried and starts being set down, and which you can only get if you have set down enough of them.

This one was being built on the opposite thing. She had a hole where the road had been and she had been caught by a man with a bar, and every day on the road she was putting something into the hole, and what was going into it was not foreknowledge. It was the thing that came after being wrong.

She worked that out sitting on a wall at the turn at the willows with a burnt stick in her hand.

“That is a different shape,” she said out loud, on a road, to nobody, in the ordinary voice.

“Different from what.”

“Different from what I had at six. That one told me where a wheel was going before it arrived. This one is built out of nearly not seeing it. It goes the other way round. It runs forward and then it gets it wrong and then it goes back and looks.”

“That is not nothing,” said Yuan.

“It is not the same thing.”

“No,” said Yuan. “It is not the same thing and it is not worse than nothing, because you have a hole in a guard's road and nine men on it, and the thing that is going to be built at seven is going to be built out of that, and when it arrives it will be a different animal.”

She put the burnt stick down.

“Say the whole thing.”

“I am not going to,” said Yuan.

“Say the whole thing, because I want to hear whether you can hear it.”

He was quiet for the length of about four counts, which for him was a long time.

“The thing you lost on the road is gone,” said Yuan. “The thing you are building now is a new thing that uses the old body's memory and does not have the old shape, and I cannot tell you from inside your skin whether it is going to arrive, because it is not the same sequence, and the only way I have ever found out anything about a sequence is whether it is there when it is needed.”

“That is not an answer.”

“It is the most honest thing I have said since the thaw and you asked for it.”

## Feng Did Not Ask

There was a thing she noticed on the fourth day of that week and let stand for nine days before she said anything about it to herself, in a loft, in the dark.

He had not asked her a question.

She had been up the tower four times since the appointment. Twice with a slate frame for a stamp and twice with nothing, and on all four occasions Feng Lushi had been on the bench with the lamp behind him and the copying going and he had said good evening, or he had said nothing at all and nodded, and at no point in four visits had he asked her where she had been that day or what she had worked or how the arm was.

In fourteen months he had asked her two questions and one of them had been about her intention and it had been the wrong one.

Now he had a fair hand and a chair with a fire in it and an office above this county that he was going to write to for the rest of his life, and he had stopped asking her anything at all.

That was not tact. She knew what tact was in this building; she had watched it for a year from a bed and she could pick it out at ninety feet.

That was a Listener keeping his hands where they could be written down.

“You do not think that is kind,” said Yuan.

“No.”

“He has been kind.”

“He has been careful,” said Ning Yan, “and careful got him a chair with a fire in it and careful is going to be in a report for the rest of his life, and I would rather be careful than kind this winter and he knows it, and I do not think he knows that I know it.”

She lay still.

“He is not doing it for the report,” said Yuan.

“No,” said Ning Yan. “That is why it is worse. If he were doing it for the report I could be angry at him properly and instead I have got a man of sixty-two who has stopped asking me how I am, because he worked it out on his own, and I did not ask him to and I did not notice it for nine days.”

## The Fever

The fever came in on the eleventh night of that period and she noticed it on the road, at the third hour, halfway down the grade, and she finished the descent.

It took her three days. She was on the road again on the fourth, at a quarter of the rate, and she wrote it in the frame that night with the hours it had actually cost her, which was one train and a half and a great deal of the day.

That was the correct thing and she did it the ordinary way, which meant she did it with a bar in her left hand and eight carts behind her and an old grey horse that had made its decision, and she got to the bottom and put the bar in the rack and told Tan the wheel numbers for the next train in a voice that came out exactly as a voice comes out.

At the weigh-station she did not get on the up train.

She sat down on the low wall of the weigh-house at the fourth hour because she had been walking for an hour and a half and the wall was there, and the fever came up out of her the way the stage had come up out of her at the water channel, without her deciding anything about the hour.

Her back was against the wall and the light on the cut was going white and she could not make her right hand do the thing she asked of it and the left one was on the stone.

Four hours short.

“Can you walk four miles.”

“I can walk four miles.”

“You can walk four miles and you will be at the top of a tower stair inside two hours of the worst of it, and you do not have a surety and you do not have anyone in this county who can stand for you.”

“I have a physician’s hour on a county sheet,” said Ning Yan, “and a stamp at a Hall with no roof, and a man at a board behind a well who will chalk me on tomorrow, and I will be at the top of that stair tonight whether there is a person in it or not.”

She got up off the wall.

It took her two goes.

She did the four miles. She did them at the pace of about a mile an hour and a quarter, which is the pace of a woman with a fever and one arm and a crate she has forgotten she is carrying, and she came up the Lowwater road in the dark with the cart track under her boots and did not once stop.

Tan came up behind her about half a mile from the turn and caught up at the willows and did not say anything about it and walked at her pace for the rest of the way, and at the bottom of the tower stair he said:

“You are ill.”

“I am.”

“You should not have come up this hill.”

“No.”

“I am going to tell the contractor you were ill,” said Tan, “because he will put you at the shed and the shed is worse than the road and you are not going to the shed for four hours.”

“Tell him what you like,” said Ning Yan. “I will be there at the ninth hour.”

She went up four flights with her hand on the rail and got to the loft and lay on the mat, and the lamp in the cage on the fourth landing was burning, and the copying room door was shut, and she was in the bed with a fever at about the fourth night hour of a day in the last third of the season with four hours short on a column and about sixteen days of road left in the year.
