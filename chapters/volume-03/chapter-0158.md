# The Weight Ticket

The Hall's copyist came up the road on the third morning with a slate and a bad hand and eleven years of writing names out of a form, and she had known he was coming since the week before and had been dreading it the way she used to dread the quarterly.

He wanted the furnace schedule with the annex on it.

“A winter return,” he said. “Not the quarter’s. The winter one goes up on the day and comes back at the reading, and there is more on it this year than there was at the thaw.”

“How much more.”

“The schedule. The annex. The irregular, twice.” He turned the slate over. “And it goes with a member’s sheet, and I am not going to ask to see it because it is not mine and it does not go through my hands, and I am telling you it goes with you because you have been listening to me for a year and I would rather you were not surprised in the spring.”

“Thank you,” said Ning Yan.

“Do not thank me, I have not done anything.” He wrote her name in the wrong order on the slate, the way he had written it every time since he came to the table. “Where are the hours from this quarter.”

“Two hirers.”

“Then there are two annexes.”

## The Second Hand

The lane had always been the easy one. A carriers’ run list is entered by the weigh-house and not by whoever holds the slate, and the county has accepted one for nine months, and the girl of twenty-four has been copying about two hundred of them since the thaw for eight coppers a sheet and nobody has ever wanted one.

The quarry had none.

There was no run list at the quarry, because a quarry does not send loads anywhere. A load comes off the cut, goes down a grade, goes up a rope, comes off the block and is cut and stacked, and it is weighed, and the weighing is the whole of the business.

The foreman wrote the number at the rope.

That was the arrangement. Bo called the number off the rope, a man on the hoist wrote it on a card, and the card went into a box, and at the end of the week the box was counted against the tin at the pay table, and there was nothing anywhere in that chain with an hour in it.

Weight is not time. Nobody had ever needed it to be, because for nine months every hour worked outside the crew in this county had come off a sheet with a time on it that a county could check against a weigh-house.

She had not thought about it once in three weeks.

She had thought about the hand. She had thought about the block icing, and the four men short, and the nine minutes of arithmetic she did every evening about the tin. She had thought about the season's road being shut and the lane being dead and the crew's extra pair going on in the second week.

She had not thought for one minute in three weeks about the fact that the hours she was about to certify had not got an hour in them.

## What It Means

It is not a small thing.

The annex returns with the quarter, on the same crate, in the same order. Hours worked outside the crew, annexed by the copyist and certified and stamped, are furnace hours by certification and not by place — that is what the relicensing bought and it is why the second condition could be amended without being broken.

But an hour has to be an hour. If the sheet cannot say when, then the copyist has nothing to write, and if he has nothing to write then the column does not fill, and if the column does not fill then there is a hole in it, and a hole in that column is a gap, and a gap in that column is not an absence. An absence is a stage that was never announced. A gap is work that was done and cannot be shown.

She sat on a step outside the Hall for about eleven minutes working out what that was.

It was this: she could carry stone until the thaw and every load of it would be invisible, and the licence would be hung on a column that had a hole in it, and the hole would not be the hole she had spent a year being furious about. It would be a new one, and it would be hers, and it would be in the hands of nine men who read their own schedule.

## Three Days at the Rope

The hoist-man was called Kan and was about forty and had worked the frame for eleven years and wrote the cards in a hand that was better than the copyist's.

She stood at the rope for three days.

She did not ask him for anything the first day. She worked the block and the grade and hauled on the second rope with the other three and put in her loads, and she watched him write the number at the rope, and he watched her watching him, and by the second day he had worked out what she was at without a word being said about it.

“You want a time on it,” said Kan, on the second day, at about the fifth hour, without stopping.

“I want an hour a county can read.”

“That is not the same as a time,” said Kan.

“No,” said Ning Yan. “It is not.”

## What It Took Him

He worked it out in about a minute and she watched him work it out.

A weight ticket with a time on it is a thing a man can be asked about. Bo could be asked to produce a card at the third hour of a morning and show the hour on it, and if the hour was the third hour of the morning then Bo had a woman on the block before the light, and if that were asked about more than once then Bo had a problem that was not about stone.

And Kan would be the one holding the pen.

He held it for about nine seconds.

“All right,” said Kan, and turned a fresh card round, and wrote the hour in at the left with the number in at the right, and the two of them were on one line and there was nothing on the line that was not stone or time.

“You are going to ask me for six weeks of these.”

“I am going to ask you for the ones I am here for.”

“You are going to ask me for six weeks of these,” said Kan, “and I am going to write them, and you are not going to say one word about it again, and in about four weeks somebody is going to come down here and ask me why my cards are different, and I am going to tell them that they are different because a member of the Qinghe crew asked for them and I said yes, and that will be the whole of what I say.”

“Agreed.”

“And if I am wrong about that being enough.”

“Then it will have been enough anyway,” said Ning Yan, “because I will be standing in front of whoever asks you, and I will say it first.”

He looked at her.

“That is not much of a thing to offer a man with a pen in his hand.”

“It is the only one I have,” said Ning Yan, “and it is the same one I gave a man in a county office twenty-five days ago, and it has not cost me anything yet, and I would rather say the true thing than say the large thing.”

## The Irregular

The copyist came back on the fourth morning with the slate and wrote the annex off forty-one cards in one sitting, and the irregular went on the front of the Qinghe furnace schedule for the third time in a year, and he stamped it, and she carried it four miles up the mountain and it sat on a board in a Hall with no roof until a clerk came for it.

He had never once asked her where the hours came from. That had been settled in the summer in about four seconds between the two of them and had not been reopened since.

She had a hole in a column that nobody in nine counties could fill and no office above this county had ever seen, and she had filled it in three days at a rope with a pen belonging to a man who had every reason not to.

She went up four miles with her left hand under the lining and her right hand open, and the winter crate went down that road on the sixth day with her hours on it and the annex on it and the irregular on it and, on a separate sheet in the middle of it, three lines and a date and a man's name.

She had about four days before it reached the Hall, and ten days after that before the Hall's clerk would put the schedule on the board and the crew would come in off the cold end and read it.

She had decided, at the water channel that morning, about eleven seconds, what she would say when that happened.

She had decided that she would say it in her own words on a furnace floor in nine days, if it took nine days, and not wait and not manage it and not have a clerk's hand do the first telling in nine counties.

She was still holding that when she got to the third flight and had to stand there for three counts.

## What Is on the Slate

“There is one thing I do not know how to ask you,” she said, on the mat in the cold loft, that night.

“You have never had that problem before.”

“No,” said Ning Yan. “What happens to the four men who read their own schedule when they read a line on it that says their licensed one was offered something by an office above this county and said no to it in a room with two witnesses in it.”

“They will look at you,” said Yuan.

“They will look at me and they will not ask, because they have not asked me anything since the summer, and they will read it about four times between now and the thaw, and every one of those times they will be looking at you.”

“Yes,” said Yuan.

“And they will not say anything to me about it. Ever. For the rest of their lives.”

“They will say nothing to you about it,” he said, “and they will be in the room on the nights you come in from the furnace, and that is going to be worse than anything they could say, and there is nothing I can do about it, and you did it anyway and you did it in a county office in front of witnesses and I am not going to be the one who tells you today was wrong.”

She lay on the mat in the cold.

“No,” said Ning Yan. “You are not.”
