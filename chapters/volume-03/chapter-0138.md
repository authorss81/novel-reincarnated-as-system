# The Four Behind the Fifth

It took a boy of seventeen with a finger and about forty minutes.

That was the whole of it. There was no room where it happened, no man who sent anybody anywhere, and no office in nine counties that had been asked to look at anything by anybody.

On the eighty-ninth day the quarter's bundle came up from Lowwater and went onto Sun He's table with about thirty other bundles out of four counties, and a clerk of four years took them off the table one at a time and entered what each one carried against what it was supposed to carry, which is what a clerk does with a quarter's bundle and has done since before the four counties had a name for a quarter.

The Lantern Register tower at Lowwater returned five sheets and a description of four hundred and eleven volumes and a column of twenty-two years.

It returned nothing for the quarter.

She was in the room. She had a county hour at the third hour and there was a queue, and she was ninth in it, and she heard the whole of it over the top of about thirty other people's heads.

“Ninth,” said a clerk at the top of the counter, without looking up.

She went up. She had her own hours to sign and her own hand to sign them in, and she signed them, and the clerk turned the sheet so the queue could see that it was signed, and she did not say anything, because there was one thing in the room she could have said and she had decided in the lane not to say it.

“Wait there,” said the clerk to nobody in particular. “Sixty-three is out.”

## What Sixty-Three Was

She waited.

The bundle sat open on the table with five sheets in it and the count written on the outside in a fair hand, and the clerk at the end of the table where the bundles were opened was a young man with good eyes who had been doing it since the thaw and who had a boy's name called out to him and was moving through a hundred bundles a quarter without any particular trouble in his life.

“Lantern Register, Lowwater,” he said. “Five sheets and a volume description. Returned: the fifth is a description of four hundred and eleven volumes off the warm shelf. The first is twenty-two working lines for the quarter. The second is an index of the column. The third is a list of four certified county copies delivered and not entered upward. The fourth is a fair line at the back of a volume. The fifth is nothing at all.”

“No,” said the clerk at the top of the counter.

“There is a fifth,” said the young man, “and it is the description of the volumes. The count on the outside is five. The number of sheets in the bundle is five. The return requires a sheet for each item carried, and item four on the first sheet is a list of four copies that were delivered to that tower and entered upward for none of the quarter in which they were delivered or any quarter since, and a list is not the copies.”

He stopped.

“Is that a discrepancy.”

“It is a discrepancy,” said the clerk at the top of the counter, “and it is not a complaint, and there is nobody in this office to complain. It is a line on the return. Write: returned five sheets against an expected six. State the missing item as the entry of the four copies at item four.”

“Entered upward for none of the quarter.”

“Entered upward for none of the quarter and, as far as that bundle goes, for no quarter at all.”

The young man wrote it down and did not ask a single question about it, because it was not his job to ask, and he had a hundred bundles to open and it was the middle of the afternoon.

## What She Said at the Counter

She waited until the queue had gone down to four and then she put her signed sheet on the counter and asked a clerk one question in the ordinary voice of a member asking a clerk a thing.

“Does that go up.”

“The return goes up,” said the clerk. “The return goes up with the quarter, in order, the way it is read here.”

“And the discrepancy line.”

“The discrepancy line goes up on the return. It is a line on a return and a return is a page.”

“Up to where.”

The clerk, who was about twenty-five and had been at that counter two years, made a small sound that was not quite a laugh and not quite a question.

“That is not a thing anybody asks,” he said. “The return goes up the ordinary way. If you have a paper in front of you with an office on it, the office on the paper is where it goes. If you do not, then it goes the way returns go.”

“I do not,” said Ning Yan.

“Then you have not lost anything by not knowing,” said the clerk, “and I would not go and find out.”

She thanked him and went out into the square.

## What She Had Expected

She stood in the square with her arm out at the side and the county hour paid for and four loads gone, and she worked out what she had expected, because she had been expecting something for four months and the thing that had happened was not it.

She had expected a rope. She had had a version ready in her head since the thaw — a man in a good coat with a date on him, and a room, and a shelf mark, and a sheet that came down from above with a name in it. She had been carrying that version for fourteen months and it had got her out of bed on about forty days out of four hundred.

What she had got instead was a boy of seventeen, at the end of a long table, with a bundle, in the middle of the afternoon, writing down that four sheets had not gone up a stair.

There was nobody to be angry at. The man who had carried the four copies down a road had done a job in the thaw and had gone home. The keeper had put them somewhere safe because a safe place had been offered to him by a hand that was not going to survive the winter. The county had asked for what a stair carries and the stair had carried it.

Four certified county copies of a furnace schedule of Qinghe, walled behind the fifth item of a volume in a tower, entered upward for nothing.

And the reason they were there at all was that a woman had stood in a lane behind a well in the thaw and asked a man she did not trust to carry four sheets down a mountain in his coat, and had not asked him what he would do with them after.

“You put them there,” said Yuan.

“He put them there. I put them in his coat.”

“You wrote the address on the outside of them,” said Yuan.

“No, I did not. Du Hong wrote the outside of them and I wrote nothing and I have checked that twice in the log this week because I could not sleep.”

He went quiet for a moment.

“Then it was not a mistake,” he said.

“No,” said Ning Yan. “It was not a mistake. It was me, in a lane, in the thaw, with a man I knew was going to be killed for it, deciding that a sheet behind a fifth item was safer than a sheet in a bundle. And I was right. I was right about every part of it and it has still cost a keeper his quarter of a stair.”

She walked the nineteen minutes back to the lane with her hand open at her side.

## The Lane

Da Shan had nine loads on the board and a queue of eleven at it, and the man over the tanner's door was not there, and the first desk in the square was empty for the first time since the snow.

She stood in the lane and looked at the first desk from about ninety feet for about the length of three counts.

Nobody at it.

She had worked out over fourteen months that a man who comes to a desk twice a week is a man buying paper, and that a man who buys paper is a man with a calendar, and that the calendar is the dangerous thing. She had built a whole year of decisions out of that, and it had been true every time, and the desk being empty on an afternoon in the first weeks of autumn was the single most frightening thing she had seen since the spring.

“He has been in his room,” said Yuan.

“He has been in his room and he has five sheets.”

“He had five sheets before he went up that stair. He has had five sheets since the eighth hour yesterday. That is what he bought.”

“He bought it for one sheet of paper.”

“He bought it for one sheet of paper and six months of standing on a stair not asking,” said Yuan. “And you are right, and it does not help, and there is a thing you should notice about it.”

“What thing.”

“He did not come to the square this morning,” said Yuan. “He has never once not come. He comes on days when he buys nothing and on days when he buys something and he has never missed one in twenty-two weeks. And today the desk is empty and the bundle went up on the eighth hour of yesterday.”

Ning Yan stood in a lane behind a well with nine loads on a board and a queue of eleven and the autumn coming down off the mountain.

“He has it,” she said.

“He has had it since yesterday,” said Yuan, “and he did not have to be in this square to have it, and that is the whole of the last six weeks and he has known it since the stair.”

She went and took up a mat.

Nine loads. She did them in the ordinary way, on the off shoulder, one at a time, and she talked out loud to a man who cannot touch anything, twice, in the ordinary voice, and the fee was about three weeks and she did not have the arithmetic in front of her and did not ask.
