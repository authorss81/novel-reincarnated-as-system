# Oil for the Cough

He came up the stair for the seventh time and this time he knocked.

She was in the copying room because she had been in the copying room for two hours waiting for a stamp, and the knock was on the frame of the door with the back of a knuckle, quietly, the way a man knocks in a building where four hundred and eleven volumes are standing on shelves.

Feng said, “Come in.”

The man came in and shut the door behind him, which he had never done, and stood with his back against it.

“Feng Lushi,” he said. “Keeper of the Lantern Register at Lowwater, twenty-two years, four hundred and eleven volumes, a column and a cough. I have read all of that in five sheets and I have stood on your stair six times and never asked you for a thing.”

“You have not.”

“I am going to ask you for a thing,” said the man, “and I am going to tell you what it is before I ask, because I have read your return and you are a keeper who answers truly, and a man who offers a thing before the terms has only one thing to offer.”

She stopped with the slate frame half-turned on the bench.

“Listen to him,” said Yuan.

“I am listening,” said Ning Yan.

## The Terms

The man took a sheet out of his coat. This one was not ordinary stationery. It was a heavier paper with a mark on the top of it that she did not have a name for and that was not the county's.

“I am not from the Bureau,” he said. “You know that and it is true. What I am is a man with a warrant that does not name this county and a room over a tanner's, and I have been in Lowwater since before the thaw and I have bought one sheet of paper and a receipt, and I am telling you that because everything I am about to say depends on you believing the first half of it.”

“I believe it,” said Feng.

“An appointment,” said the man. “A Listener's appointment. It carries a living wage at the district rate, quarterly. It carries oil for the lamp and a hundredweight of coal for a winter, delivered to the door of this tower, in the first week of the cold.”

Feng put his brush down.

“And after that,” he said.

“After that, a chair in the county office at Lowwater on the county's reading days, at the second hour, with the county's own fire, and access to any volume the county holds in this district.”

The brush stayed where he had put it. He did not pick it up again.

“A chair,” he said.

“A chair, in a room, on reading days, with a fire in it,” said the man. “That is what the appointment buys a man who copies cold for twenty-two years. I will not dress it up as anything else, because you would see through it in about four seconds and I would rather you did not have to.”

“Then the other half,” said Feng.

“The other half is on the sheet and I am going to read it to you because you are entitled to have it read and because you are entitled to have it in the ordinary voice, not mine.”

He read it.

It was four lines long and she understood all four of them from the tower stair, standing at the end of a bench with a slate frame under her arm.

*A Listener reports the Lantern Register's paper upward truly and in full, at the quarter, in the form in which it is carried, and reports it whether or not it is entered, and whether or not the keeper's hand is in it.*

*An omission by the Listener is an omission by the office and is entered against the office and not against the keeper.*

*The appointment is for life.*

*It may be set aside on request from without the bound, without the consent of the Listener, upon the same terms as a designation.*

She heard the fourth line go into the room and she did not have to look at Feng Lushi to know what his face did.

“You have read me a line about set-aside,” said Feng, “in a room where I have copied four hundred and eleven volumes of what is written at the top of announcements in nine counties.”

“Yes,” said the man. “I have.”

## The Hook Was Not Hidden

They stood there for a moment.

“I want to say three things and then I will go down your stair,” said the man. “The first is that the hook is not hidden. It is written on the sheet, it is read aloud, it will be entered in a book above this county, and I did not put it in and I cannot take it out.”

“The second.”

“The second is that you are not being bought.” He said it in the same flat voice. “Nobody in this building has been paid anything by me and there is no coin in this room. If you take this you are given a wage for a life and the wage is real and it will be paid in the cold of the coming winter to a man who cannot afford the cold of the coming winter.”

“And the third.”

“The third is the one I would take back if I could,” said the man, “and I cannot, so I am going to say it plainly instead. I did not choose you.”

He put his thumb against the edge of the bench.

“A county examined this tower because a stranger asked a lawful question, and the answer came back with five sheets in it, and the four copies behind the fifth item are the whole of why there is a chair in this county with a fire in it. There is a member of this county in the sixth stage of Condensing whose hours are on two of those five sheets. I have not mentioned her and I am not going to.”

He stopped.

That was the sentence. That was the whole of it, and it had been in a room in a tower in a plain coat in about ninety words, and he had put it in the plainest sentence he had used all day.

## What She Was Standing In

She did not move for about nine seconds.

She had been in this room fourteen months. She had put a county licence on a bench in it. She had watched a man of sixty-one copy the shape of a word he could not read off a grey wafer because a request from outside the bound told him to, and she had been in the stairwell when it happened and had not gone in.

And the answer that had gone up the stair at the quarter had gone up because Feng Lushi could not lie, and because she had stood on a stair and asked him one question and been told *no*.

And there was now a chair in a county room with a fire in it, and a hundredweight of coal, and a district wage for life, and all four of them were in a tower because of a column she was written into.

“Go down the stairs,” said Yuan.

“No,” said Ning Yan.

“He is talking to a man of sixty-two.”

“He is talking to a man of sixty-two and I am in the room and I am not going to be the reason a man of sixty-two takes a life appointment he does not want.”

“You are the reason the chair exists.”

“I know that better than anybody in this tower,” said Ning Yan, “and it is not going to be said out loud in this room, because if it is said out loud in this room then a man of sixty-two has to answer it, and answering it is worse than taking it or refusing it.”

## Feng Lushi Answered

He took about eleven minutes over it. She counted, later, by the lamp.

He read the sheet again from the top, twice, with his finger on the lines, and he did it at the speed of a man who has copied four hundred and eleven volumes and can find a line faster than most men can read it.

Then he said, “You have said the hook is not hidden. Is it concealed anywhere else.”

“No,” said the man. “It is four lines and it is the whole of it.”

“If I take it and I copy a thing that is wrong, what happens.”

“It is entered against the office that receives it, and against me, and I will say so in a room if you are asked.”

“If I take it and I do not copy a thing —”

“You cannot refuse to copy. The line says you report in full whether or not it is entered. That is not a term I can read around and I would not read around it.”

“Then that is the whole of it.”

“That is the whole of it.”

Feng looked at the sheet for a while, and then he said something she had not expected him to say and which turned out to be the reason she could sleep for most of the next month.

“My column is at five,” he said.

“So I am told.”

“It has stood at five for twenty-two years and I have never entered a figure that was not in a hand in front of me.” He put two fingers on the stone of the bench. “If I sit in a county room at the second hour on the county's reading days with their fire, then in about four months somebody is going to ask me whether the column could be brought forward a place.”

“Yes,” said the man. “They will. I would be telling you the truth if I said they would not.”

“Then I would rather be in this room with no fire in it,” said Feng Lushi.

He turned the sheet round and looked at the third line, which was the line for the appointee's own hand, and did not write on it.

“Read it again tonight,” he said. “You have read it to me once and I would like it read twice, and then I will give you an answer.”

“When.”

“The ninth hour tomorrow,” said Feng. “I copy at the sixth and at the ninth and I will not have an answer before the ninth.”

The man nodded and put the sheet back inside his coat and said the thing that was the whole of what was left of it.

“It does not expire,” he said. “There is no date on it after today and I am not going to put one on it. You may hold it for a week or a year and it will be here, and if you take it in a year the coal will come in the first week of the cold of that year and not this one.”

“I understand,” said Feng.

## After

The man took the sheet and put it inside his coat and thanked him, in the ordinary way, and said he would have the coal sent in the first week of the cold and not before, and went down four flights.

She stayed in the copying room with a keeper of about sixty-two who had a quarter off his stair, and a sheet in a plain coat going down four flights with an answer due at the ninth hour tomorrow, and a hundredweight of coal on the far side of that answer.

He picked up his brush.

He did not cough.

That was the thing she noticed and could not put anywhere.

He had been coughing on that stair for fourteen months, in the morning and at the second hour and about nine seconds after any conversation that went past a count. He had made it up the last flight that day and the day before without stopping once.

He had just read four lines of an appointment, refused to put a name on it and sent a patient man away with a promise for the ninth hour tomorrow, and he had not coughed at all.

“Feng,” she said.

“Lighter,” said Feng. “It has been lighter for a week.”

“You are coughing less.”

“I am coughing less,” said Feng, “and I have been coughing for eleven years. In about four days a man is going to send a hundredweight of coal to this door, and in about three months I am going to be sitting in a warm room at the second hour.”

He looked at his own hand on the bench.

“If you tell me that is not a good thing for a man of sixty-two with what I have got in my chest, then you have come four miles up a stair to say it.”

He squared the brush on the bench.

“I have also put my name under a line that says I report the whole of it in the form it is carried, including what is not entered,” he said, “and I am going to do that, and you are in it, and I am not going to pretend to you tonight that I do not know what is in it or where it is going.”

“Feng.”

“Do not,” said Feng.

She went up to the loft.

## What She Said Up There

She lay on the mat with her arm out at the side and said it out loud into the room, which she did, and there was nobody in it.

“They will not have to ask him anything,” said Ning Yan.

“No,” said Yuan. “That is what a Listener is. He is the reason a man with clean hands does not have to come up a stair.”

“He wrote me one line in the spring. At the back of a volume off a warm shelf. And now there is a wage and a fire and a hundredweight of coal and he is going to sit in a county room and copy my hours upward for the rest of his life, and he has an answer due at the ninth hour tomorrow and he knows exactly what is in it.”

“He knows what is in it.”

“I did not ask him to refuse it,” said Ning Yan.

“You did not ask him to refuse it.”

“I would not have been able to live with myself if I had,” said Ning Yan, “and he would not have taken it, and the two of those facts are the same fact and I have had about four hours to look at it.”

She lay with it.

“You are not going to thank me.”

“No,” said Ning Yan.

“You are not going to thank him either.”

“No.”

“You are not going to forgive me and you are not going to forgive him and you are going to lie on this mat at the ninth hour for another month thinking about a man and a fire.”

“Yes,” said Ning Yan, “and he is alive, and he is going to be alive in the winter, and that is a thing I am allowed to be glad about and I am going to be glad about it, and in about four months every hour I put in a column is going to go up a stair in a hand that belongs to a Listener.”

“You do not have to choose one.”

“I know,” said Ning Yan. “That is the part that is new. I have spent fourteen months being told that there is a cost and a date on it, and there is not, and there is just a man in a warm room being glad.”
