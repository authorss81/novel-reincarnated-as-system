# The Last Card

There were five men at the cut where there had been eleven, and the difference was not the frost.

The frost was on the face of the rock and had been since the middle of the month, a white skin that came off in sheets when you put a bar into it and made the first two hours of every day sound like a man breaking plates a long way off. The difference was that Bo had put four men on the haul and one on the hoist, and had done it on the fourth day, and had not said why.

She found out why by standing at the rope for a morning and watching what the others did not take.

The face had six benches left in it that a bar could reach. There were eleven men when she came. There were four days of stone in the ground now, and after the four days there was nothing a bar could reach and would come down, and the frost would go deeper into the face for the rest of the winter and put a foot of white over the top bench that a man would have to go round in the spring to find.

Eleven men had been eating the ground alive. Five men finished it.

## What Bo Asked

He asked it at the rope on the second morning, when the light had come off the face and the hoist had stopped for the hour.

“Will you be here in the spring,” said Bo, “when the face opens.”

He was about fifty and he had a way of saying a thing as if it were about something else, and he did not look up from the block he was marking while he said it, and neither did she.

“I do not know,” said Ning Yan.

“That is the first straight answer I have had about this cut since the thaw,” he said. “The last three said the cut would open in the second week and be full by the third, and it is not, and they are not liars. They are carriers and they do not know what a face is.”

He marked the block and went on up the rope hand over hand.

She had been working three miles east since the first month of the cold and she knew what a straight answer cost in that cut, because the two things she had said yes to in the winter had both been asked in exactly that voice by a man who does not look up. She had said yes to both. One of them had cost her the heel of her hand.

## The Cards

Kan had the slate on a board by the hut and a charcoal stub in a roll of leather, and he wrote the hour in at the left and the load in at the right on one line, and he had done it on every day the rope ran since she came to it without being asked twice.

Forty-one cards.

They were the reason the winter had hours in it at all. A quarry has no carriers' run list and no weigh-house to certify one, and Bo called the number off the rope and weight is not time, so an hour at a stone end in this county was uncertifiable until a man who was owed nothing by anybody stood at a rope with a slate and wrote it down.

He had started doing it three days after she asked him and had not mentioned it again, and he was not thanked, and she was not going to, and that was the arrangement and it was going to hold.

“Forty-one,” she said.

“Forty-one,” said Kan.

“There will be forty-three. The face has two days left in it and there is one card a day while it runs.”

“There will be,” he said, “if you are here for the last one, and the last one is a long sledge and not five loads, and I am going to write it as one load and I am not going to split it across three lines because a card is a card.”

“Write it as one load.”

“I was going to write it as one load,” said Kan.

## Where A Card Goes

He showed her, once, without being asked to, because she had worked out in the autumn that the only way to know whether a thing would be believed in nine counties was to look at where it stopped.

That was the end of it at the rope. Kan’s cards went into a wooden frame by the hut and the frame went up the hill four miles to the Hall on the morning the crate went, and the Hall’s copyist turned every one of them and stamped the bundle irregular in the ordinary place, and the bundle went into the crate and the crate went to the county office on the square and a clerk put it in a press.

That was the whole road. Nine steps, four of them done by men who did not know what she was.

“A card does not know what it is for,” said Kan. “That is the good thing about a card. A man can have an opinion about an hour and a card cannot, and that is why the county would rather have cards and would rather not have slate, and the county is right about that and they are wrong about the word on the front and both of those are true at once.”

“You have thought about this.”

“I have been the man holding the pen since the ice came on,” said Kan. “You think about a pen.”

## The Grade

The light went off the hill earlier every day, and by the middle of the month it was going off the grade before the hoist could be called twice.

That was the real arithmetic of the place. Stone off the face was worth nothing at the bottom of a hill if it sat on ice until morning, and the road three miles east has one grade in it where all the water that falls on this cut comes down, and in the second month of the cold that grade freezes at about the fourth hour and stays frozen until the sun gets onto it at the ninth.

Bo would not run the hoist after the light.

He did not explain it and she did not ask, because she had been on a road in the dark in the autumn with a crew of men and a lead line, and had learned the one thing there is to learn about ice, which is that it does not care what a man intends.

## What She Decided

She took the four days.

She had been counting the tin since the middle of the month and it was doing what it always did, which was to be enough for about nine days of a thing she had not decided on yet. About two hundred and thirty coppers in a tin in a wash house four miles off, and a winter with no wage behind it, and four miles of hill and a flight of stair that cost her three counts on the way up.

And there were four days of stone in the ground three miles east, and then none at all until the thaw.

So she said yes on the second morning at the rope, in the ordinary voice, to a man who did not look up.

And then she said the other thing, which was the one that cost.

“The last four days go up as one bundle,” she said, “on one line, and the county puts the word on the front of them again, and I am going to be standing in the Hall when it does.”

Kan stopped writing.

“The word on the front,” he said.

“The word you know the word is,” said Ning Yan. “It is on the front of the Qinghe schedule and it is the reason you are standing at a rope with a slate instead of sitting on a cart. It has been on the front three times this year and it is going to be on it a fourth,”

“and I am not going to have four days of my hours disappear into a hole in a column in the worst month of the winter because a clerk in a county thought a word was tidier than an irregular line.”

“It is not tidier,” said Kan.

“No,” she said. “It is on the front, and nine men can read it, and it is mine. That is the whole of what it does, and it is enough.”

He put the charcoal back into the leather and thought about it for the length of one hoist.

“Four days,” he said, “on one line, hour at the left and load at the right, and if the clerk stamps it I will write down that he stamped it and the day, because I am the man holding the pen and I would rather it was on my card than on nothing.”

“All right.”

“I am not doing it for you.”

“No,” said Ning Yan. “You are doing it because it is the only honest way to write down what happens on a rope, and because the alternative is that two months of it goes up as a gap, and a gap is worse than a word.”

“Yes,” said Kan. “A gap is worse than a word.”

## The Arm

On the fourth day her right arm went at about the first hour, on a block that needed setting down square instead of rolled off, and it went the way it goes, all at once and with no warning in it.

She put the block down on the face instead of letting it go, because letting a block go four feet onto a cut is how a man loses a foot, and she got her shoulder against it and held it while the arm did what it was going to do, and then she stood with her hand inside her coat and worked the fingers one at a time, thumb, second, third, the way a person works a hand that has gone to pins.

It took about a minute and a half to come back and about four minutes to be sure it was coming back.

Nobody on that face saw it, because they were all on the fourth bench and a man at a rope is looking at the rope, and that was not luck. That was two months of building a rhythm around the third of the time, so that the third of the time happened where nobody was standing.

She had done the same thing with the hour at the county office, where she wrote the column with the flat of a second finger and took eleven minutes over what used to take four, in a room with a queue in it, on a day when a man with a certified copy read the same column aloud and wrote down what he read.

Nobody in nine counties knew what her hand was.

## The Last of the Fifty-Three

On the second night she counted them on the board by the hut, in the light off the window of the hut, with Kan holding the lamp and not saying anything about the fact that a woman was counting another man's cards.

She looked at the dark end of the road and at the white on the top bench and at the lamp in Kan's hand and thought about the whole of it in one line, the way a person thinks about a thing they can do once.

Forty-one.

Forty-three when the face was empty.

And the forty-third was a long sledge, because the last block in a face is always the one at the bottom, and it is the longest haul on the ground, and the light goes off the grade at the second hour now, and a hoist does not run after the light.

She looked at the dark end of the road and at the white on the top bench and at the lamp in Kan's hand and thought about the whole of it in one line, the way a person thinks about a thing they can do once.

Tomorrow was the last day of work in this county until the thaw.
