# A Date in a Counting Room

The girl came to the troughs on the third afternoon of the first month of the cold, which is about four minutes earlier than she ever came, and stood at the far end with a slate under her arm and did not say anything until Ning Yan had put her last mat down and had both hands free.

She had been told in advance. Three washerwomen had been told in advance, and there was a place kept clear at the far end of the trough, and Ning Yan had said thank you for about nine months to nobody at all and had got very good at it.

“Eight coppers,” said the girl, and held the slate out.

## The Slate

There was one of them under her arm and she had copied it because she copies things. That was the whole of the reason. It had come into the wash house folded in four on a carrier's errand and the wash house is where a slate with a stranger's writing on it ends up, because a wash house is where carriers wash and carriers carry paper.

Ning Yan took it in her right hand, because her left hand was under a strip of lining and would be until the first week of the next month, and turned it round.

The front was the slate she already knew about, or most of it. Twelve household lines in a hand that was not the county's and not the Hall's, and a stamp at the bottom, and the number she had written out once in the spring in a ledger in the wash house and had never spoken about again.

The back had a second line on it.

Six words and a date, and then the two characters that go on a paper when there is money in it, and the date was written as a day counted off an origin the girl could read and had underlined twice because that is what you do with an origin.

“It is in the future,” said the girl.

## What a Counting Room Does Not Do

She stood at the far end of the trough and worked it out and the girl watched her work it out, which the girl had always been willing to do and had once told her was the reason she kept coming.

A creditor waits. That is what a creditor is. A creditor with eight hundred jin on a woman in oilcloth has waited through a thaw and a summer and a season's road opening and shutting and has not once been in a room with her and has not once written a date, because a creditor who writes a date is a creditor saying out loud that he is going to do something, and this one had said nothing at all to anybody for a year.

And somebody had put a date on the back of a copy of a slate.

“Who wrote it,” said Ning Yan.

“I do not know,” said the girl. “It is not the hand that writes the front.”

“You are sure of that.”

“I have copied about two hundred of these since the thaw,” said the girl, “and I could tell you the hand that writes the front from a mile off and I would not know that one. It is a different person and it is a better pen.”

## What the Date Is For

It took her about eleven minutes at the far end of a trough and it was not hard.

It was not a date to pay by. Nobody enters a date to pay by in a counting room, because paying is a creditor's own business and does not require writing.

It was a date to answer by.

The line said that on that day the standing claim would be entered for execution, and that the entry would be made by the counting room on the creditor's behalf and against the person named, and that everything required for the entry would be on file already.

Everything required for the entry would be on file already.

That meant a copy of her licence with the claim written on the back of it in the ordinary place. It meant a county that stamps those things. It meant an address, which was four miles up a mountain and was on the front of a furnace schedule that nine men read.

And it meant that the eight hundred jin — which for a year had been the heaviest thing she had carried and had ridden at the top of every stack of paper against her ribs — was going to stop being paper.

A date turns a claim into a schedule.

And she had known that arithmetic for about thirteen months, because it was the arithmetic of the one thing in her life that had never once had a date on it, and she had learned it out of a killing on an escort road in the summer and had had it written into a county's record in her own hand.

## What She Asked

She paid the girl the eight coppers on the spot for the hour it had taken, which was the arrangement and had been the arrangement since the autumn, and the girl took it and put it inside her coat and did not say thank you and had said in advance that she would not be thanked, and that was the whole of that.

Then she said, “Where did it come from.”

The girl looked at her.

The wash house went on with its noise. Three washerwomen at the far trough. Steam off the water. The lath-and-cord on the girl's own leg crooked under a coat that was not hers and the eighth estate's four paid hours out of six and the two unpaid ones that nobody was going to pay.

“It came in on a carrier's errand,” said the girl.

“Which carrier.”

“I do not ask that,” said the girl. “You know I do not ask that. I have copied two hundred run lists and I could name the yard it came off in nine cases out of ten and I have never once written one down, and you did not ask me to and you have never paid me to and I would not take it.”

“I know,” said Ning Yan. “I am asking anyway.”

“You are asking anyway,” said the girl, “and you know what you are doing, because it puts me under the line. It is a line about money and now there is a date on it and a man who wanted to know where a dated slate came from would ask a copyist, and there is one copyist in this town who copies everything, and she is standing in front of you with a splint on her leg and four paid hours out of six.”

The trough went on making its noise.

“Yes,” said Ning Yan. “That is what I did.”

“You know why I come and tell you and you do not ask me anything about it,” said the girl, “because if you asked me anything I would have to decide whether to answer, and I have decided a long time ago and I am not going to un-decide it in the middle of a winter. But you just put me under the line and I want it said out loud between us so that it is a thing we both know happened.”

“It happened,” said Ning Yan.

“Right,” said the girl, and picked up her bundle, and went about eleven steps and stopped and came back, which she had never once done.

“Do not go and ask him,” she said.

“I have never been in a room with him.”

“Stay that way,” said the girl, “and do not ask a carrier's clerk either, because they will tell a county that a member of the Qinghe crew is asking about a dated slate, and the county writes that down, and it goes up with something, and then it is in a book with your name in it and not just on the back of a piece of county paper in a lane.”

She went.

## Three Things She Cannot Do

She worked it out at the quarry that afternoon, on the back of a load going down a grade, standing still at the lip because a woman standing still at the lip of a quarry is not a woman who has stopped.

There are three things you can do about a dated entry against a claim. You can answer the claim. You can get the claim off the paper. Or you can wait and find out what wants the date.

Answering it needs eight hundred jin, which she did not have, and the only man in this county who would take them is the one she has been told to leave alone and has never met and is not going to meet, because the day she meets him is the day a county has two things to write down instead of one.

Getting it off the paper needs an office, and the only office she has any standing in writes in one column and does not touch the other, and she learned that standing at Sun He's table in the spring, in about four seconds, in front of nine people and a queue.

So there were two, and she did not like either of them, and the third — waiting — was not a thing she had any practice at.

What she could not do, and did not try to do, was join the date up to anything else.

There was no reason to. A thing had been offered to her in a county room and she had said no to it, and a separate thing had happened to a separate man with a separate book, and those are two different rooms in two different towns and there is no line between them that a person with any sense would draw.

She drew the line anyway. She stood at the lip of a quarry and drew it anyway, in her head, and it took about nine seconds, and it was the first time in four days she had thought about the county office at all.

## The Last Thing

Ning Yan stood at the far end of a trough in a wash house in Lowwater with a slate under her right arm and eight coppers lighter and a line of light along the middle of her chest where the cold comes in.

She had not gone and asked anybody anything. She had asked one question of the wrong person and had been told so by a twenty-four-year-old with a leg set crooked, in about eleven sentences, without heat.

The claim had been standing for a year and unanswered and unlived-with, and it had been a weight, and a weight is a thing you can put down on a bunk at the end of a day.

It had a date on it now, and she did not know who had written it, and she knew within about an hour that she was not going to find out by asking anybody in this town, and that finding out was going to require standing in a place she did not have a reason to stand in, in front of a man who wrote everything down.

Nine weeks to the date. She did not know the date. She knew the number of days off an origin and she knew roughly how far through the cold it fell, and she put the slate in the wash house's own press under a weight with the other slates that carriers left and washed her hands and went out into a cold that took the feeling out of her right fingers in about four minutes.

And the first thing she thought about, standing on the step of the wash house, was not the eight hundred jin.

It was a girl of twenty-four who had told three washerwomen in advance so that she could not be thanked, going back up a lane with a bundle, having been thanked in advance by a woman who could not say it.
