# The Hire Sheet

The lane behind the well in Lowwater was forty paces long and had been a cart track before the well was dug, and there were four tables under a stretched awning and at three of them nobody was sitting.

The fourth had a man behind it with a board on his knees, and the board had a column of names on it in a hand that had been writing other people's names since before the sun was over the roofs, and had stopped caring about it somewhere in the second hour.

She had come down the mountain on the first cart day with the filing copy in the lining and the licence under it, and she had walked past three county desks to get to this lane, and that was the whole of the plan and she had made it on the tower mat at two in the morning with a calendar and a lamp turned away.

Nobody at any of the four tables had heard of a Licence to Practise Past Registration. She had known that before she came. She had come anyway, because the alternative was to find out what paid work outside the crew was from a person who had an interest in her finding out.

## The Board

“Stone,” said the man behind the fourth table, before she had said anything at all. “You are looking at the stone end and you are looking at it because everybody who looks at this lane looks at it because the oil pays better and the oil wants a man who can hold a lamp and you have got one arm.”

“I have got one arm and a half.”

“You have got one arm and you are the wrong shape for oil,” said the man, “and the oil men know it, and the oil men have stopped asking, which is not the same as the oil men not having looked.”

He turned the board round.

It was not a county sheet. That was the first thing and it was the whole of what she had come four miles down a mountain to establish.

A county sheet has the office mark on it and a heading and a column that is the same width all the way down because the same clerk ruled it. This was a board with a column of names on it in chalk gone soft at the edges, and a second column of numbers beside it, and no mark of any kind on it anywhere, and no name on it, and no place on it where a name would go if the county ever wanted one.

“Nobody countersigns that,” she said.

“Nobody countersigns it,” said the man. “Nobody certifies it, nobody stamps it, nobody has ever heard of it, and if you go to the county with it they will look at it and ask me where I got it. You pay me. I pay you. The road does whatever the road does.”

“That is the most honest thing anybody has said to me in four months.”

“You have been at the Hall,” said the man. “Everybody who has been at the Hall says something like that to me twice a year and then works a month for me.”

## The Signature

He wanted a name and a number and he wanted the number in chalk and the name in the column, and when she put her right hand out to take the chalk it did not close.

It did that about a third of the time. She had tested it twice a day since the autumn and it answered on one test and not on the other and she had built an entire rhythm of her life around which one she was getting, and she had never once told a person at the moment it was happening.

Eleven strangers were in the lane. She had counted them going in.

She put her left hand out instead and took the chalk and wrote her name in a slant no clerk in nine counties can read quickly, and the man at the table read it back correctly in one go, because a name that is written badly and written slowly is not the same as a name that is written badly and written quickly.

He did not ask what she was.

He did not ask where she worked. He did not ask what her arm was, or how long she had had it, or whether the other one was worth anything, or whether she was the woman from the assembly floor, and she stood at a board in a lane forty paces long and waited for every single one of those questions and none of them came, and what came instead was a thumb pointing at a mark on the board and the word *six*.

“Six loads to the water and you get paid at the water and not here,” said the man. “I do not want a woman carrying my stone down to the ditch on a bad back and then telling the foreman it was my stone. You get it to the water.”

“How much is a load.”

“Two coppers the load and there is a man at the water who pays me and I pay you and if he does not pay me that is between me and him and not you.”

“And if you do not pay me.”

“Then you stand at this table and I write your name a second time and everyone in this lane knows what a second time means,” said the man. “I have had this table eleven years and I have written nine hundred names on it and I have written a second time about forty and I have never had to write a third.”

She took the six loads.

## The Weight

A load of broken stone is not a heavy thing. That is the thing nobody tells you about a stone end, and it took her four loads before she understood that the weight was never the problem and the problem was that the weight arrives all at once on a forearm that cannot be asked to hold it.

They came in a woven mat with a rope at the four corners, and the mat went over her right shoulder, and the mat came down off the shoulder, and she got it to the water with both arms and a bad angle in the left one and grit in her teeth, and the man at the water counted six and put twelve coppers in her left hand and did not count them out loud.

Twelve coppers.

She had been paid in a tithe for four months, where an hour entered Shen Xiu's book was worth one part in nine hundred and forty and the worth of it was that it stopped the book being short. She had been paid by a broker's man, once, in coin, for a sheet of paper.

Twelve coppers was a fixed small ordinary amount and she stood at the end of a water channel in a lane behind a well and found that she did not know what to do with her face.

“You have gone strange,” said Yuan.

“I have gone strange.”

“You have been paid twelve coppers by a man who does not know your name and you have not made a sound, and I have been listening to you for four loads.”

“I have never been paid by somebody who did not already own me,” said Ning Yan. “Not one of them. The Hall owns me and pays me in a column. The broker's man paid me for a sheet. Du Hong paid me in a coat he stole.”

“That is a list of grievances and you have had it in your mouth since the sixth day of the winter.”

“It is not a grievance,” said Ning Yan. “It is that I have been worth a fixed small ordinary amount for the first time since I was eleven years old, and it turns out that is a thing a person can want, and I want it, and it is going to be paid by loads.”

## The Second Table

She took eleven more loads before the sun came off the roofs.

Somewhere in the middle of them she stopped being a woman who was looking at a board and became a woman who was in a lane, and the difference showed in the way the lane behaved: the man at the fourth table stopped explaining the work to the other people on the lane and started explaining it more briefly, and the carrier who came down the track with a barrow stopped saying good morning and started saying good morning, which is a different thing and only slightly.

Nobody in that lane said a word about what she was.

She noticed it at the end of the day, standing at the fourth table with grit in her teeth and twelve coppers in her left hand, and it took her about four counts to work out what was unusual about it: eleven people had been four paces from her since the second hour, and not one of them had wanted anything off her.

She went up the tower stair at the ninth hour with grit in the lining of the coat and twelve more coppers than she had come down with, and the tower was lit from the inside, and Feng was on the copying floor with a cough that had got worse over the summer and a lamp that was always burning.

He looked at her hands.

“Stone,” said Feng.

“Stone.”

“Not oil.”

“Not oil,” said Ning Yan, “and you are not going to tell me about the third storey of the tower, and I am not going to ask you, and I have come up here to say that I am going to be at this every day until the season's road shuts and that I want you to write the ninth hour down for me and enter it as a tower change and not as a request, the way you did the fifth.”

Feng put his brush down.

“You have got the filing copy in your coat,” he said, “and you have got the season's hours on a schedule that goes up at the quarter, and now you have got a board behind a well with your name on it in chalk. Do you know what a county does with a woman who has three schedules?”

“No.”

“A county does nothing with her,” said Feng. “A county does not have a column for it. It is not a thing anybody has ever had to write down.”

She had heard that shape of sentence twice in eleven months and both times it had been a relief and once it had been the mechanism by which eight hundred jin went on standing unanswered, and she heard it a third time and it was neither.

“That is the first useful thing anybody has said to me about paid work,” she said, “and I am going to be careful with it.”

“Be careful with it,” said Feng, and picked the brush up again.

She went up to the loft and put the coat down and got the coat back on and looked at the two documents in the lining — four feet of county paper and a worn filing copy with a hole going soft at the fold — and added a board with a column of chalk to the two of them.

She sat down on the mat with her feet out in front of her because the lane had put more into her legs in one day than four months of the furnace floor had.

Twelve coppers a load. Six loads. Seventy-two coppers, less whatever the man at the table kept, which would be nothing, because the man at the table was not keeping anything and had told her so by not saying.

## What She Took

Here was the thing she had come down the mountain to find out, and she had it now, and it was smaller than the licence and it was the whole of the licence.

Paid work outside the crew is stone, oil, paper, water, and one road-guard hire on the Lowwater road. It is paid by the load and by the day. The sheets it is on are chalk and board and a name in a column, and they are not certified by anybody and countersigned by nobody, and they do not go up a stair, and they are not copied upward to an office that never names itself.

And the licence that took four months and a dead man's name and a defect in her own hand says, in five words in the middle of it, that she may take it.

She wrote it in the log on the back of the docket, in the wash code, lying on the loft mat with the lamp turned away.

*Hired. Stone. Board behind the well. Six loads to the water. Twelve coppers the load. Left hand on the board because the right one is out about one time in three and the man did not ask why.*
*The sheet is not a county sheet. Nobody certifies it. Nobody copies it upward. Nobody has ever heard of it.*
*I am on it in chalk in a lane forty paces long, and there are eleven strangers in that lane and not one of them knows what I am, and I am going to keep coming back for as long as the road is open.*

Then she stopped, and looked at it, and added one line underneath in the same code, because it was true and because she was the sort of woman who had been the sort of woman since she was eleven.

*That is better than being known and I am going to have to be careful about how much I like it.*

Down at the bottom of the lane, in the last of the light, the fourth table was still up and the board was still on it and a man was wiping the chalk off the day's column with the back of his hand, because a board does not get taken in.
