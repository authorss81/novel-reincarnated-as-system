# Under Licence Scope

Escort work had to be annexed, and annexing it was not the road's problem.

That took her three days to find out and it cost her the best part of a week.

The second condition of her licence said the hours were furnace hours, certified by the Hall's clerk, returned at the quarter. The second condition had been amended in a county room at the middle of the summer so that the certified annex to the Qinghe furnace schedule was furnace hours by certification and not by place.

A guard's hire on the Lowwater road was paid work outside the crew. It was inside her scope. It was also, on its own paper, a chalk mark on a slate in a frame in a shed at the weigh-station, wiped every evening and chalked again in the morning, and it carried trains and loads and twenty coppers and not one hour.

Which meant that for eleven days she had been doing work that was going up to a registrar and a man with a fair hand with no second hand on it anywhere.

## The Hall Has No Roof

She went up at the second hour of the hundred and tenth with a slate frame and a question.

The copyist was slow with a poor hand and the Hall had no roof and water was standing in the low corner for the ninth day of rain, and he had nine other sheets on his bench and a stack behind him, and he looked at her slate for about a count and then put it on the stack.

“It goes on the stack.”

“It needs to go on today.”

“Everything goes on the stack.”

“How long is the stack.”

The copyist considered the stack for a while, which he did honestly, because he had been putting sheets on it for eleven years and had never once been asked how long it was.

“Two days,” he said. “It was nine deep when I came in on the day before yesterday. It is eleven deep now.”

“Two days before I asked.”

“Yes.”

“And the season's road shuts when the weather shuts.”

“That is not my department,” said the copyist.

## The Second Hand

The fix was obvious and it had been obvious the whole time and she had not seen it, which she was annoyed about for a day and a half before she worked out why.

A charter is not an hour, and a charter cannot be annexed, and a member who wants her own hours on a furnace schedule has to write them in her own hand — irregular, stamped, the whole arrangement — and the irregular entry only stands up if there is something to set it against.

Her loads at the lane behind a well stood against a slate with names on it and nobody to read it after dark.

Her loads on the escort road stood against a frame at the weigh-station, and the frame was wiped at the end of every day, and about a quarter of a mile from the frame there was a building where about a dozen women sat all day and copied out every list that came into this town, for eight coppers a sheet, and had been doing it since the thaw.

She went to the wash house at the ninth night hour.

## The Girl's Slate

“I want a sheet copied,” said Ning Yan. “Not for eight coppers. I want to pay you for it and I want it today and I am going to tell you what it is before you say anything.”

The girl put her slate down.

“Go on.”

“There is a carriers' run list. Every train that comes down the Lowwater road, with the day and the loads and the hour it left the weigh-station. You have been copying it. I know you have been copying it because it comes in on a carrier's slate about eleven times a day and you have copied about two hundred of them since the thaw and you have never once told me what was on any of them.”

“I copy,” said the girl.

“I know what you do. I want to know whether a list of carriers' runs would stand in a county room as a second hand on a set of loads worked on a road by a licensed member.”

The girl was quiet for about four seconds.

“It would stand if it were a run list and not a carrier's slate, because a run list is entered by the weigh-house and a carrier's slate is entered by whoever holds it, and the county knows the difference and the county has cared about the difference since before I could read.”

She picked the slate up.

“How much.”

“Enough that you do not do it for nothing, because I am not going to have you copy a thing for nothing and then have to look at you about it for the rest of the year. Eight coppers a sheet and I will find four sheets, and I will pay you on the day you hand them over and not before.”

“That is more than it is worth.”

“It is more than it is worth,” said Ning Yan, “and I am going to tell you why, and the reason is that nobody in nine counties has ever wanted a list of yours for anything, and a woman who has copied two hundred lists for nobody is not going to be asked by an office, and I am asking you by an office.”

The girl looked at her for a moment.

“Do not thank me,” she said.

“I am not going to.”

“You are about to say something about how useful this is.”

“I was not,” said Ning Yan, “and I am not going to, and you would have stopped me.”

The girl got the ledger out from under the settling floor at about the fourth hour, where she kept two months of carriers' lists that nobody had ever paid her to keep, and she found four sheets of run lists in about nine minutes.

## The County Room

The clerk at the side counter took the four sheets and read them, and the brass rule, and then she wrote out her own hours in her left hand for the eleven days and put them on the sheet, and the clerk compared.

It took him about a quarter of an hour.

“They are the same loads.”

“They are the same loads.”

“The run list gives a train and a day. The sheet gives a train and a day. They are the same eleven days and the same trains and the same loads in the same order, and the loads are the loads a guard's hire is paid on.”

He put the brass rule down.

“Then you have two hands on eleven days of work and one hand on the rest.”

“One hand on the rest is the frame I have written every day in since the spring, with the word *irregular* on the front of it and a stamp under it.”

“That is one hand and a stamp,” said the clerk, “which is worth something, because a stamp is an office and an office is a witness even when the man who put it down does not want to be one.”

## The Man at the Counter

He was at the first desk with a sheet of his own under a sheet of paper that was not the notice about the water rate.

She saw him when she came into the square and she did not change what she was doing, and that was the whole of the discipline of that morning and it took everything she had.

He bought nothing. He wrote for about four minutes, then he put the pen down and turned the sheet over and read the back of it, and then he looked up at the queue at the counter, which was a queue of eleven people doing ordinary things, and he looked at her for about a count and a half.

That was all. There was no message in it. He did not move and he did not speak and he did not buy anything, and she finished her hour and went out into the square.

“They are going to write down that your hours are on two hands,” said Yuan, “and they are going to write down that the second hand is a run list out of a counting house at Lowwater, and in about four months somebody is going to look at that and ask why a licensed member of the furnace crew of Qinghe needs a counting house at Lowwater to stand behind her hours.”

“He did not buy anything.”

“He did not buy anything and he was at the first desk on a morning when a licensed member came in with four sheets and a stamp, and he looked at her for a count and a half and wrote it down, and that is the entire mechanism of the last three months and there is nothing in it I can do anything about.”

“No,” said Ning Yan. “There is not.”

## The Two Hands on One Document

She worked it out on the lane at the second hour with nine loads on the board and the rain starting again on the cut.

The scope line was what let her take the work.

It was one sentence on a licence granted at the third hour of a day in the middle of the spring, and it said a servant-born who breaks in the open may draw sect qi, take paid work outside the crew, and be taught past her stage, under countersignature at that county.

Without it she would have been at a furnace floor nine hours a day on a tithe and nowhere else.

And the seventh condition was on the same sheet, in a registrar's ordinary hand, entered because he was required to enter it, and it put eight hundred jin on the back of the one piece of county paper that carried the sentence which had let her stand on a road at all.

She had got the freedom and the exposure from the same document and had signed for both and would sign again.

“The day you got the licence,” said Yuan, “you got a wage and a price in the same envelope.”

“Yes,” said Ning Yan.

“And today you had to bring four sheets out of a counting house to stand behind eleven days of a hired guard's work so that a clerk at a brass rule would not write *irregular* and nothing else next to it.”

“Yes.”

“That is the whole of the seventh condition and the whole of the scope line and the whole of what a legal practitioner's life at the ninth stage of Condensing costs in this county,” said Yuan, “and you are standing at a board behind a well at eighteen with nine loads on it.”

She took up the mat.

“Ten,” she said to Da Shan.

“Ten is what there is.”

She did the ten, one at a time, on the off shoulder, and she talked out loud to a man who cannot touch anything, twice, in the ordinary voice, and the fee was gone in the afternoon and would be gone again in the morning, and about nine days of road were left in the season and she was four hours short on a column that a man with a fair hand was going to read upward for the rest of his life.

“Do not tell me to be tired,” she said to the air at the second hour of the night on the lane behind a well.

“Tired is free,” said Yuan. “It is the only thing in this arrangement that has not been put on an invoice.”
