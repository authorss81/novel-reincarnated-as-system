# What Da Shan Told Him

The winter crate came up to the Hall in the second month of the cold and sat on a bench for a week, and the schedule came out of it and went on the board at the second hour, and nine men read it before the eighth hour of that day.

The return itself had not come back down. It was in a county office on the square four miles off, waiting to be read in front of a room, and she was not going to be in that room when it was.

She was standing at the end of the furnace floor when it happened and she did not need to be told. A crew reads its own schedule. That had been true in the autumn when a clerk had written one word on the front of it and stamped it, and it was true now, and nine men walked past a board in a hall with no roof and stopped.

She let them.

It took about four minutes and she stood at the end of the floor with a bar in her hand and counted it, and nobody said anything while any of it was happening, and that was correct, because a man reading his own hours does not want the furnace stopped for it.

Then Gao came off the grate and looked at her and said, “Right,” and went and got his coat.

And that was the whole of the response of the crew of the Qinghe furnace to the fact that their licensed one had been offered something by an office above this county and had said no to it in a room with two witnesses.

The next day there was a pair of boots on the second flight that had not been there before, and after that there was often a man in the room earlier than she was, and neither of those things was ever mentioned by anybody, ever, at any time, for the rest of her life.

## What It Costs

She had been braced for the crew and the crew was cheap.

The crew was nine men who had been told the worst thing first, at the ninth hour, on a furnace floor, by the person it was about, and who had therefore had a week to put it somewhere, and who had put it in a grate and a pair of boots on a second flight.

That was not the expensive part.

The expensive part was that she had spent about four weeks being frightened of nine men reading a board, and had been right about the mechanism and wrong about the men, and had built the whole of her behaviour for a quarter out of the mistake.

## The Board Behind the Well

She went down the four miles two days later and stood at the board at about the fifth hour, when the light was going, and there were four men at the fourth table and she had not been one of them since the board went dead, and two frames out of the eight on the wall.

Six frames.

In the autumn there were eight, and two of them had been up since the thaw and one of those two had been there because a woman with one arm put nine loads a day on the fourth table and made it worth two men.

She stood at the side of the board for about four minutes with nothing to say, which was the first time in a year she had stood next to a man and had nothing to say.

Then the man at the first table — a carrier, fifty-odd, who had been at that lane since before her — said, over the top of his own mat:

“You are after the fourth one, are you not.”

“Which fourth one.”

“The frames,” said the carrier. “They are not frames, they are days. There is a frame out for a bad week and it comes back when the work comes back and there has not been a frame out here since the year before.”

“No,” said Ning Yan. “I am not after a frame.”

“You are after something else, then, because you have been standing at my board for four minutes and nobody who wants a load stands at it like that.”

## What She Found Out

“Was there a man here,” she said. “In the autumn. About eleven, twelve weeks back. Asked about a furnace schedule.”

The carrier thought about it for about four seconds, which is about four years.

“Aye,” he said. “Stood where you are standing. Read one standing up, like a man reading a bill.”

She did not say anything.

“He had it in his hand,” said the carrier. “It was a Hall sheet, ours, the one off the board behind you with the stamp on the front. He did not take it off the board, he stood and read it the way he read the thing in the office in the summer, all the way down, and he was on it a good while because there is a lot of it in the middle with the hours on.”

“And it had a stamp on the front.”

“It had a stamp on the front and it had a word written on it in a clerk's hand that I do not know the meaning of,” said the carrier, “and it had the name at the top in the wrong order, the way it always has.”

He went back to his mat.

“I did not think anything of it,” he said. “He asked Da Shan for it. Da Shan gave him the reading of it, because it is our schedule and it is on the board and a man asks to read it. That is all that was.”

## That Is All That Was

That is all that was, and it was everything.

She stood at a board in a lane and worked out what a man with no coin, no writ, and a clerk's misspelling of a name had done in eleven weeks.

He had asked four or five ordinary people what a furnace schedule was. One at a time, politely, buying nothing. And every one of them had told him, because none of them had any reason not to, and the last one had been a man at a board behind a well who had been asked to read a sheet off his own wall and had read it.

And the sheet had a stamp on it that said *irregular*, in a clerk's hand, on the front, in nine counties, because of a decision she had made in the summer about how to certify hours that a woman's hand could not be converted into by a clerk's rate.

She put both hands flat on the board.

That is what the price of being hunted is, and it is not paid by her.

It is paid by a man at a board in the cold who has six frames out instead of eight, because a woman with one arm made two of those frames worth having in the autumn, and because in the autumn a stranger stood where she was standing and read the sheet off his own wall and had a word on the front of it that she caused.

She had put the word *irregular* there. She had put it there deliberately, on purpose, in the summer, with the whole of the cost worked out in about eleven seconds, and she had been right about it, and it had cost a man she did not know the name of two tables in a lane.

## What She Did

She did not thank Da Shan.

She did not ask him about it. She did not say a single word to him about the eleven weeks, or the schedule, or the stranger, or the reading of it, and she stood at his board with her two hundred and thirty coppers worth of winter coming and her right hand open, and she looked at two empty frames.

Then she went up four miles and got Gao and three others and put it to them on the floor at the ninth hour, and it was a request and not an order, and she was specific about it.

“Your own time. Not the crew's hours and not the tithe. Four days a week, the first hour after the carts, for as long as there is stone coming off carts. He does not know you are coming and he does not know why, and I am not going to tell him, and if he asks you where it came from you tell him the crew sent you.”

Gao looked at her for about four seconds.

“If he asks me where it came from I am going to say the crew sent me,” he said, “because that is what you have told me to say, and if a man at a board asks me a second time I am going to tell him it came from you, and you know that I am going to tell him that.”

“Yes,” said Ning Yan.

“All right,” said Gao.

And that was the entire transaction, and there was no speech in it.

Nine men at a furnace floor never once mentioned where four loads a week were going to, for about four weeks. A man at a board behind a well had two frames out of the wall again inside a fortnight and never knew why, and did not ask.

That was the single greatest kindness anybody did her that winter. It cost her about four loads a week out of her own hours, and she did not tell him, and was never going to.

## The Second Time He Was Asked

He asked on the fourth day, at the fifth hour, in exactly the way she had said he would.

Two men at the fourth table, a frame out of the wall, and a man at a board saying, to a stranger in a coat at his elbow, *where did these come from*, in the ordinary voice of a man asking a thing he had not bothered to wonder about until it had been happening for four days.

Gao said, “The crew sent me.”

Da Shan looked at the frame, and then at Gao, and then at the lane behind him, and asked the second question about four seconds later, and did not make it out of politeness or out of a book. He made it out of nineteen years of a man who has had things come down a lane for free.

“Who on the crew.”

“The licensed one,” said Gao.

He did not say it as a favour and he did not say it quietly and he said the whole of it in one go, which is what she had told him he would do, and which is a different thing from what she had hoped for.

Da Shan stood at his board for about a minute.

Then he took a charcoal stub and wrote the day up along the top, the way he wrote every day up along the top, and he did not write a name against any of the four loads, and he put the frame back on the wall himself at about the sixth hour.

He never asked her. That was the thing about it, and she had thought about it on the road up the hill, and it was the only part she had any trouble with. Not one question, in the whole of that winter, to her, ever, at any hour.

She had built four crewmen out of her own hours to put two frames back on a wall, and had told the man in front of them to say her name out loud, and he had said it, and the man had put the frame up.

## What She Was Left With

She had gone down that hill to get something off a man who had given nothing away on purpose, and she had come back up it with a thing done that a man's name was on.

It was the third time in a year that she had gone somewhere to correct a document.

It had worked twice.

She lay in the second bunk that night with the hand under the lining and the short breath going to three on the third flight and about two hundred and thirty coppers in a tin in a wash house, and she did not lie there and think about the man at the board at all, because the man at the board was not the point.

The point was that there was a word on the front of a schedule in nine counties and a man at a board who had read it out to a stranger in a lane, and that she would take that word off that schedule in about nine weeks or she would take it off by never letting a single hour of hers be irregular again, and that there were four months left in which to try either.
