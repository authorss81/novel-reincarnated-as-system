# The First Desk

The first desk was the one nearest the well and it was the desk a carrier went to when a job was finished, and it was open on market days and shut the rest of the time, and she had been in Lowwater for eleven days and had not once looked at it directly.

She looked at it directly on the eleventh.

He was there at the ninth hour with the market going hard around him, and he was in his forties, and his coat was plain and clean and his hands were clean, and those were the two things about him that a woman who has spent nine days in a lane behind a well notices first, because a man at a desk in a market does not have clean hands unless he is a man who does not work.

He had been there twice a week since before her licence was stamped. He bought nothing. He gave his name to nobody. He told carriers he was not from the Bureau.

She walked past him and came back, which is a thing a person does twice in one morning in a market and which four people saw her do.

## The Question

He did not get up. He did not get between her and the desk. He turned his head about a quarter of the way, the way a man does when he has been in a market for an hour and has decided that the whole of his attention is available and no more than that.

“You are the one at the stone end,” he said.

“Yes.”

“You work six loads and you put the mat down in the middle of the lane at the third hour some days and go somewhere and come back, and on the fifth day of the first week you did not come back for two days and on the sixth you were at a county counter, which is a long way to walk and back in a morning.”

“I did not know you were counting my days.”

“I am not counting your days,” said the man. “I am counting my own. I have been at this desk twice a week since the snow and I have the days marked because a man who does not mark his days ends up going to a market on the day the market is shut.”

That was true. She could not see any part of it that was not.

“You have a licence,” he said. “It is a new one. It is about six weeks old, and it is the only one in this town that is six weeks old, and everybody who has been to a county office in the spring knows that.”

“You have been at the county office.”

“I have been at this desk,” said the man, “and this desk is two hundred paces from that office, and I have watched four hundred people walk in and out of it since the snow, and about a dozen of them walked out with something in their hands and looked like they had been given something.”

He said it pleasantly. He did not lean. He did not lower his voice, and there was a queue of nine at the next desk and two carriers arguing about a barrow and a woman selling oil out of a jar on a stool.

Then he asked it, in the ordinary voice, at the volume of a man making an observation in a room.

“Who certifies your hours.”

## The Shape of the Question

She had a quarter of a count to answer and she used all of it, and what she did in that quarter of a count was to run the question against every question she had been dreading for six weeks, and find that it was not one of them.

*What is in your head.*

*Where do you sleep.*

*Are you the woman on the fourth register of the county sheet of Qinghe.*

*How much is the claim.*

None of those. It was a clerk's question. It was the exact question a man asks when he is trying to find out what shape a piece of paper is, and it was asked in a market at the ninth hour by a man with clean hands who had bought nothing since the thaw.

A bounty man buys proof. A bounty man wants a place and then a name, and he has to have the place before he can have the name and both of them before he can move, and the order is fixed because a body is a body and a body is only worth something with a roof on it.

That man did not want a body. She knew what a man who wanted a body looked like and it was a good coat with a tube in his hand, and it had been on a wall at the bottom of this road since the autumn, unhurried, buying nothing, and she had offered it coin at a shut desk and had been refused.

This one wanted the schedule.

The county office at Lowwater did not keep furnace schedules. Feng had told her that at a table four miles up a mountain and the clerk at the third desk had told her it again in a corridor, and she had understood it as a closed door and it had been sitting behind her eyes for four months as the one piece of her arrangement that no man in a market could buy.

He was asking her which office certifies them, which was not on a licence and was not on a filing and was not on any paper she carried, and which was the answer to one question and the answer to every other question behind it.

She could have said nothing at all and walked on.

She could have said *my hours are my own*, which is not a lie and is not an answer, and a man who reads paper would have written that down and let her go.

She could have said a name that was not the name, which is what she had done four times in the winter to men who deserved it less than this one.

She did none of them.

“The Hall's clerk at Qinghe certifies them,” said Ning Yan. “Under a maintaining member's stamp. They go up the stair with the quarter's crate and they come down to the county office, and the county has them in the fifth week of summer and not before.”

## The Door

She said it in the ordinary voice, at the volume of a woman answering a question about a job in a market, in front of a queue of nine and two carriers and a woman selling oil out of a jar.

Yuan said nothing at all, which was the loudest thing he could have done.

“I have said it out loud,” she thought. “In a market. At the ninth hour. To a man with clean hands.”

The thing she had said was six words long. She had said it out loud eleven weeks to a room with a queue in it, and to three sponsors and a registrar, and to a clerk at a third desk, and to a keeper of a tower, and to a tithe clerk with a broken finger.

She had never said it out loud in a market.

Nobody in that market could take it back off her. Not the Hall, not the county, not the tower, not Feng. She had said it to a man on a street and there is no office in nine counties that keeps a column for what a woman said to a stranger on a street.

“There,” said Yuan.

“There,” said Ning Yan.

“You have just told a man who reads paper where the paper comes from.”

“I have told a man who reads paper which office certifies my hours, on a market day, out loud, and he will remember it in the way that people remember things they did not expect to be told, and in six weeks some clerk in that office will be asked a question about it by somebody, and the somebody will not be me.”

“You cannot un-tell it.”

“No,” said Ning Yan. “I cannot un-tell it, and I would not have given it up to be polite to him, and I am not sorry. And that is the part I am going to have to look at tonight.”

## What He Did Not Do

He did not buy anything.

He did not give his name, and she did not ask for it, and she understood in the middle of not asking that she had been given no reason to ask and that there was a difference between a man who withholds a name and a man who has not been asked.

He did not say *I am from the Bureau* and he did not say *I am not from the Bureau* this time; she had that from a sheet out of a tower in fair hand and it was true and it was the single most useful thing she had learned about him.

He did not press.

That was the worst of it and she had to stand in a market and take it.

She had given him the answer at the ninth hour and he had said *thank you*, and had not asked a second question, and had not asked a third, and had turned back to the desk and to the market and to nothing, and had not looked at her again for eleven minutes while she stood there.

A man who had got what he wanted and pressed would have been a man she could have measured. A man who had got what he wanted and stopped was a man who had got what he wanted and would come back when he had used it, and he had all summer and all autumn and a road that shut, and he would come back on the first day the market opened in the cold with one question more.

“Thank you,” he said, at the twentieth minute, without turning round, and it was the same voice, and it was not false, and she believed that he meant it and she believed that he had said it in exactly that way to four other people this month.

She walked away from the first desk at the ninth hour and a half with the market going hard around her and nothing whatever in her hands.

## The Feeling

She got as far as the lane behind the well before it came.

He was not what she had been dreading.

That was the first half, and the first half was enormous, and she had been carrying it since the autumn without ever having put it down: she had been walking four miles up a mountain and four miles back with a stone in her shoe since the sixth day of the winter, and the stone was *somebody will come for me*, and it turned out that the somebody was a man in his forties with clean hands who asked about hours and said thank you.

The second half came a quarter of an hour later and it curdled the first one whole.

A man who wants a body takes a day. He is greedy and he is in a hurry and he pays too much and he tells somebody in a tavern. A man who wants a body is finished with you when he has got it.

A man who wants paper does not take a day. He takes the whole of a season, and he is patient, and he never has to lie, and he buys almost nothing, and everything he buys is a receipt, and when he has got the schedule he will read the schedule at his own pace and decide what a person is and then decide what she is, and neither of those decisions needs him to have been in a hurry.

She stood at the head of the lane with her hands empty and understood that she had spent the morning being relieved, and that relief was the wrong feeling and had been the wrong feeling since the ninth hour, because there was no version of this in which a man who reads paper is a smaller danger than a man who reads bodies.

He is smaller. He is slower. He is going to get there anyway.

“It is not worse because it is slower,” said Yuan.

“No,” said Ning Yan. “It is worse because it is slower and it does not need me to do anything wrong.”

## The Log

She did not write it up that night.

She went up the tower stair and past the copying floor, where the lamp was going and Feng was not there, and up to the loft, and lay on the mat with the licence and the filing copy in the lining and did not get the docket out for about an hour.

When she got it out she put one line in the wash code and nothing else.

*Day 11. Asked me who certifies my hours, in the market, at the ninth hour, and I told him, out loud, in front of a queue: the Hall's clerk at Qinghe, under a maintaining member's stamp, up the quarter. He did not buy anything. He did not give his name. He did not ask me a second question and he said thank you at the twentieth minute.*

*That is the whole of what I said. I am not writing down what I think he wants with it, because I do not know, and I have spent a year writing down what I think things want and every time I have been right in a way that has cost somebody.*

*One of the four things I have done this year that I cannot get back.*

Then she put the docket in the lining and lay on the mat with her arm out to the side.

The fourth estate ran carriers' lists through that wash house and put them up on a board at the lane mouth, and one of those boards had a woman on it who copied every list in the town and could not answer a question about a licensed woman that had just been asked in a market by a man who did not give his name.
