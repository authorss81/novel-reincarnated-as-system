# The Last Load

The season's road closed on the hundred and seventeenth day and she was on it until the last train, which was a thing nobody had told her and which she found out at the weigh-station at the fourth hour on the hundred and fourteenth from a boy holding a lantern.

There was no announcement. Nobody came down and said anything. The contractor's chalk was simply not on the slate the next morning, and the frame was still standing, and the road was there.

## Four Days of It

She had been feverish for four days and had been on the road for three of them, which was not the same thing and which she had checked against a physician's hour and a physician had said something reasonable.

The reasonable thing was: a body at the sixth stage of Condensing, working a grade, with a crack in it that runs a cold line the length of her left ribs and pays at the rim on every use, does not get a vote about what happens next.

She had put it in the frame that night as one train and a quarter and the hours it had cost her, which was most of a day, and the frame was the true one, and the column would be short again at about the third hour and she would be four hours short on the day of the last load with about nine hours of road left to make them up in.

## The Last Train

It was stone. Four loads of dressed stone for a weigh-house at the far end of the county that would not be finished before the cold, which is the way stone always goes, and the fourth load had a dressed face on it that had not been dressed square and would not sit down flat.

The contractor wanted it on.

“I will not take that one,” said Ning Yan.

“It is going.”

“It will not sit. Look at the face.”

“It has sat on every cart in this county for a month,” said the contractor, “and your man at the back will get his shoulder under it and it will go.”

It went, and it did not sit, and it went over on the turn above the ditch with eleven tons on it, and Tan got the near horse and she got the wheel with her left forearm at a cost she did not count and they had it back on the road inside four minutes and the load was cracked in two places and the contractor did not say anything about it for a mile and a half and then said:

“Take it off.”

“Take it off where.”

“Leave it at the side of the road and put two stones on it and a marker, and it will be the first thing a cart hits in the spring and whoever comes down here in the spring will know it is not ours.”

She wrote it in the frame that night. *Fourth load of the last train, not taken. Left at the turn above the ditch with two stones and a marker, per the contractor. Hours against it, four and a half, at the rate.*

Four and a half was the four.

## The Count Came Out

She got it level at the second hour of the hundred and fifteenth, on a slate frame, in her own left hand, with about eleven lines still open and a clerk at the Hall with a stamp who had been told by three men at the west end of a Hall with no roof that there was a member with an annex on the schedule and no roof over the sheet.

The asked figure was fifty-eight.

It was on the certification line in her own hand, and under it, in the Hall clerk's hand, the worked figure, and they were the same figure, and the Hall clerk read them both twice and then put the stamp down without being asked to hurry and without being asked anything at all.

That was the whole of the relicensing.

She had spent four months of a tithe in an enemy's ledger to get a number she could not use. She had spent nine days of not eating to put a stage on no paper. She had two marks and a stamp and a clearance and a sentence in her own hand under a defect line about an aperture that had not healed by one letter.

And she had got to the end of a season with fifty-eight hours asked and fifty-eight hours worked.

“Level,” said the Hall clerk.

“Level.”

“I am going to write *asked and worked level at the close* on the return and not *complete*, because complete is for an ordinary member and you are not one, and I would like the distinction on the face of it.”

“Write the distinction.”

He wrote the distinction.

## What Was Still Exactly Where It Was

She stood in a Hall with no roof at the west end of it with the water in the low corner up to the edge of the step, and she did the sum, and it came out the same both times.

The aperture was the aperture. It had been cracked through in the winter and stabilised at the crack and permanent, and a relicensing had not touched it, and the defect line was on a licence in oilcloth against her ribs in her own hand saying that the defect restores nothing.

The fourth stage of her life was on no paper in nine counties and never would be. The county sheet read one, two, three, five, six, and then a line where four should have been, and there was a man over a tanner's who had read that column at a public counter in the spring and said a true thing about it out loud in a room with a queue in it.

The right arm was open about one time in three and had been since the winter and was going to be so, and there was a cold line down the left ribs that had got about a hand's width longer since the thaw.

The road was four miles and it shut with the weather and it had shut.

Hou Sanliang was sitting in Lowwater with a piece of county paper he had not paid for and a counting room that had opened its book again in the middle of the summer, and he had not been in a room with her and had not been paid and was not going to be paid, and the eight hundred jin stood and answered nothing.

And a girl of twenty-four was in a wash house four miles down the road with a leg set crooked, copying lists for the eighth estate, and she had a quarter of a slab on the fourth day of that week because Ning Yan had paid her on the day she handed it over, which was the arrangement, and she was alive.

## The Fourth Stair

She came up the mountain in the dark on the hundred and fifteenth with a fever in her back and went up the first flight and stopped, and then went up the second and stopped, and then got to the fourth and there was a man on it with a satchel in his hand and a bundle at his feet and the lamp in its cage going above him.

“A clerk came up for it at the third hour,” said Feng, “and went down again with it, and I stood on this stair and watched a quarter's bundle leave this building in a satchel for the first time in twenty-two years.”

“That is a quarter's bundle.”

“That is a quarter's bundle,” said Feng, “and there is a line in it about you that a clerk carried down four flights in a satchel, and I am going to copy it out myself tonight and send my own copy up with the next quarter's bundle, and it will arrive after his by about six weeks and it will say exactly the same thing, and I am doing that because a Listener's first return is his, and not because it changes anything.”

It changes nothing at all.

“I know,” said Feng. “I am sixty-two and I have been a keeper for twenty-two years and I have never sent up a paper that changed anything in my life, and I am going to go on doing it.”

He picked up the satchel.

“Go up,” he said. “You are ill and you have come up four flights to stand on a landing.”

“I have come up four flights to be level on a column.”

“You are level on a column,” said Feng. “The clerk stamped it at the second hour and I saw the stamp from this stair, because I go down to the Hall at the second hour on the certifying days and I have been going down for eleven years.”

He put the satchel down on the step.

“Today I stood in a Hall with no roof and watched a stamp come down on a figure of fifty-eight that a woman wrote in her own hand. It is level. It is the first number in this county that has ever come out the way she meant it to.”

She got up the last flight.

## The Bed

She got to the loft and lay on the mat with the lamp turned away and her arm out at the side and the fever coming up out of her for about the fourth time in nine days.

She had expected to be glad and she was not, and she worked out why on the mat at about the fifth hour, with a burnt stick in her hand and nothing to put it on.

Nine months ago she had been at Condensing five with a licence that capped her there, and she had spent four months of tithe to get the document that removed the cap, and the document had not given her a single hour that the furnace did not already have her in for.

Every hour of the last nine months that had been free had come off a lane, off a board, off a road, or off a slate frame carried four miles to a stamp in a Hall with no roof.

And in about four months there would be five and not six on the column sheet in Lowwater, because the fourth was gone, and a man over a tanner's had already read that column and said a true thing about it out loud in a room with a queue in it.

She lay there and looked at the number and did not like it and did not have anything to do about it.

“About a month to the cold,” said Yuan.

“About a month.”

“Then there is a winter coming and there is no road and there is no lane behind a well because a lane behind a well is not work in the cold, and there is a furnace floor at Qinghe with a crew of nine men on it and four and a half hours of it a day that will put you under a roof and in front of a fire.”

“Yes,” said Ning Yan.

“And a licence with a ceiling of nine that says a member may be taught past her stage under countersignature at that county, and there is not one man in nine counties who is going to teach you.”

“That is true.”

“So you are going into a winter at the sixth stage of Condensing with about three months of channel and no work in this county that will let you put a body into a column.”

She lay on the mat and let it be the true thing.

“I will do the furnace floor,” said Ning Yan. “I will do four and a half hours a day and I will do the tithe and I will not miss a county hour, and if the season opens before the thaw then I will take a lane on the first day it opens, and that is the whole of my winter and it is better than the winter I had last year.”

“Better is not the question.”

“No,” said Ning Yan. “No, it is not.”

The fever came up the rest of the way about the seventh hour and took the stair and the road and the lane and the four miles out of her, and she was asleep before she had finished working out what she was going to do with it.
