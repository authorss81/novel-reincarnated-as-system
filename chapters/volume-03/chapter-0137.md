# A Keeper Answers

The guard's hire started on the eighty-seventh day and the first thing she learned about it was that eight men on a string of carts could make less noise than one man carrying a slate frame four miles down a road.

They went down at the ninth hour with four loads of dressed stone and a load of oil and a load of paper for a shop in the town, and she walked on the off side of the near horse with a quarterstaff in her right hand, and the near horse was an old grey that had decided the whole of it was beneath it.

Twenty coppers and a quarter of the train.

The quarter came to one whole copper and a bit, and she put it in the lining where the log was, and the log was in the lining where the licence was in oilcloth, and the plate was above the licence, and by the ninth night hour she had four separate pieces of paper against her ribs and had not fallen over once.

“Your arm,” said Yuan.

“My arm is out about one time in three and has been since the winter.”

“It has not been since the winter. It is worse than it was in the winter.”

“I know what it is,” said Ning Yan. “I am the one carrying it.”

## The Ninth Hour

Feng had a sheet in front of him and the lamp turned away and his back to the stair when she came up with the slate frame under her arm, and it was simply the ninth hour, and he had been sitting with it since the sixth.

He had written it in one sitting.

That was the first thing she understood, from the look of it. The ink was even from the top of the page to the bottom of it. A man writing carefully does not do that. A man writing carefully stops and finds the place again and the ink goes thin and thick.

He had sat down at the sixth hour and written the answer to a lawful request for the paper his stair carried upward, all of it, truthfully, in one go, and had then gone and got a cup of water and drunk it.

“Sit down,” he said.

She sat down on the stool with the stool at the copying floor of the Lantern Register tower in Lowwater, which she had not sat on in about a year.

## What He Had Put In It

She read it twice. It was five sheets, and it was in his fair hand, which was the hand that had been on her licence's witness line in the spring, and it was the clearest writing in the room.

**One.** The stair's working sheets for the quarter, in the order the stair had carried them, twenty-two lines, each with the hour and the volume it went into.

**Two.** The volumes themselves off the warm shelf, four hundred and eleven, described by shelf and by number and not copied.

**Three.** The column.

That was a page. It was an index in a keeper's hand of every entry the column had ever held, twenty-two years of them, the volume and the page and the day.

It stood at five.

“You wrote out five every time,” said Ning Yan.

“It has stood at five since the market day in the spring and before the market day it stood at five since I could not remember, and that is twenty-two years of the same figure,” said Feng. “A request for the paper this stair carries does not get the figure in the column. It gets everything that has ever gone into it.”

**Four.** A list of the county copies.

She stopped there for a while.

There were four of them. Four certified county copies of a furnace schedule of Qinghe, delivered to this tower by a man off a cart in the thaw, read by the keeper in the presence of the deliverer, and put behind the fifth item in the copying room on a shelf, and entered upward for none of the quarter.

One of the four had been struck through.

**Five.** A line in his own hand, on the last sheet, on its own, not attached to anything.

*Stage five, at Qinghe, announced. Witnessed a countersignature at the county office at Lowwater. Copy of the announcement struck under request from without the bound. Entered upward with the quarter. Entered here. Not entered in the column.*

She read it and put her hand flat on the bench, because the bench was the only thing within reach.

“You put it in as a numbered item.”

“I put it in as a numbered item because it is a numbered item,” said Feng. “It is written in this room, it is a thing the stair carries, and a man has asked me for the paper this stair carries including what is not entered, and that line is what is not entered.”

“It is a line about me.”

“It is the only line about you in nine counties and it is true and it is in my hand,” said Feng. “You said on a stair in the spring that you would not read it before it went, and I have written it because it has to be written, and you are reading it now because I asked you to, and that is a different thing and you will have to carry both of them.”

## The Line She Did Not Ask About

There was one other thing in the five sheets and she left it alone.

It was on the second sheet, item one, in the working lines: an intake hour, entered as a tower change, with the hour on it and no reason, the way a form carries an hour.

It was the ninth hour. She had known it was the ninth hour since the day after a man with a good coat learned her pace from a carrier, and it had been the ninth hour through two months of a market, a county hour at the third, and a licence, and it was a line on a form in a room with four hundred volumes in it.

“Feng,” she said.

“No,” said Feng.

“You do not know what I was going to ask.”

“I know exactly what you were going to ask and I have had six weeks to work out that you were going to ask it,” said Feng, “and the answer is that the request is for the paper the stair carries and not for the line, and that the line is a form and I entered a change on a form in the ordinary way, and there is nothing about it for you to have.”

“It moved me off the fifth hour so that I was one of forty.”

“Yes,” said Feng.

“And you did it because a carrier had told a man at a first desk that there was a woman who set the loads down slow, and you heard it, and you changed a form.”

“I heard it in the spring,” said Feng, “and I entered it at the second hour on a day I could tell you exactly and have no reason to.”

He put the brush down.

“A request for the paper this stair carries does not ask me about the days.”

She let it go, because he was right about that too, and because the last six weeks had taught her that a keeper has one door and it is the door that matters, and this was not her door.

## What She Did Not Say

He squared the five sheets with the edge of the bench.

He did not ask her.

That was the whole of what happened in the next four seconds, and at the time she simply sat on a stool in a tower and watched a man of sixty-two with a cough not ask a woman of eighteen whether she wanted anything.

She had the standing to ask. She was the member the line was about. She was a licensee with hours in a county book and an annex address on a sheet of her own hand in a room four miles away, and if any person in nine counties had a right to say *leave that out* to a keeper, it was her, and she had used that kind of standing exactly twice in her life and both times it had cost somebody else everything.

“No,” she said out loud, to a man who had not asked her.

Feng looked up.

“Say the rest of it,” he said.

“There is nothing else to it.”

“There is about nine sentences in it,” said Feng. “I have been copying for twenty-two years and I can hear a woman holding four of them behind her teeth. Say them or do not, but do not sit on my floor and be polite at me, because I have never once been helped by anybody being polite at me in that room.”

She looked at the five sheets.

“I could have stopped it,” she said. “On the stair, the night it was served. There was a thing I could have done in the county and it would have gone in a book with my name in the first line and it would have been true, every word of it, and you would have been off the stair.”

“Why did you not do it.”

“Because you are sixty and it is the winter you cannot afford and I have spent fourteen months learning exactly how a thing like that gets read,” said Ning Yan, “and because you wrote me one fair line in a fair hand at the back of a volume off a warm shelf and you never once asked me for anything for it, and I have not got a single reason to put you on a county sheet.”

Feng put his hand flat on the top sheet.

“You should have done it,” he said.

“I know.”

“You should have done it and I am glad you did not and I am telling you both of those because one of them is what a keeper owes a member and the other one is what I am going to think about on the top of this stair at four in the morning for the next three months, and I would like you to know which of them I am going to think about.”

“Which one.”

Feng picked up the bundle.

“The glad one,” he said.

## The Bundle

He tied it with a length of cord in the way the stair tied a quarter's bundle, which she had watched him do about nine times in fourteen months, and he wrote the count on the outside in his fair hand, five sheets and the description of four hundred and eleven volumes, and he put it on the shelf by the door to go with the quarter.

Then he went back to the bench and picked up his brush and started on a working sheet for the eighth item of a stack of nineteen, and that was the end of it, and it was not dramatic, and she sat on the stool for about a minute afterwards because she had been braced for something and had got this instead.

“You are not thanked,” said Yuan.

“No.”

“You are not going to be.”

“He is not going to say it and I am not going to ask him and we both know what that means and neither of us is going to say it out loud,” said Ning Yan.

She got up off the stool and put the slate frame on the bench for him to see, because he read it, because he read everything in the room, and then she went down four flights and out into a lane where the tanner's door had a strip of sacking on it with a name on it that a woman who cannot read had written down off a mouth.

The man over the tanner's was not at the first desk. He was at his window with the shutter up and a sheet of his own under the sill, writing something, and he wrote while she was at the door and she went on down the lane without stopping, and she understood that he had known about the bundle before she did, because a bundle that goes on a quarter's list is a bundle that a man with clean hands has been waiting six weeks to be produced.

That was the cost of the answer.

A man had asked a keeper for his work. It had taken twenty-two years to produce and one sheet of ordinary paper to request, and a man over a tanner's had not had to do anything at all with it except wait.

Now he had five sheets and four hundred and eleven volumes described in order, and one of the five sheets was a line about her in a fair hand at the back of a fair volume, and the whole of it was going up the stair at the quarter to a room in this county that has a public counter and a queue in it.

She went back up the four miles and did twenty loads' worth of walking and got four of them, and sat down on a wall at the turn at the willows because the short breath came up on a rise she had gone up a hundred times.

“Do not tell me not to be frightened,” said Ning Yan.

“I was not going to.”
