# The Two Clocks

She was doing two jobs and the second one was two months old.

The furnace shift at Qinghe began at the ninth hour. Da Shan's board in Lowwater was chalked when the first cart came down off the mountain, which was later than the ninth hour on a cleared day and earlier than it on the days the pass was shut, and between the two of them stood four miles of road and a county hour at the third hour of the middle of the day.

The lane was nineteen minutes from the square, and twenty-one when the square was full, and both figures had been hers since the eighth day of the season.

Nineteen minutes out and nineteen minutes back.

That was the whole of what a licence had cost her in a year. Four loads at twelve coppers, every county hour, for as long as the weather let the road take a cart. She had done the arithmetic so often in six weeks that she could do it without the numbers, and what the arithmetic came to was a quarter of a day's pay for the privilege of standing in a room and signing a book in a hand that worked.

She had expected the ceiling to feel like something.

It felt like the roof on a building where you already lived.

## The Week

On the forty-second day she was on the furnace floor from the ninth hour to the second night hour, which was the crew's day and which nobody on the crew had ever asked her to explain. Then she walked the four miles down, because a cleared cart day was a cleared cart day and the next one was not promised.

Da Shan had a board behind the well and four tables around it and about forty men at it on a good morning. He had chalked her name on the same line since the fourth day and had never once asked her what the licence did, which was the most respectful thing anybody had done to her in a year.

“Ten loads,” he said.

“Ten is a lot.”

“Ten is what there is. If you want nine, stand at nine.”

She took ten and did nine and had the tenth on the ground before the middle of the day, which was the kind of arithmetic the lane was built out of, and at the third hour she put the mat down at the water channel and walked nineteen minutes into a square that was full, and stood in a queue for a clerk to write down that she had been there.

Four loads gone. Eight coppers gone. Half a day's pay, gone, in a room with a door.

That was the first clock.

The second clock started when she came back down the lane and ended when she stopped talking, and between them they had been running for six weeks and had never once agreed with each other.

## What She Said to Him

“You do not have to count the walk,” said Yuan.

She was on the stone lane at the ninth night hour with a mat on her shoulder and the short breath still in her chest from the climb, and she said it out loud to nobody, in the ordinary voice, because she said things out loud on lanes.

“You do not have to count the four miles either,” said Yuan.

“There is a column in a book that counts hours and I am the hours,” said Ning Yan. “You do not get an opinion about how I am going to be in it.”

He went quiet, which was the thing he did when she had said a thing that was correct and did not want the credit for it.

## The Copy on the Board

The thing that went wrong went wrong on a clerk's estimate and not on any road.

The Hall's copyist certified the annex fortnightly. He was a slow man with a poor hand and he had been writing names out of a form for eleven years, and what she had worked out at the county counter in the spring was that the annex was hours annexed by certification — that what the copyist wrote onto the Qinghe furnace schedule would be hours on the Qinghe furnace schedule, and would come back at the quarter with the schedule it was annexed to.

What she had not asked about, because nobody had offered to tell her, was where the copyist got the hours from.

He got them from the hiring sheet.

Da Shan's sheet was a piece of slate in a wooden frame and it was wiped at the end of every day and chalked again in the morning, and it carried names and loads and nothing else. There was no hour on it anywhere. There had never been an hour on it in the nine months that any of these jobs had existed.

So the copyist took the loads, and the Hall's clerk converted them, and the Hall's clerk converted them at the rate of a woman who had two arms and a mat on one shoulder and got a load down in the time a woman with one arm gets two-thirds of it down.

She went up on the forty-fourth day and read the certification in the Hall's own duplicate, and the arithmetic was not hers.

Thirty-one hours, against fifty-eight asked.

“Thirty-one,” she said.

The Hall clerk was a man of about fifty who had been copying for twenty-six years and did not look up. “That is the load figure at the Hall's rate.”

“The Hall's rate is a two-armed rate.”

“It is the rate in the book,” said the clerk. “The rate in the book was set in the year before you had a licence and it applies to every load on every schedule, and if you want a different number on a column then you want a different number on a column in a book that thirty people use.”

She stood in a Hall with no roof and let that finish.

“Then I want hours on it,” said Ning Yan.

“The hours come off the sheet.”

“The hours come off me,” said Ning Yan. “I will write them out myself, every day, in the order the days happened, and I will bring them down on the day you certify, and you can put my hand against them and yours underneath.”

“That is a different document. You understand that a member who certifies her own hours is certifying the thing that certifies her.”

“I am not certifying mine. I am writing down where I was, and you are certifying where I was.”

He looked up then, for about a count and a half.

“That is a request for an irregular entry,” he said, “and I would have to write *irregular* on the front of it, and *irregular* goes up with the quarter.”

“Write it.”

“You want the word *irregular* on the front of a column in a furnace schedule in a furnace crew's book.”

“Yes,” said Ning Yan.

He wrote *irregular* on it, and wrote her hours beside it, and stamped the Hall's mark on it at the second hour of the forty-eighth day, and it cost her nothing at all except that it had to be done every day and had to be done in her own left hand and there was no one to do it for her.

## The Cost of Doing It Yourself

That was the part she had not costed and she costed it standing on the stone lane on the second evening.

Every day she wrote down where she had been and for how long, in a slate frame she had bought off a boy in the lane for two coppers, and every day she carried it four miles up the mountain to have it put on a schedule by a man who stamped it, and every day that was four miles and a stamp and an hour of the furnace day that she did not spend on the furnace day.

The four county hour was four loads.

The annex sheet was a day's ninth hour.

She stood on the lane with a mat on her shoulder and worked the sum twice, because she did not trust it the first time and she never trusted it twice.

It came out the same both times.

The relicensing had bought her a ceiling and a column, and the column had to be filled by hand every day, at her own cost, in a slow hand, and she was losing more to the paper than she had ever lost to the arm.

“You have gone quiet,” said Yuan.

“I have gone quiet because you have just watched me work out that the price of a licence is a slate frame and two coppers a day,” said Ning Yan, “and I want you to say nothing clever about it.”

“I have nothing clever about it.”

“Good.”

## The First Week

She got through seven days of it.

That was the whole of the first week of the double schedule and there was nothing in it that anybody would have written down.

The furnace shift from the ninth hour to the second night hour. Four miles down on a cleared day and four miles up at the end of it. Six loads on a good morning and five on a bad one, and the fifth she did with the mat on her left shoulder because the right one was out, and the man at the water channel did not comment.

The man at the board did not comment either. Eleven men at a lane who had worked beside her for two months had not asked her one question in two months, and she had stopped finding that strange and started finding it the best thing about the lane.

The county hour at the third hour. Four loads.

The slate frame, written in her own hand, in the order the days happened, handed to a man with a stamp at the second hour.

The quarter's stair at the ninth hour, four miles down, where Feng copied upward and did not ask her anything, and where a lamp burned in a cage on the fourth landing whether anybody was going up the stairs or not.

And at the end of the seventh day she stood in the second bunk in a room with nine men and her in it and twelve bunks, and did the sum, and the sum was that in seven days she had lost four loads and an hour and four miles twice, and had put seven lines on a schedule in her own hand, and had not been paid for one hour of it.

Nine counties had been told in a county room that she was licensed.

That was not a feeling. She had braced for a feeling and there was not one there, and what was there instead was a woman sitting on the edge of a bunk in a coal-smelling room doing arithmetic she did not need to do twice, because she had already known the answer since the third day and had been checking it anyway out of what she had been calling caution and was not going to call it that again.

“You have a ceiling at nine,” said Yuan.

“I have a ceiling at nine.”

“Nine is four stages above where your body is.”

“Yes,” said Ning Yan. “And four stages above where your body is there is a number in a column that I have to write up every day in a left hand at the second hour, for nothing, on a road that shuts when the weather shuts.”

She lay down on the bunk with her arm out to the side.

“Say the weeks again,” she said.

He would have given her the ceiling twice if she wanted it, and the column, and the thirty-one hours, and the six weeks in front of her, because those were figures she had somewhere to put and she would ask again in the morning and get them again.

The weeks he gave her once, and she knew before she had got to the end of her own sentence that he would not do it twice, and she had known it since the thaw and had asked anyway, because asking was cheaper than carrying a number without checking it.

“A ceiling at nine,” said Ning Yan, “and six weeks of this in front of me, and a column that is thirty-one hours behind a figure I wrote myself in a room with four tables and a frame of twelve unsigned sheets on the back wall.”

She put her hand over her eyes.

“The apprentice at the Hall stamps it every day,” said Yuan, “and he does not charge for the stamp.”

“He does not charge for the stamp and I pay four miles for the privilege of bringing it.”

“Then it is cheap.”

“It is the cheapest thing anybody has ever sold me and I have been alive eighteen years,” said Ning Yan.
