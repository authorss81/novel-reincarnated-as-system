# The Pay-Off

The last block of the face was at the bottom bench and it was the biggest thing in the ground, and it had to come off before the light because the haul was long and the grade was white.

She was at the lip at the first hour because Bo needed the fastest pair of hands on the setting, and the fastest pair of hands on that face had been hers for eight weeks, and Bo had stopped pretending about it in the fourth week by putting her on the lip first and then pretending afterwards.

The block came down in three pieces and the third piece was the one that moved.

## The Light

The sun comes onto that grade at about the ninth hour in the month they were in, which is late, and it comes onto the road and not onto the face, so the face is in its own shadow until about the second hour and the road goes white about an hour after that.

Everything in that cut was timed off those two facts and nothing else.

Stone off the face by the second hour. On the sledge by the third. Down the grade by the fourth, or it sits in the dark where the water comes down and waits for the sun, and a sledge that waits is a sledge that has to be brought up twice.

She had watched a load sit on that grade for two nights in the middle of the month while Bo did not say one word about it, and then had watched him break it up with a bar at the sixth hour when the sun finally came through, in front of five men, by himself.

She did not intend to be the second thing that happened on that grade in front of those men.

## The Lip

The setting is the worst part of any face and it is not the striking. It is the two inches at the end, where the block has come off the bench and is sitting on the lip with nothing under it on the downhill side and a man with a bar trying to walk it over without letting go of it.

Her right arm went at about half past the first hour, in the middle of the second piece.

It went the way it goes. She felt it go from the shoulder down in about a second and a half, and the bar came round with her, and the piece she was walking over the lip shifted a foot toward her and kept going.

She had time to do exactly one thing and the thing she did was not a good one.

She put herself down flat on the lip with her face toward the face and her arms in and held on.

That is the position a man takes when he has decided that the rock is coming and has no time to stand up in it. It does not help. It is not supposed to help. It exists so that a man who is about to be hit is not standing up in the middle of what is happening, which buys the rock nothing and buys him the half-second in which somebody else can arrive.

## Kan

He came down the face at a speed that no man should go down a face at, got an arm under her at the shoulder, and took her off the lip backwards and up onto the bench in one movement, and then stood over her with the bar in his own two hands and did not move for about four seconds.

Then he looked up the face.

The lip was empty. The two pieces were down on the bench below where they belonged, lying still, and no dust was coming off the face, and nothing had fallen.

“You had a leg under it,” said Kan.

“I had a leg under it.”

“You had a whole leg under it and your arm was open.”

“It was open,” said Ning Yan.

He stood there with the bar in both hands for another few seconds, and then he went and got her coat off the rock where she had left it, and put it over her, and did not once ask her whether she was all right, because she was standing up on her own feet in front of him and he could see it.

“You will not get anything for that day,” he said.

“I know.”

“I will write the hour and no load.”

“Write the hour,” she said, “and the load at the right as the block, and not as a sledge, because the block came off and it counts.”

“The block came off after you were off the lip.”

“The block came off in the same stroke,” said Ning Yan. “Write it in the same stroke. If you do not want that on your card, write the hour and leave the right side of it empty and I will not argue with you about it in front of Bo.”

Kan looked at her for a second.

“The block came off in the same stroke,” he said.

## Down the Grade

They got the last piece off at about the second hour and the sledge went down at the third with four men on it and two behind, and she walked at the back of it with a bar in her left hand because it was the only hand that could hold one.

The sun came onto the grade about an hour after that, exactly where it always comes, and the road went from grey to white in about the time it takes to walk a hundred paces, and the frost came off the top of the sledge first and off the bottom of the wheels last.

Nobody was hurt.

That is the whole of the physical part of it and it is worth saying plainly, because in a cut this size in this month a thing like that has one ending nine times out of ten, and the once in ten is decided by how fast the second man moves.

Kan had moved fast. She would think about that for a while and she would not be able to pay him for it and she would not try.

## The Count at the Rope

Bo paid at the rope, in the open, with the money in a cloth bag and the cards on the board, and he read them out, because that is how a quarry pays and there is nothing private about a face.

There were eight weeks of them on the board.

She had put a hundred and twenty loads on that rope herself, at twelve coppers, and had worked the days she could between the crew's hours four miles up the hill.

“One hundred and forty-four hundred,” said Bo.

Nobody said anything, because it was a count and not a sum, and a hundred and twenty loads at twelve coppers is fourteen hundred and forty and every man there could do it.

“And there is the hand,” said Bo.

He did not look up. He said it in the same voice he used for a block.

“The hand is a third off every load she has carried since the ice came on, and the hand was on this face, and the ice was on this face, and I am not going to stand here and pretend the second thing happened by itself.”

Kan said, “The hand was on the sledge.”

“The hand was on the sledge,” said Bo, “on the ice, on the fourth of the month, and it cost me two loads off a man on the fourth bench because he cannot get a load up to the lip on his own any more, and it has cost her a third of a hundred and twenty loads.”

He put his thumb into the cloth bag and counted out the difference on the flat of the board.

“Four hundred and eighty is what the hand took out of it,” he said. “Nine hundred and sixty is what she has got. And I am telling the whole of it in the open because the alternative is that the man on the fourth bench finds out in the spring that his two loads were mine, and he will not find that out from me twice.”

Four hundred and eighty.

Nine hundred and sixty.

She had known the number for two months. She had worked it out on a sledge on the ice with her hand under her coat, and then she had worked it out again with a figure on a slate, and again with a figure in a bag in a wash house, and the three times had been arithmetic.

It was different being handed to it by a man in the open in front of nine others.

Nobody said anything about it. Every man there could multiply two hundred and forty by twelve in his head, and every one of them looked at the board instead, because that is what a man does with a number that belongs to somebody else.

## Three Miles West

She walked the three miles west in the dark with the cold in the front of her and the arm open about half the way and closed the rest, and a hundred and twenty loads in the cloth bag against her ribs under her coat.

Nine hundred and sixty coppers, on top of about two hundred and thirty in a tin four miles off.

That was eleven hundred and ninety coppers in two places in this county, and she had never had that much money in one place in her life, and she was going to spend most of it before the thaw, and there was no work of any kind in this county to spend it on.

“You are thinking about the money,” said Yuan.

“I am thinking about the money.”

“You have four hundred and eighty coppers of it that you did not have in the autumn, and that number is the hand, and you have worked it out four times and you are working it out again.”

“I am working out whether a man can afford a thing I have not asked him for,” said Ning Yan. “Not you. He is owed nothing by anybody and he came down a face at a speed that has a cost in his knees and he did not ask me a single question about the arm while he was doing it.”

“I know.”

“You do not know that. You can see an arm go. You cannot see a man at the bottom of a face.”

“No,” said Yuan. “I cannot. And I am not going to tell you that I can, and I am not going to tell you what he was thinking either, because I do not know.”

She came off the three miles at the wash house road and turned up the hill, and the third flight cost her three counts, and she let it be three.

Behind her four miles east a face had four days of stone in it at the first hour this morning and none at all at the second hour, and a hoist that will not be turned again until the thaw.

There was no stone in this county now for five coppers a load, and there had been for eight weeks.
