# One True Sentence

The wash house at Lowwater had three troughs, one window that did not open, and a bench down the middle of it that four people used, and the eighth estate's girl had the far end of it with her leg up on a bucket and about forty carriers' sheets squared against the edge.

There was a lamp going. There was always a lamp going in there. Water ran the whole length of the troughs the whole time, and the room smelled of wet lime and hot iron and the particular sourness of a leg that has been set crooked and worked on anyway.

Ning Yan came in at the fourth hour of the afternoon with grit still in the coat and sat down on the other end of the bench, which was the end she always sat on.

“Sit up,” said the girl.

“I am sitting up.”

“You are sitting like a woman who has been on her feet for nine hours and has decided that a bench is a bed,” said the girl. “If you are going to sit there, sit up, because I am not having anybody go over on my lists.”

## What Was On the Bench

The girl put the brush down flat, which was the thing she did when she was going to stop working.

“A carrier asked me a question on the eleventh.”

“What carrier.”

“I do not know which one. He had a barrow with the second estate's paint on it and he came to the board at the lane mouth at the ninth hour and he stood there the way a man stands when he is going to ask you something and be annoyed if you say no.”

“He asked you something about the county and you did not want to say no to him.”

“He asked me,” said the girl, “whether the licensed one at the stone end is licensed by Qinghe or by the county.”

She said it flat, and she did not look up, and she squared the brush against the bench.

“And I said I did not know.”

“You knew.”

“I copy lists,” said the girl. “I do not read them. I count them, I copy them fair, I square them, and I put them on the board at the lane mouth, and I have done that for four paid hours a day since the thaw and I have never once read one, and that is not an accident, and it is the only thing I have that is worth anything.”

She looked up.

“And then he said *that is all right, thanks*, and he went away, and he was not being unkind, and he had asked the question because he wanted to know and I could not tell him, and he had already gone by the time I had worked out that I wanted to tell him.”

## Fourteen Days

“You have had fourteen days,” said Ning Yan.

“I have had fourteen days,” said the girl, “and I have written down every day of them, and here is the whole of what I wrote down.

Day one: a man at the first desk bought nothing and asked a girl on the ninth line what a schedule is.

Day four: a foreman at the oil end started calling her the licensed one.

Day five: she put a mat down in the middle of the lane.

Day eleven: he asked me the question and I said I did not know.”

She put her hand flat on the forty sheets.

“I am the safest thing in this town,” said the girl. “I have been since a winter I do not describe, and I have told you that once and I am not going to describe it now, and the whole of what that is worth is that when somebody in this town asks me a question about a person, I can answer it.”

“That is the trade. People come to me because I know things, and what I know is what goes on a board, and I am the only person in Lowwater who can tell you what went on a board.”

“And you cannot tell him whether she is licensed by Qinghe or by the county.”

“I cannot tell him whether she is licensed by Qinghe or by the county,” said the girl, “and I have written that sentence on this bench in my own head fourteen times, and every single time I have come to the end of it I have not known what to do, because I have never in my life not known.”

She pulled the next sheet across and did not square it.

“I have a leg that goes and I copy for an estate that does not know I do six hours and pays me for four, and I have been the safest thing in this town since a winter I do not describe, and the reason it works is that I am never wrong in public.”

“If I am wrong in public once, I am the girl who did not know. And then everybody in this town knows that the one thing I had was that I did not know things either, and there is nothing left after that.”

## The Sentence

Ning Yan looked at the window that did not open for the length of three counts.

Then she said it out loud, once, in the plain voice of a woman saying a fact in a room, to a girl of twenty-four with a leg on a bucket.

“By the county,” she said. “The licence is issued by the Bureau of Records' county office at Lowwater, under Clause Four, and it is countersigned by the county and not by Qinghe, and Qinghe has nothing to do with it except that my hours are on a Qinghe furnace schedule and they go up the stair with the quarter's crate.”

The girl had stopped.

“You may put that on the board,” said Ning Yan, “and you may say it to any carrier in this town who asks you, and you may say it in a lane or in a market or on a barrow, and it is true and it is the whole of it and you do not need me for anything else.”

“That is one sentence.”

“That is one sentence and it is all true and there is nothing else in it.”

The girl picked the brush up, squared it against the bench, and put it down again without using it.

“I am not going to say it back to you.”

“You do not have to say it back to me.”

“I am not going to say it back to you because if I say it back to you it is a thing you have given me and then I owe you a thing, and I have got enough of those,” said the girl. “You have said it in a room with a third person in it. There is a washerwoman at the far trough who is going to have heard the whole of it and she will have an opinion about it by Thursday.”

“There is a washerwoman at the far trough?”

“There are three women at the far trough,” said the girl, “and all three of them know everything that happens in this building, and by Thursday one of them will have said it in a market, and you did not buy it and you did not sell it. You just said a true thing in a room where people were.”

## Not Thanked

The girl got her slate under her arm.

“You are not going to thank me.”

“No,” said Ning Yan.

“Good,” said the girl. “I want to be very clear that I have not given you anything and you have not given me anything, and that what has happened in this room in the last quarter of an hour was a question about a word and an answer about a word, and that if either of us ever says otherwise, in any building, to anybody, then it was not free, and then people will want the second one.”

“I will not say otherwise.”

“Write it down somewhere,” said the girl. “Not in the wash house. Some place that is not a wash house.”

“I have a docket in a coat lining and I write it in a code that no clerk in nine counties can read quickly.”

“Then write it in that,” said the girl, “and do not write down my name next to it.”

## The Return Sheet

Ning Yan stood up off the end of the bench.

The girl stopped her with the brush, not looking up.

“The other thing,” she said. “You did not ask and I am going to tell you anyway, because you will ask in about a fortnight and I would rather you did not have to stand in this room twice for it.”

“Tell me.”

“The county asked for the furnace schedule in writing. Twice, on the ordinary form, any applicant can fill in. It came down the road with the quarter's crate and it sat on a shelf in the third room for two days because nobody in that building knows what a furnace schedule is and nobody wanted to send it back wrong.”
She pulled a sheet across. “So I have written it up on the return sheet with the hour on it, and the hour is my hour and not yours, and it goes up the quarter with everything else that goes up the quarter, and you cannot stop it and you would not want to.”

“You told me that in the spring.”

“I told you that in the spring and it is still true and it is going in the crate on the twenty-eighth day,” said the girl, “and there is a difference now, and the difference is that this time there is a man in this town who has asked me four times in six weeks which office certifies her hours, and the answer is the same answer, and the answer is going into a crate in eleven days on my hour.”
“I am telling you so that you are not standing in this room in a fortnight being surprised.”

The water ran the whole length of the three troughs.

Ning Yan stood in it with her hand flat on the bench and looked at a girl of twenty-four with a leg on a bucket who had just told her, correctly and without being asked, that the thing she had said out loud in a market was going into a crate on her own hour.

“All right,” she said.

“That is all?”

“That is all,” said Ning Yan. “There is nothing else you want and there is nothing else I have got that is worth having, and I am not going to make a speech about it in a wet room because you have told me not to and because it would cost you something.”

The girl almost smiled and did not quite.

“Go and take your ninth hour,” she said.

## What It Cost

It went up the lane behind her in the last of the light, the way everything in this town went up the lane behind her, except that this time she was not the thing being carried.

A washerwoman at the far trough had heard it, and by Thursday there would be a market in Lowwater in which a sentence about the licensed one at the stone end was a thing a person could say, and it would be true, and there would be no office in nine counties that could find out who had said it or strike it, because a sentence in a lane is not a document and a county keeps documents.

That was the part she had not expected at all.

Eleven days and a stranger had come at her with the one question that was the wrong question, and she had answered it, and now a girl who copies lists for an estate that pays her for four of six hours was going to make that answer into something a lane could repeat, and she had not paid for it and had not asked for it and could not stop it.

Four miles up the mountain there was a schedule with nine households on it and three hundred and eleven hours in it and a season that closed on the last day of the thaw, and a clause on the back of her licence that said the hours on that schedule were the hours she had, and no other schedule counted, and there was not one office in nine counties that could see a single one of the hours she was working in a lane behind a well.

She had been paid by loads for thirteen days and not one of them was on any paper in this bound.

It went up the lane ahead of her, in a wash house, in a market by Thursday, in a mouth she had not asked.
