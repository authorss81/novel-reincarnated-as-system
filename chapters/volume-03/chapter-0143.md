# Mercy With a Hook

He signed it at the ninth hour on the ninety-seventh day with her standing at the end of the bench, and he did it in the ordinary way, having read it the fourth time.

That was the thing she had braced against and did not get.

He squared the sheet. He turned the brush. He wrote his name on the third line in a fair hand that had copied four hundred and eleven volumes and put it down the way he put everything down, square with the edge.

He did not cough. He did not put the brush down twice. He did not say anything at all for about four seconds, and then he said:

“It does not expire, so I have about as long as I like, and I am not going to use it. I am sixty-two and there is a hundredweight of coal coming up a road in about four days and my chest is not going to see another winter out of this room without it.”

“Yuan.”

“No,” said Feng. “Not yet. You are standing at the end of my bench and I have known you fourteen months and I am going to do this properly for about ninety seconds and then I am going to work.”

## Ninety Seconds

He did them in order, which she did not expect, because he had done everything in order since the spring.

“First. The wage is at the district rate and it is quarterly and it comes from an office above this county, and it will be in my hand on four dates a year for the rest of my life, and it is a great deal more than a tower stipend and I am going to be able to eat in the winter.”

“Second. The oil and the coal are for this room and they come to this door and I have been putting lamp oil on my own account out of a stipend for twenty-two years, which is a sentence that should not be true about a man of sixty-two.”

“Third. The chair is on the county's reading days at the second hour with the county's fire, and I have been copying cold at the sixth for twenty-two years, and in about three months I am going to be warm at the second hour, and that is the whole of what a hundredweight of coal does for a man and I am not going to make it into anything more.”

He turned the sheet round and squared it again.

“And that is all of it,” said Feng. “That is the ninety seconds. Everything else about that piece of paper is on it and I have read it three times and it does not lie anywhere, and I have known since the eighth hour of the day you came up that stair what it was and where it sat.”

## The Part That Was Hers

“You are going to ask me,” said Feng, “and you are going to ask it badly, and I am going to answer it before you do.”

She had not asked it.

“Feng, I am the reason that chair exists.”

“You are one of the reasons,” said Feng. “There are three that I know of and there are probably nine that I do not. A stranger asked a lawful question of a tower. A county read a bundle and found four sheets that had gone nowhere.”

He turned the sheet over and turned it back.

“That is a concealment in a return. The county wrote it down because a boy of seventeen had a finger, and an office above this county read the line and put a chair in a room with a fire in it. You are in two of the five sheets that came back. You are not in the four that were missing.”

“That is not comforting.”

“It was not meant to be comfortable,” said Feng. “It was meant to be true, and you should have the true one because in about four months you are going to want to argue about this with me and you will not be able to, and I would rather you did not try.”

He put the brush down.

“Now. The part that is yours, which is the only part I am frightened of.”

“Feng.”

“I am going to copy every hour you put in a column for the rest of my life,” said Feng Lushi, “in order, including what is not entered, and I am going to do it in a fair hand at a county's second hour with a county fire, and a man is not going to have to come up my stair again as long as I live.”

He looked at her.

“You are going to hate that. Not today. In about four months, when you have put something in a column that you would rather went nowhere, and you are going to remember this morning and wish I had not done it.”

“I do not know what I am going to do.”

“No,” said Feng. “That is what I have just decided. I am sixty-two and I am going to be dead before this goes wrong, and you are not, and the whole of what I am about to hand you is a man who will be honest about it in a county room at the second hour for longer than it is any use to anyone.”

## Relief, Immediately Curdled

She stood at the end of a bench in a tower four miles down a mountain and felt, in about two seconds, something she had not felt since the thaw.

It came up out of her the way heat comes up out of a stone.

He was alive. He was going to be warm in the winter. A hundredweight of coal was going to come up a road in about four days to a door in a tower where a man of sixty-two had not had a fire in twenty-two years, and the wages were at the district rate and quarterly, and the chair was on the county's reading days, and he was going to see another spring.

That was the whole of it, and it was real, and she let it be real for about two seconds.

Then it turned over.

That is what a mercy with a hook is. She had known it was a hook when she read four lines of a sheet in a plain voice in a copying room and she had understood inside of an hour that every hour she worked from that day forward was a thing the office would have on its own hook, in order, in a fair hand, written by a man who could not lie and who had just signed for life.

She had gone up the tower four times in nine days to make sure he was not asked to refuse it.

And the man who held her hours — who had held them at the back of a fair volume off a warm shelf because he could not think of anything else to do with four hundred volumes and a cough and a foreign clause on a stranger's licence — was now the office's man.

She did not say any of that.

“You are not going to say it,” said Feng.

“No.”

“I would have come to the same place and I got there on my own and it took me one night and about a hundredweight of coal,” said Feng, “and I would rather you did not say it, because it does not help either of us and it is not true, and I have known what I am signing since the eighth hour of the day you came up that stair.”

“You are not a man I forgive,” said Ning Yan.

“No,” said Feng. “I have never been that and I am not going to start.”

He put his thumb on the bench and took it off again.

“There is a woman in this county whose name I wrote at the back of a volume. I did not do it for you and I have never once asked you for anything for it. That is the whole of what I have.”

“It is going upstairs on a quarter's bundle in about nine weeks. There was never anything I could do about that and there is not anything I can do about it now.”

He picked the brush back up.

“I am going to work now,” said Feng. “There are four hundred and eleven volumes on those shelves and a quarter of a stair to make up and a bundle that goes up on a clerk's arm.”

## What She Did Not Do

She did not thank him.

She did not ask him not to sign it, and she had refused to, and she had been right about that and would not have been able to live with herself if she had not been.

She did not ask him for anything else. Not a word, not a favour, not a delay, not a copy of anything, not a line left out.

She walked four flights down a tower and four miles down a road and put in nine loads on a lane behind a well, and Da Shan chalked her on, and the man at the water channel was about sixty and did not ask her anything, and she worked until the light went off the cut and then went and got a mat and slept in the loft with the lamp going in its cage on the fourth landing because the keeper was still in the building.

## The Ninth Hour After

He came up the stair at about the third hour on the ninety-ninth day, while she was on the road, and knocked on the frame of the door, and she was not there.

Feng came down to the lane at the second night hour, which he did not do, and stood in the lane in front of the tanner's door in a coat and said her name once, in the ordinary voice, and she was forty feet away with eight carts.

He had a sheet of his own in his hand.

“A Listener's first return is a copy of the quarter's bundle and it goes up with the next quarter,” he said. “I am told to make the copy. The county's clerk has been up twice and taken the bundle and I am not on the stair, and this is a piece of paper that says I am.”

He held it out.

She took it in her left hand.

It was the bundle's contents. The working lines for the quarter. The volume descriptions. The column index. The list of the four county copies. The fair line at the back of the fair volume, which was the fifth, and which was in her, in his hand, at the back.

And at the top of it, on a fresh line, in a fair hand, at the ninth hour on the ninety-ninth:

*Appointed this quarter. Reporting in full from the next.*

“Who is it going to,” said Ning Yan.

“An office above this county,” said Feng, “and I do not have a name for it and I am not going to look for one, because a Listener who reads the office on his own reports is a Listener who has started keeping copies.”

“That is sensible.”

“It is the only sensible thing anybody has said in this building in a year,” said Feng, “and you are not going to tell me who you are, are you.”

“No.”

“I know,” said Feng. “You did not tell me in the spring either and I have never asked and I am not going to ask now, and I would like to be clear that the two of those are the same thing and not one of them being politeness.”

She stood in a lane holding a piece of her own paper.

From that hour every hour she copied went up truly. And the man over the tanner's would never have to stand on a fourth stair again as long as Feng Lushi lived.

He had taken it for a hundredweight of coal.

She had not asked him not to.

There is no version of that in which she is the one who did it.

“You will want to put that down,” said Feng.

She looked at it for about a count and a half.

“No,” said Ning Yan. “It is the only paper I have got that says where I am, and I am not going to lose it.”

“Then take it up the hill.”

She took it up the hill.

Four miles, and the short breath came up at the turn at the willows on the same rise as always, and she stood at the side of the cart track for about four seconds and then went on, and a hundredweight of coal was on a road behind her going the other way, and both of them were going up four miles to the same door.

“Do not tell me to be glad about it,” she said to the man in her head, halfway down, in the ordinary voice, on a road, to nobody.

He said nothing at all, which was the correct thing, and she went on up.
