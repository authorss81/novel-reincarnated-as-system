# Eleven Minutes

Five days after the ninth hour the winter schedule came down off the Hall board with the nine on it, and nine men read a board in a hall with no roof and one of the two holes under it was new.

The word *irregular* was on the front, in a clerk's hand, for the fourth time in a year.

She watched all nine of them read it. She stood at the end of the furnace floor with a bar in her hand and let them take as long as they wanted, because she had decided what she was going to say to them about it, and saying it after nine men had formed an opinion of their own would not have been saying it.

The holes were easy to count. Nine lines of a stage column and three gaps, and a county hand that could add.

Nobody said anything about the gaps.

They had said nothing about the word on the front for four months too, and she had learned in the spring what the crew's silence was worth, which was less than she had priced it and more than she had deserved.

## What Nine Men Had Noticed

They had noticed the hand.

She worked out the week it had started the way she worked out everything, which was by the first thing that changed shape. Gao's bar had stopped asking her to hold the top of the slate in the second week of the month, not because he had noticed it but because a man who holds a slate for four minutes instead of two notices the four minutes, and it had gone on like that for nine weeks without one word about it in any direction.

Qin had started walking to the counter with her.

That was the whole of it. He did not carry anything and he did not do anything and he had no business at a county counter in the fourth hour of a morning, and he walked four miles down the mountain and stood eight feet off in a queue of eleven people while a woman with a ruined left hand wrote a column, and he had done it four times.

There was a young clerk at the counter at Lowwater who had been on the desk since the thaw, and he was quick and not unkind, and on the fourth morning after the announcement he put his hand flat on the binder and said, in the ordinary voice a clerk uses with a member of his county.

“I can write this for you, if you want it written.”

“No,” said Ning Yan.

“It is four minutes. It is four minutes for me.”

“It is eleven for me,” she said, “and if you write it, it is four minutes and a clerk's hand, and then there are two hands on it, and one of them is not mine.”

He took his hand off the binder.

“Then I will press it,” he said, “when you have finished.”

“Thank you.”

“I am not doing anything.”

“No,” she said. “You are letting me take eleven minutes at a counter in a queue, and nobody has ever let me do that, and it is the first time in this county that a person has noticed it and not tried to shorten it.”

## Saying It Out Loud

She did it at the ninth hour on the furnace floor, standing at the near end where the grate was, with all nine of them there, because eleven minutes is not a thing that can be said to two men and then be true.

It took about four sentences and she had had them ready for nine weeks.

“The heel of my left hand is gone,” said Ning Yan. “It came off a sledge handle on the ice at a quarry three miles east on the fourth of the month. I can hold.”

“I cannot make the turn at the end of a stroke, so about a third of every load I carry goes and does not come back, and six hundred and eighty coppers of that was counted out in the open at a rope in front of four men and I did not say a word about it because there was nobody there to say it to.”

Nine men stood at a grate.

“I write the county column with the flat of a second finger. It takes eleven minutes where it used to take four. That is in every hour I have signed since the middle of the month, in this county and on the Hall's sheet and in the copy that goes up the stair, and every one of those hours is a real hour and a legal hour and it is on the paper in my hand and it is slower than it was.”

She put her left hand out flat on the frame of the grate, palm up, where nine men could see it, and then she closed it as far as it goes, which is most of the way.

“I am not asking any of you for anything. I am telling you because nine men have been standing at a counter with me and holding slates for me for nine weeks and not one of you has said a word, and I do not want a thing going round this crew that nobody said out loud.”

## What They Did With It

Gao looked at the hand for about four seconds and then said, “It is the heel.”

“It is the heel.”

“You write the Hall's sheet with it.”

“I write the Hall's sheet with it.”

“Then the Hall's sheet is eleven minutes long now,” said Gao, “and I have stood at that frame every day since the middle of the month and let a woman write on it like she was carving a date.”

“Yes.”

“Then say so at the frame,” said Gao, “and I will hold it.”

And that was all of the response of the crew of the Qinghe furnace to the fact that their licensed one had lost the heel of her left hand, and she stood at a grate with her hand out flat and understood for the second time in a year that she had been pricing nine men far too high.

Qin said, at the far end, to nobody: “I have walked four miles four times.”

“I know you have.”

“I was going to keep doing it.”

“Keep doing it,” said Ning Yan, “and do not stand eight feet off, because a queue is a place and you are a man in it, and it looks worse for you than it does for me.”

That made him laugh, once, which had not been expected at the ninth hour of a furnace shift by anybody including her.

## The Frame

The Hall's sheet is written at a frame by the board in the hall with no roof, in the morning, by the crew's member who holds the licence, because it is her sheet and her hours and the signature at the foot of it has to be a person and not a clerk.

Gao held it on the fourth morning after she said it out loud.

He held it for a quarter of an hour without once asking whether she wanted him to, and he held it at the top of the frame with both hands because the frame is at chest height for a man standing and chest height for a woman standing is the middle of the frame, and he had worked that out in about two seconds and had said nothing about it.

She wrote the week in with the flat of the second finger and every figure finished by a drag out to the right, and it took eleven minutes, and it took eleven minutes the day before that and the day before that, and there were two men at the near end of the hall who had gone out of their way to not watch.

When she put the pen down Gao took the frame and looked at it, because a man holds a thing that a woman has just finished writing on.

“That is nine lines and three gaps,” he said.

“Yes.”

“And a word on the front of it.”

“Yes.”

“Both of those are going up the quarter,” said Gao, “and both of those are going to be read by a man in a county, and the hand is not on either of them, and that is the part I want to say out loud at a frame once so that it is in the air.”

“All right.”

“The hand is not on either of them,” said Gao, and put the frame down on the bench and went back to the floor.

## What It Cost

It cost the last thing she had.

In eighteen months she had built a life out of paper and out of other people not knowing things about her, and every one of those not-knowings had been a wall she was standing behind, and this was the last wall, and it was the smallest and the most useless one, because nine men had already worked out the shape of it and had been standing next to it for a month out of a politeness she had been reading as a courtesy.

Now nine men knew. Tomorrow the fourteen year olds in the crew quarters would know, because that is what happens in a room that sleeps ten, and by the end of the month a man at a wash house and a man at a board behind a well would know, because a carrier's yard is four hours from a quarry and news travels in one direction in the cold.

And she had said it to get it in front of them herself, which was the thing she had learned to do at a furnace floor in the autumn, and which had worked then and worked now, and which cost the same both times.

Nobody thanked her. She had not told them to and she would have been furious if they had.

## Six Days

On the sixth day after that it was the third hour and it was the sixteenth of the month, and the county office on the square had a queue in it, and the Hunter would be at the back of it eight feet off the wall buying nothing.

She had a morning to sign in, and after it there were three days.

She went down the four miles with her left hand inside her coat and the flat of the second finger warm from the road, and on the mat in the second bunk the night before she had said one sentence to the man in her head about what she was going to find out in that room, and it had been a question and not a plan.

The answer was going to be arithmetic, because everything in this county that had ever happened to her had been arithmetic, and the one thing she had not been able to do since the summer was add up what a licence was for.
