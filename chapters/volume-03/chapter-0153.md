# The Monthly Reading

A quarterly return is a thing that happens four times a year and gets signed.

A monthly attendance is a thing that gets read.

She had worked that out on the road on the second day of waiting and she had got it right, and being right about it in advance did not help at the third hour on the one hundred and forty-seventh day when the queue went round her and the clerk brought a second chair out from under the counter and put it beside Sun He's table, facing out, so that anybody in the room could see the two people sitting at it.

“Attendance,” said Sun He. “First of these. It is read and then it is signed, and if it is read first and signed second and you have something to say about the reading, the reading is what stands. That is the whole reason it is read.”

## One, Two, Three

The member's sheet went across the table and turned round.

She had seen it eleven times in her life and never at a distance of four feet with somebody else's eyes on it.

The hours. Annexed hours. The irregular mark twice. The defect line, and under it the second defect line, which was the same sentence again because it was a new document.

And down the right-hand side, in its own ruled column, the stage column, which is not on the schedule and is on the filing and hangs the licence off it, and which Sun He had told a clerk in the spring was right about, out loud, while writing a number.

He read it out.

Not the whole sheet. The column, at the ordinary volume of a man reading a document in a room where a queue is waiting, because that is what the third column of a county sheet is for.

“One. Two. Three. Five. Six. Seven,” said Sun He.

Then he stopped, because there were two ruled lines after the seven with nothing in them, and he had read to the end of what was there and he was not going to invent the next line.

## What He Said to the Room

“The registrar’s ruling of this year,” said Sun He, “is that an absence is not a breach. An empty line in a stage column is an absence and an absence is not a broken term, and a filing does not fail because a column is not filled, and I have entered no inference against this member and there is none to enter.”

He put the sheet down and squared it.

“That is the ruling and it stands and it is not made today.”

She sat in a chair four feet from a registrar who had just read a hole in her life out loud to a room with nine people and a queue down the step, and then read out the law that made the hole permanent.

He was right. That was the thing that took her about four seconds to arrive at and about four more to get past. He was entirely right, and he had said it in the spring, and she had been furious about it on a road at the time.

A column that is filled has to be filled. A column that is empty is an absence, and an absence is not a breach, and there is no office in nine counties that is entitled to come and look at what is under an absence.

Which meant that nobody would ever come.

She had held the fourth stage down once, alone on the flats east of the town with a stone in her hand, and no office in nine counties had ever heard of it, and a registrar had ruled the empty column an absence and closed the door on it, and she had been furious for about a week and then she had been quietly glad for eleven months.

## Eight Feet Back

The man was at the far side of the room with his back to the wall, in the same place he had stood at a public counter eight feet from a queue in the summer, and he had a book out of his coat and open on his thumb, and he had not been in the queue and he had not gone to the counter.

He did not ask her anything.

He did not ask the room anything. He wrote from the moment the column was read until Sun He had finished, and then he wrote for about a minute after Sun He had finished, which she noticed and which told her that he had a habit of writing what a room said after it had finished saying it.

She signed at the third hour and eleven minutes, in her own hand, and the date went in beside it, and the clerk ruled a line under both and turned the sheet round so the queue could see that it was signed, which is what the queue gets to see.

There were nine people in the room and about eleven in the queue, and nobody in either had looked up during the column.

That was the other thing she had not expected, and it took her out of the building before she understood it. A county office reads a stage column out loud the way it reads a number out loud. It was not dramatic in the room.

It had been dramatic in four counties. It had been dramatic at four in the morning in front of nine men. This morning it was a man reading down a sheet for a queue that wanted to be served.

That was what her life was now. The only room in nine counties where it mattered was the one with nobody in it but the three of them.

Then she stood up and went to the counter to file her copy, and eight feet away a man closed a book.

## The Step

She went past him and he did not get in her way.

“I will be at the next one,” he said.

“I know you will.”

“That is not a threat and I am not making it one,” he said. “You will look for me and I would rather you did not waste an hour of a cold morning on it.”

She stopped, because a man who says *do not waste an hour* is a man telling you that the hour is expensive, which means he has counted it.

“Why are you telling me you will be there.”

“Because you would work it out anyway,” said the man, “and because you have been in this office four times since the thaw and you have looked at the back of this room every single time, and I would rather say a thing than let you keep spending a morning on finding my face.”

“You have never given me your name.”

“No,” he said.

The line went through her like cold water, and she understood it about a second late, and it was this: she knew the name. It had been written into two books in this room eleven days ago and it had been in her head for eleven days, and she had never once, in any of the four times she had looked at the back of this room, been able to put a face on it.

He was going to stand at the back of that room once a month for the rest of the winter and she would not know him by sight, and the only reason she knew his name was a form.

“You have bought one thing off this county in all the weeks of this cold,” she said.

“I have bought one thing off this county in all the weeks of this cold,” he said, “and there was a reason for it in June that has been made out of date by the autumn, and I am not going to buy a second thing, and I would rather tell you that than have you stand at a counter wondering.”

“What was the reason.”

“Ask me in the spring,” said the man, “when it has not already been answered.”

## The Copy in the Lane

She stood in the lane behind the well with it until the wind got cold enough to make her hands.

It was a sheet of county paper about the length of her forearm. Hours in a column. Annexed hours in a column, irregular, twice. The stage column with seven entries in it and nothing after the seven. The defect line, and the second defect line under it.

And in the ordinary place, at the foot, three lines and a date and two syllables.

She had refused in a room and a clerk had written it and the room had gone on, and eleven days later it was on a piece of paper in her own hand's keeping, in a lane in Lowwater, and it looked like nothing.

That was the thing about documents, and it was the thing she had built a life on out of for fourteen months. Every piece of paper that had ever come at her had been a room with people in it — a queue, a clerk, a table, a man who could not refuse her because the rule said so — and on a sheet of paper on its own it was nine lines of writing about a furnace worker.

Eight hundred jin on the back of a licence was a weight. On its own, in the ordinary place, on a sheet of county paper, it was four words and a number.

She put it inside the coat, against the ribs, above the plate, on top of the licence in oilcloth.

Three papers stacked against her chest that she had not put there on purpose and could not get out, and the only one of them that hurt was the shortest.

## The Hill

Three miles east to the west and then four miles up the mountain, at about the fifth hour, with the light gone off the cut and the hoist stopping a quarter of a mile off, and the third flight at the top of it.

She had lost a day. Eighteen coppers a load, six loads, over a hundred coppers, and the whole of the sixth hour of work, because a monthly attendance is read at the third hour and a quarry is worked at the third hour.

That was the price of a diary and she had been told the arithmetic eleven days ago.

“You have not said anything,” said Yuan.

“There was nothing to say in there.”

“You could have said the true thing.”

“The true thing in that room would have gone into two books and been read by a man who has never lied to me,” she said. “And it would not have changed the column.”

“No,” he said. “It would not have changed the column.”

She stood at the top of the third flight in the dark in a room with ten sleepers and counted, because her breath had changed since the fever and she could not stop doing it.

One. Two. Three. Then it came.

Three counts, and a quarter of a mile to the west with no light on it, and eight counts again on the way down, and then nine men breathing in a room that had held twelve three winters ago.

The count was three.

And in a county office on a square four miles down that same road, a man with a book had written down a number she was frightened of and nothing about the thing that had put it there, and had gone out into the cold to wait for a month.
