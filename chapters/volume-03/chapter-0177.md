# Out of the Oilcloth

She took it out on the second evening after the crate went, on the bench in the wash house, under the lamp, with the door shut because the lamp in that room is a lamp the eighth estate pays for and the eighth estate counts the oil.

The oilcloth came open with a sound like a page.

It had been folded in three since the fourth day of the summer. It had been against her ribs through a whole autumn and a winter and a hunt, through a quarterly and a monthly and a reading at the second hour, and it had been taken out twice in that time, both times in the dark on a mountain road, both times by feel.

She laid it flat on the bench and smoothed it with the flat of her right hand.

## The Front

The front was the thing she had spent four months of paper to get.

**Licence to Practise Past Registration.** Clause Four. Her name at the top in the county's hand. Below that, the ceiling, in her own writing: **Condensing nine**, entered at about the fourth hour of a morning on a loft mat in the summer, with a new line under the defect.

It restores nothing. That was in her hand too, under the defect, and it was the sentence she had added when she understood what a relicensing actually is, and it was true, and it had been true since the summer and it was going to be true for as long as the paper existed.

She had reached nine on the fifth day before the reading and the front of this document had been finished ever since.

## The Back

Seven conditions, written small, in the county's hand, with her own hand at the foot of the fifth.

She did not read them.

She had read them once, in a county office, in the summer, and she had counted them, and she had known ever since what they were, and reading them again tonight would have been a way of feeling something about them that she had not earned.

She turned the sheet over and looked at the last line.

**The eight hundred jin.**

The claim. Seventh. Last thing on the paper. Travelling with the document, and unanswered, and going up every quarter with a schedule, and sitting in a counting room she had never been in against a date entered by somebody who was not the man who counted.

She looked at it for a long time.

She was not going to tear it off. It was the seventh of seven and the last line, and the paper behind it was her licence, and the claim had been entered on a document at a registrar's desk because a registrar is required to enter it, and there is not one clerk in nine counties who could strike it without striking the licence with it.

But she looked at it, and that was the first time in five months she had laid her eyes on it properly, and she had to look at it because the day before it had gone up a stair with everything else in it in both directions at once and she wanted to know it was still there.

It was still there.

## The Plate

Under the oilcloth, on her ribs, where it had been since the sixth day of the winter before any of this, was the Clay Plate.

She did not take it out. She put two fingers on the oilcloth over the shape of it and left it where it was, because a plate is not a document and there is no line on any paper in this county that a plate can be entered against, and because she was never going to surrender it, and because the register still read *reconciled* and always would.

## The First Licence

The first licence was crooked over the foot of her own bunk, four feet up, on two nails, and it had been crooked since the day she put it there in the spring, because she had nailed it at about the fourth hour in a hurry in a room with people in it.

It had gone soft at the crease of the defect line. You could see it if you held it to the light — a white line across the paper where the fibres had been pressed flat and had given, and the writing along that line had worn pale and had not come back.

She took it down.

That was not sentiment and it was not tidiness. A sheet going soft over a bunk in a room that sleeps ten, in the third month of the cold, with a stove in it and nine men breathing, is a sheet that will be unreadable before the thaw. She rolled it and put it in the lining of her coat, against her arm, on the other side from the oilcloth, and she did not flatten it and she did not fold it at the crease.

The second one was going up.

## Four Nails

She bought them at the square on the way home from the counter, from a man who sells nails and hinges and oiled cloth and who does not ask a question of anybody who comes into his trade in the third month of the cold.

Four nails. Six coppers.

They were ordinary square nails about four inches long with cut heads, and he counted them out into her hand and put the six coppers in his box and did not look at her arm or her coat or at the four miles she had walked.

## Practice

She practised at the foot of the bunk after the lamp was out, sitting on the floor with her back against the frame, with a scrap of soft pine off the Hall's crate that a man had left for her at the end of the day.

The problem was the arm. She had held one nail this way two winters ago with two hands and had put the licence up crooked at the fourth hour in a hurry, and she had got it crooked because she was holding the nail with one hand and swinging with the other and neither of them was steady, and a nail that is held badly and struck badly does one of two things: it goes in crooked, or it splits the wood.

She had two hands this time as well and a different problem.

She held the nail between the second and third fingers of her left hand flat against the wood, and she swung with the right, and it worked.

Three times out of five.

The first went in crooked, because she had not set the head square against the face. The second went straight, and she could feel the difference in the wood through two fingers. The third went in about a third of a right angle, and she took it out again with the flat of a nail and did it over.

The fourth went straight.

The fifth split the pine, from the head down about an inch and a half, because she put too much of the swing into it, because a nail that is nearly home wants a tap and not a blow and she had been giving it a blow out of habit.

She sat on the floor in the dark with five nail-holes in a piece of pine and her hand aching, and worked out the two things that mattered.

The first was that she could not make the tight turn at the end of a stroke, which had been true since the ice, and that a nail does not want a tight turn. A nail wants to be *struck*, and striking is a different motion, and the whole difference between the hand she had had in the autumn and the hand she had now was about a quarter of an inch at the end of the stroke.

The second was that she had two arms' worth of practice at this and one arm, and that the practice had been spent putting the first one up crooked at the fourth hour in a hurry.

There was no version of tomorrow where she could correct it afterwards.

## The Yard Behind The Hall

She took the scrap of pine out to the yard behind the Hall in the morning, in daylight, with the sun on the wall, because a thing you have only learned in the dark is a thing you have half learned.

There was nobody in the yard at about the seventh hour and there was a bench along the wall that had been there since before she came to the Hall, and a scrap of pine on the end of it about the size of her hand, left there by a man who repairs the Hall's shutters and does not clear them off.

She hammered eleven nails into it over the better part of two hours.

The first three were crooked. The fourth went in about a third of a right angle. The fifth was straight. The sixth was straight. The seventh she split, because she put a swing into it, because a nail that is a third of the way home wants a tap.

The eighth, ninth, tenth and eleventh were straight, and she knew which of them had been straight and which had not before she looked at them, which is the whole difference between a thing done in the dark and a thing done in daylight.

And in the sun, with one wall warm down the side of her, she found out a third thing, which was that the hand did not want to be watched.

The first hour she was putting the hammer on the nail. The second hour she was not. Somewhere in between the stroke had stopped being something she was doing and had become something she was letting the arm finish, and the arm knew a good deal more about the end of a stroke than she did and always had.

She had been trying to do the stroke. She had not been trying to let it be done.

That was worth the two hours on a bench in the sun.

## The Hour

She would do it at the seventh hour, which was when nine men came in off the shift and the room filled up and somebody said something about the fire.

Not at the fourth hour in a hurry. Not at the ninth hour with the whole crew at the grate, which would have made a thing of it out of a document. Not in daylight.

At the seventh hour, with ten people in the room and the fire going and a nail in her teeth.

She put the scrap of pine under her mattress for the morning and lay down in the second bunk with the four nails in a cloth on the shelf at her head, and the oilcloth against her ribs, and the first licence rolled in the lining of her coat, and the Clay Plate under all of it.

Four nails and a licence with nothing left on it above nine and three holes in nine lines and a claim that was going up a stair in both directions in the same crate.

One of them had to be straight.
