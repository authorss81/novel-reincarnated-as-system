# Six

She hit the number on the eighty-first day at the water channel, in the middle of the seventh load, with four miles behind her and the county hour still ahead.

The plan had been in chalk on a ledge above a mat in a tower loft since the forty-ninth night, and it had one column in it, and the column was loads.

Two hundred and forty.

She had written the number in her own left hand and not told anybody, and she had gone and looked at it every night for six weeks the way a person goes and looks at a thing they are afraid of, and on the eighty-first day she put the seventh load down at the water channel and worked the sum in her head and it came out on the two hundred and fortieth.

Two hundred and forty loads in six weeks.

That was what the first six weeks of a relicensed licence had cost her body and what it had bought, and the two numbers were the same number, which was the whole of what she had understood about it in six weeks and had not been able to put anywhere else.

## The Six Weeks

She did not write it down as six weeks. She lived it as a set of days that all looked the same and were not.

The furnace shift at the ninth hour, four and a half hours on the floor and the rest of it in the settling work. Four miles down on a cleared day. The lane at the second hour, the county hour at the third, four loads lost, four miles back up at the ninth.

The slate frame every night, in her left hand, in the order the days happened, brought up to the Hall's clerk at the second hour and stamped and carried back down.

Forty-one days of that. Forty-one stamps. Forty-one four-mile walks she was not paid for, and a crew of nine who had watched the irregular word go onto the front of their own schedule and had gone on not asking her about it, which was its own kind of answer.

The arm stayed where it was. She had braced for the six weeks to take something out of the arm and the arm was exactly where it had been on the forty-second day: open about one time in three, on an eleven-minute window, tested twice a day, and now tested twice a day and also worked eleven times a day without being asked to.

“You are getting better at putting it down,” said Yuan, on about the ninth week of it.

“I am getting better at putting it down with one hand.”

“You are getting better at putting it down.”

“That is not the same thing and you know it is not the same thing.”

“No,” he said. “It is not.”

## The Third Week

The girl came to her at the wash house at the ninth night hour on the sixty-second day with a slate of her own under her arm and the leg set crooked the way it had been set in the winter, and she came at the hour Ning Yan came, which she had worked out from about four visits and had never once been thanked for.

“Four hours,” said the girl. “Out of six. It has been four out of six since the spring.”

“I know.”

“I am not telling you I am owed six,” said the girl. “I am telling you a thing because you are the only person in this town who would do something with it and you are not going to be able to do anything with it.”

“What thing.”

The girl put the slate on the trough edge and turned it round.

It was a carriers' list. The eighth estate's runs, a column of dates and a column of numbers, the ordinary paper of a town that moves goods.

Underneath it, on the same slate, was a second list.

Household lines. Nine of them, or twelve, in a hand she had never seen, with a stamp in the corner that was not the county's and not the Hall's.

“A counting room in Lowwater had this copied,” said the girl. “Not the carriers'. The household lines. And I copied it because copying is what I am paid for and the man who brought it did not ask me what it was.”

“You read it.”

“I copy,” said the girl. “I do not read. That is the arrangement and it is the only reason the arrangement has worked for two months.”

“Who brought it.”

“A man with a clean coat and a list of his own,” said the girl. “He counted my slate with his thumb before he paid me, and he paid me, and he wrote his own name on a slip and gave it to me and I am not going to tell you what it says because I did not read it.”

Ning Yan looked at the twelve household lines for a while.

“Is my name on it.”

“No,” said the girl. “That is why I brought it. If your name had been on it I would not have brought it, because then I would have been asking you to do something.”

She picked the slate up again and put it under her arm.

“Do not thank me,” she said. “I know what you are going to do and I have told three washerwomen I told you, so you cannot, and that is the arrangement and it was made before you came in.”

Ning Yan did not thank her.

“Three washerwomen.”

“Three,” said the girl. “One of them tells the carrier who hauls for the counting room, because that is how it works here, and he will know it in about a week and so will everyone who hauls for them.”

She went out into the lane with the leg wrong and the slate under her arm, and she did not look back, and the water ran in the trough behind her.

## The Fifth Week

On the seventy-fourth day a load came down wrong at the stone end and she put her shoulder under it.

Not the furnace floor. The lane, in front of forty people, a dressed block that had been set on the edge and rolled.

She got it. She got it down and her right hand went in the middle of it and did not do anything at all for about four seconds, and she stood with a dressed block of stone under her left forearm in front of forty people at the second hour of the morning, and the man at the water channel came and took the other end off her without being asked and set it down and went back to his channel.

Da Shan, at the board, did not stop writing.

“Ten loads,” he said.

“Eleven,” said Ning Yan, “when they are dressed and not broken.”

“Eleven is what there is.”

He chalked nothing, because the board was a board and not a county sheet, and a day on a board in Lowwater was a day that left the lane with the sun and went up four miles and got stamped in a Hall and came back down as a line of figures in a book that nine counties could read.

She wrote it in the slate that night.

*Eleven. Second hour. Right out for about four seconds and the block came down on my own forearm and I did not put it down until it was down.*

And underneath, because she had started doing that in the sixth week and had not decided to:

*Everything else the same.*

## The Eighty-First

The stage came up out of the load the way it had come up out of everything since the winter, without her deciding anything about the hour.

She was at the water channel at the third hour of the eighty-first day with a mat on her left shoulder and her right hand open beside her, and she had been working since the second hour and had done seven loads, and the light on the cut stone had gone from white to something else.

Then it came.

Not a noise. A settling, the way a bucket settles when the weight in it finds where it is going to sit, and then everything in her ribs went quiet, and then the quiet went out through her arms and down through her back into the stones of the channel, and she was aware of the cold stripe along the left ribs and of the mat going down on the ground because her left knee had gone, and of forty people at a lane who had not made a sound.

He was there before she had finished falling.

“Nobody touches her,” said Yuan, “and nobody looks like anything has happened, and you are on a stone lane in Lowwater at the third hour of a day and you have not told anybody what you are.”

“I can tell them.”

“You can tell them whatever you like and you are going to have to tell them something in about eleven seconds.”

She got up.

The right hand came in at about the seventh second. That was faster than it had been in four months and she noted it the way she noted everything, and then she stopped noting it because the lane had gone quiet in a way it had not been quiet before, at the second hour, for no reason that any of the forty people standing in it could put into a sentence.

She put the mat on her shoulder.

“Six loads,” she said to the man at the channel. “I have six on me.”

“You have seven on you,” said the man at the channel.

He was a man of about sixty who had been pouring water into a cut in a stone lane for longer than Ning Yan had been alive, and he looked at the mat and then at her right arm, and then he went and got a rag and wiped the water off the top of the block and did not say anything about it, and she understood then that he had already decided, some time in the last six weeks, not to be a person who asked.

She worked the rest of the hour.

## The Sentence

She said it at the fourth hour, alone, in the tower loft, with the lamp turned away and her arm out to the side.

He had it ready. He had had it ready since about the third week, because he could see it coming in her the way he could see the arm go, and he had not said one word about it for six weeks.

“Say it,” said Ning Yan.

He let it go.

[Assembly Hall of Qinghe. Ning Yan of the Qinghe furnace crew, servant-born, on a licence whose ceiling is nine. What is carried past the fifth is let go. Condensing, stage six, at Lowwater is held.]

It went out of the bound at once, the way they go out, in every voice in nine counties at the same moment, and it was the first time since the fourth day of the winter that the layer had said a single word to anybody, and the first time since the fourth hour of a morning at a furnace floor in the spring that a sentence had gone out with her stage in it.

She lay on the mat and waited for it to be over, the way she always did, and it was over in about four seconds.

“Six counties have women in them who are at five and cap themselves,” said Yuan. “Four of them are caped by a county and one of them capped herself. You have just told nine counties where you are, and you told them on purpose, and you would have been telling them in about four weeks anyway because the paper says so.”

“I know what the paper says.”

“I know you know. I am telling you what I saw.”

“All right.”

“No, that is not what I saw and you know it is not.”

She lay still.

He was right. She had known it since the third week.

The announcement says *at Lowwater*. Not at Qinghe, not at a county, not at a lane. **At Lowwater** — a town of about four thousand people with two Bureau rooms in it, and one of those rooms had her licence in it with her annex address written down in her own left hand on the address line, and the other room was four miles away in a tower and had nothing in it anybody could use.

“You said Lowwater,” said Ning Yan.

“I said what the layer said.”

“The layer said where I am standing. You drafted it.”

“It is the same sentence.”

“It is the same sentence and you chose the town,” said Ning Yan, “and in about four days a man at the first desk is going to read a public announcement that says a furnace crew member of Qinghe is at the sixth stage of Condensing at Lowwater, and there is one building in this town where that sentence can be turned into a door, and he has been standing at a counter in that room twice a week since before I had a licence.”

He did not answer that, which was his way of agreeing.

## What She Told Nobody

She went down to the wash house on the eighty-third night because there was one person in Lowwater who had already told her something and had not been thanked, and she wanted to have been the one who said it first.

She did not say it. The girl was sitting on the trough step with the slate under her arm and a strip of willow bark in her teeth, and she looked up, and Ning Yan stood in the water-run lane and said:

“The stage went out this afternoon. It is six.”

The girl took the bark out of her mouth.

“Your name went out with it,” said the girl, “and you told them the town.”

“Yes.”

“Then it is not the carriers' run they will be watching,” said the girl. “It is whoever has your address on a piece of paper in the county, and there is one piece of paper in this county with your address on it, and it is not mine.”

“No,” said Ning Yan. “It is not yours.”

The girl put the bark back in and chewed it and looked at the water.

“My three washerwomen heard it before I did,” she said. “One of them told a carrier and the carrier told a man who hauls. It will be in the market by the end of tomorrow.”

“I know.”

“I am telling you so that you know I am not going to be able to help you with it,” said the girl, “and so that you do not think I did not notice.”

“You noticed.”

“I noticed at about the second hour,” said the girl, “and I have been sitting here since the ninth working out whether to tell you before you told me, and I decided not to, because I would only have been about a day in front of you and there is no use in that.”

She got up off the step with the leg wrong and went into the wash house, and Ning Yan stood in the lane for a while with the water running and the lamp at the tower burning four miles up a road.

Nine counties.

She had wanted four stages and she had four. She had spent nine days in the winter not eating to get a sentence out about the fourth and it had gone nowhere and was never going to go anywhere, and now she had said a sentence about the sixth and it had gone out bound-wide and told every county between here and the marker post exactly where to send a man with a reason.

She walked back up the road in the dark and did the sum and it came out the same both times.

Two hundred and forty loads. Six weeks. Forty-one stamps. Four loads a county hour, every time, for six weeks. A woman of about sixty at a water channel who decided not to ask. A man at a board who chalked nothing. Eleven men at a lane and nine men at a furnace and a girl with a crooked leg and three washerwomen who could not have kept this quiet if they had wanted to.

And one sentence, out loud, in the ordinary voice, at the fourth hour of the eighty-first day, in a loft with the lamp turned away.
