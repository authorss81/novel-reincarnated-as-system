# A Hired Guard

The contractor put a second man on the train at the end of the first week of the hire, and paid him by the day out of his own pocket, and told her so.

“Tan,” he said. “Twenty coppers like you. He is not faster than you and he is twice the size and he does not open his hand.”

Tan was about thirty and built like a man who had moved stone when he was young and had not stopped. He had been doing the road nine years and he carried a bar shorter than her staff and he walked on the off side, and on the first morning he did the whole descent without saying a word, and at the bottom he said, “You are on the wrong side.”

“There are two sides.”

“You are on the wrong side of your own train,” said Tan. “You are on the side where the near horse is.”

## What She Had Lost

The wheel went on the fourth day of the second week and it went a quarter of a mile before it got there.

She felt it through the ground rather than saw it. That was the thing, and it took her a count and a half to understand what it was, and what it was was that she had been walking this road for a fortnight with a hole in her where the road had been, and she had not known the hole was there until the hole told her.

The third cart from the back. The drag had come its pin on the wet stone and the near wheel had begun to lean at about a quarter of a mile and she had been looking down the train the whole way and had not known any of it.

She knew the ground. She did not know the lean.

“Left wheel, third cart,” said Tan, at a shout, and did not wait for her.

He had the wheel off the road before it went into the ditch on the inside bend. It came down off the hub with forty pounds of stone on it and about eleven inches of clearance and the river was four feet below the ditch lip and it was moving fast because of the thaw higher up and it was the fourth week of the season and there had been rain on the mountain for nine days.

Tan got it. Then he got the drag pin back in with a bar and a thumb and stood there breathing hard.

Then he looked at her.

“Say it.”

“I did not see it.”

“You did not see it,” said Tan, “because you have not seen anything on this road since the day you started it, and you have been walking it twice a day and looking at the horse.”

“I have seen the horse.”

“You have seen the horse,” said Tan, “and you have seen the ground, and there is a difference and you know what it is because you are standing in it.”

## The Difference

She had known what it was before he finished the sentence and she said it out loud on a country road with eight carts standing behind her and a river going four feet below the ditch.

“It used to tell me,” she said.

“Told you what.”

“Where a wheel was going,” said Ning Yan. “A quarter of a mile before it got there. Five weeks of this and it was the best thing in my body, and I spent it on a man in good boots in about nine seconds on a road I am paid to walk, and now I am a guard who can tell you what a load weighs.”

Tan looked at her for a second and a half.

“Aye,” he said, “well. Now you are a guard who can tell you what a load weighs and there is nine of us on this road and I am on it, and if it happens again I will be there and I would rather be there than at the weigh-station arguing with a boy.”

He shouldered the bar.

“Do not tell the contractor. He will take you off the road and put you at the shed counting loads and it is a worse job and there is not a quarter of it in it.”

“I am not going to lie to the contractor about a wheel.”

“Then tell him the wheel and not the reason,” said Tan. “The wheel is the truth and the wheel is what he would have to pay for, and the reason is your business and you have got exactly one piece of paper in this county that says anything about you and I would not put it in a shed foreman's mouth.”

## Licence Scope

She got it in the end, and it took four days.

The county examined it at the third hour on a wet morning, at the side counter, with the brass rule, and it took four days because escort work is not a name anybody in that office had to put in a book, and because the question underneath all of it was whether walking beside a cart train on the Lowwater road was paid work outside the crew under Clause Four.

It is. A clerk of four years found the word *escort* in a tariff of the district and brought it to the registrar, and the registrar read the scope line, and then read the second condition, which was the amended one, and then looked up at her over the brass rule for about a count and a half.

“It is inside your scope.”

“It is.”

“It also goes on your schedule by certification like everything else, which means a Hall copyist writes it down and a Hall clerk certifies it and a maintaining member stamps it, and you write it out yourself in your own hand every day.”

“Yes.”

“Twenty coppers and a quarter of a train.”

“Yes.”

“A quarter share, worked at the rate of a two-armed woman, is a figure that will be questioned by somebody in about nine weeks who has no reason to know anything about you.”

“That is the whole of it,” said Ning Yan.

Sun He wrote it on the side of the return and turned the sheet so she could see it, and did not say anything about her arm.

## What She Was Paid

The arithmetic came out where the arithmetic always came out, and she did it on the lane at the ninth night hour for about eleven minutes and got the same answer twice.

Twenty coppers a day and a quarter of a train's share. On a good week the share was about a copper and three quarters.

Her load rate at the lane behind the well was twelve coppers. She was getting, on a good week, about twenty-two, which was more than any load day she had ever worked in her life.

And the rate per hour of it was a third of a two-armed guard's, because a two-armed man on that road carries the bar and walks, and she carried the bar with her left and her right was out about one time in three, and the quarter was divided by eight and then by the days the road took.

She was being paid as a licence holder and worked as a woman with one arm, and both of those were on paper, and the only one of the two that anybody would ever read was the one on paper.

“Nobody is cheating you,” said Yuan.

“No.”

“Everybody at that board is being straight with you and the number is straight and the rate is straight and it is a fair rate for the work.”

“It is a fair rate for the work,” said Ning Yan, “and the work is a half of me on a good week and a third of me on a bad one, and in about nine weeks a clerk upstairs is going to see a column of hours on a furnace schedule and compare it with the wage at the district rate for a guard, and he is going to ask.”

“He is going to ask and there is going to be an answer.”

“What answer.”

“The true one,” said Yuan. “Which is that a man with two arms would have taken this road in about nine weeks and you have taken it in fourteen, and the county has no way to write that and the county is not wrong.”

## The Thing She Had Not Expected

She had not expected Tan.

That was all it was. She had been on this road for six weeks, after the day under the bridge, working a four-mile road out of Lowwater and back with a stone lane and a county hour and a four-mile walk up a mountain, and in all of that time the number of people who had done anything for her without being asked had been one, and one of them was dead.

Tan did not know what she was. He did not know what she was carrying on the licence or against it or on it, and he had not asked, and he had not looked at her arm except once, on the first morning, when he had said *that is a right arm that is not always there* and had then gone and walked twenty feet in front of her and never mentioned it again.

On the fourth day he took a wheel that was going to put eight carts and a river in the same place.

“You did not have to tell me not to tell the contractor,” said Ning Yan, at the second night hour that day, on the steps of the shed where they put the bar in.

“No.”

“Why not.”

“Tan,” said the big guard, “is not a man who asks a woman why she cannot see a wheel. I have three sisters and I have been wrong about all of them and I am right about this one.”

He put the bar in the rack.

“You are a better guard than the last three they put on this road,” he said, “and you will not be on it in a fortnight if you keep working like that, and if the contractor asks me I will tell him the wheel and not the reason, and I will do that whether you asked me to or not.”

She went up the four miles.

## The Lamp

The lamp in the cage on the fourth landing was burning when she came up, and the copying room door was shut, and there was a clerk's satchel on the bench that was not there in the morning.

She did not go in.

That was the whole of what she had given herself about Feng Lushi, and she was keeping it: she did not go in, and she did not ask, and she did not stand on the fourth stair to look at a shut door the way a man over a tanner's had stood on it six times.

He had a hundredweight of coal coming up the road in about four days and a wage at the district rate and a chair with a fire in it and twenty-two years in his chest, and she had been in that room four times in nine days and she was not going to be in it a fifth time to look at a man who was not asking her for anything.

She went up to the loft and lay on the mat.

“Tan caught the wheel.”

“Tan caught the wheel.”

“Next time I do not know if he will be on the far side.”

“No,” said Yuan. “Next time you will be a guard with a bar in your left hand and one arm out about one time in three, on a road with a river under it, and you will not know about the wheel before it arrives, and that is the price of a body on the Lowwater road and you paid it in nine seconds with a yes.”

She lay with it for a while.

“Do not say it was the right thing.”

“I was not going to say it was the right thing,” said Yuan. “I was going to say that in about four weeks you are going to see a wheel going and know about it again, because practice rebuilds substance and cannot rebuild order, and you will not know at the time whether it has come back or whether you have simply got lucky.”

“Which will it be.”

“I do not know,” said Yuan, “and I am not going to lie to you about it tonight, because that is the one thing I have never once done to you and I am not starting at the end of a road.”
