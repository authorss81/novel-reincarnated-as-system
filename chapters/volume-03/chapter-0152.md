# Slate and Chalk

The board behind the well had been wiped and not chalked on four days out of nine, and the reason it had been wiped and not chalked was that there was nothing to chalk.

Da Shan wrote the day up along the top in charcoal because he had always done it, and then he stood at the side of a frame with his back to it for about a minute, and then he went and had a smoke, and there was one man at the fourth table on a bad morning and none at the first.

She had eleven weeks of the lane in her at the thaw and four weeks left in it now, and she did the division on the step on the morning of the one hundred and fortieth day, standing up, in the cold, with the numbers going out of her head before she got them all down.

## Two Days a Week

The carts came on two days and then on one, and the one was not fixed, and the carts came when there was stone in Lowwater to move and stopped coming when there was not, and what there was in Lowwater to move in the first month of the cold was not much.

The water channel paid twelve coppers a load and a load was a mat of broken stone on one shoulder from the fourth table to the cut, and a woman with one arm got two loads where a man with two got three, and the gap between those two numbers was where she lived.

On a two-day week she made about a third of what she had made in the autumn. On a one-day week she made about a sixth. There was no arrangement of that arithmetic that made it a living, and there had not been one since the thaw, and she had been living on the furnace and the tithe and about eleven weeks of savings, which was not a plan.

She had thought about a second schedule. That was a thing she had done twice before in this year and it had worked both times.

## What There Was East of Lowwater

She had walked out of the town four days before, and had not walked back until the light went, and she went out again twice in between and worked the water channel on the days in between.

Three miles east on the Verge road, where the road leaves the flat and goes up into the cut the ash came out of in the summer, there was a quarry, and it had been there a great deal longer than the road, and there were eleven men in it and a hoist worked by four of them by hand.

It was the same stone as the fourth table behind the well. That was the first thing she established and it took her about an hour, standing at the lip looking down, and it is the reason she went back.

A man will lie to you about what a load weighs. A man has very little room to lie to you about what a load is, if you have carried the stone yourself and you know what the stone looks like when it is split and when it is not.

## A Full Day at It

She stood at the lip of that quarry for a whole day and then came back and worked the water channel for two more days and then went out again, because she wanted three days of watching before she put a shoulder to anything, and because on the first day she had watched the four men on the rope and understood in about four minutes that they were the whole of it and everything else was carrying.

A hoist is four men and a rope and a frame. Four men haul it up, two men swing the block, one man sets it, one man calls the number.

The load comes off the cut on a sledge with two runners and no wheel on the near side. A man shoves it down the grade with a bar behind his shoulder. A woman shoves it with the same bar and gets less of it down and is slower, and not slower in a way anybody can put in a book.

The pay was eighteen coppers a load. Not twelve. Eighteen, because it was the cold and because the road was shut and because eleven men in a quarry in the first month of the cold is eleven men who would otherwise not be there.

The foreman was about fifty and called Bo, and he asked her one question, on the first day, which was whether her arm was always like that, and she said it was not, and he said *right* and went back to the block, and that was the entire conversation either of them had with the other all week.

## The Slate

They paid on a slate in a wooden frame on the wall of a hut, and the foreman wrote the number at the rope in his own hand as each load came up, and did not offer her a copy and did not say he would keep the slate.

That was on the fourth day and she noticed it and did not understand it yet, and it was going to cost her three weeks of winter and a hole in a column before she did.

She went back out on the fifth day and said yes.

## Wanting

It was not a large want.

That is the part of it she could not have explained to anybody in the quarter, and would have been ashamed of if she had tried. She had not wanted a wage for four months.

What she had wanted was the eight hundred jin off the back of her licence. She had wanted the fourth hour of a morning to mean something. She had wanted a room in a tower with somebody else's lamp in it.

None of those was a wage and none of them had been available, and she had gone on working without any of them, because a body with three things at the ninth hour does not stop to want a fourth.

The quarry was at the third hour of the afternoon and three miles east and paid eighteen.

And on the fifth day, standing at the lip with the rope going and four men hauling and the light coming off the cut about two hours early because it was the turn of the cold, she felt something she had not felt since the thaw.

She wanted a wage. Not a licence and not a stage and not a day when nobody is looking for her. Money, in a count, at the end of a week, for loads she had carried.

She went down on the sledge that afternoon with her right arm open and her left taking the bar, and she put down six loads before the light went, and Bo wrote the number at the rope every time and did not say anything at all.

## Nine Men

She told them on the third evening, at the ninth hour, on the floor, because nine men who have not been told where a crew member goes in the cold will find out by looking at her hands.

“Three miles east,” she said. “The quarry on the Verge road. Six days. I will be at the third hour and off at the light.”

Gao asked the question that mattered, which was the one he had asked in the summer about the word on the front of the schedule, and he asked it the same way, standing at the end of a row with his arms folded.

“Under your licence?”

“Annexed to it,” she said. “Same as the lane. The copyist certifies it and it goes up with the quarter.”

“Then it is in the book.”

“It is in the book,” said Ning Yan.

That was all of it. Nine men looked at her and one of them nodded and the ninth hour came on, and that was the end of the notice and it was the correct amount of notice.

## What She Did Not Say

There was a thing in the room she did not say, and it had been in the room since the day before.

She had refused a piece of paper in a county office, in front of two witnesses, and a clerk had written a man's name into a Warden's book because a form asked him to. In about three weeks a copy of her member's sheet would come down that same road, with the schedule and the annex on it, and it would carry the whole of it in the ordinary place.

Nine men read their own schedule. That was the phrase. She had used it against Feng Lushi in the summer as a thing that was true and unfavourable to somebody, and it was true now and it was unfavourable to her.

She could tell them in her own words on a furnace floor in the next nine days, or she could wait three weeks and let a clerk's hand do it in words she had not chosen.

She worked it out in about eleven seconds, on the floor, with the hot coming up out of the grate.

She was going to wait.

Not because she was ashamed of it, and not because it did not matter, and not because she thought nine men would take it badly. Because a thing said in her own words is a thing she has to carry, and a thing said in a clerk's hand is a thing nine men have already got a shape for before she opens her mouth, and there is exactly one of those two that she can put down.

She put it down.

## The Road Back

Three miles east to the west, at about the fourth hour, with the light gone off the cut behind her and the hoist stopping about a quarter of a mile off, and then four miles up the mountain to the crew quarters, and the third flight at the top of it with three counts on it.

That was the shape of a day in the first month of the cold and she would be living in that shape for the next eleven weeks.

“A day,” said Yuan.

“A day.”

“It is four and a half hours on a furnace floor, eighteen coppers a load, three miles east, a hoist that four men work, and a flight of stair that costs you three counts,” he said. “And you have never in your life worked a shorter day than that and you are describing it as if it were the other thing.”

“It is the first day,” said Ning Yan, “where I have known in the morning what I am doing in the afternoon.”

He did not have anything to say to that.

It was the seventh of the month and the monthly attendance was seven days off.

On the morning of that day she would stand in a county office at the third hour, in front of two witnesses and a clerk and a man who had bought one certified copy of her schedule in eleven weeks. The county would read her column out loud with him in the room. And there was a line on the sheet for the day after that, in his own hand, in a book that was not hers.

She had known none of that on the fourth day of the thaw.

“The right hand,” said Yuan.

“It was open twice today.”

“I know,” he said. “I have been able to see it go since the first year, in a body I do not have, and I am not going to charge you for it and I am not going to stop.”
