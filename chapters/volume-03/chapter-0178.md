# Straight

It was the first week of the deep cold, and the fire in the crew quarters went down twice between the fourth hour and the light and had to be fed again both times, and the window had a board in it that had been there since before she came and a gap under it that a person could put two fingers through and feel the outside on.

Ten people slept in twelve bunks.

She was in the second bunk with nine men breathing in a room that had held twelve three winters ago, and the quarters were four miles up a hill of a thousand feet above a town where one light was burning in a tower, and she could see it from the stair.

She looked at it every night now. It had burned every night since the coal came, and the thing that went up that tower's four flights of stair this quarter went up in a district clerk's arms and came down in his arms empty, because the man who owned the lamp had asked for the arrangement in writing in the middle of the autumn and had not been refused and had not been thanked for it.

## The Seventh Hour

The fire was going and nine men came in off the shift and the room filled up the way it filled up, which was loud for about a minute and then was not.

She had the licence in her left hand. It was folded in three and it came out of the oilcloth under her coat that morning and it had been out of the oilcloth for two days now, which was the longest it had ever been out, and she had carried it down the hill and up it and to the square and back and had not folded it into the oilcloth once.

The nail was between the second and third fingers of her left hand and she had it standing on the head of the board above the foot of the bunk, with her arm flat against the board and her whole weight holding it there, which is the only way to hold a nail with a hand that cannot make the turn at the end of a stroke.

She had two arms' worth of practice at nailing.

She had one arm.

She brought the hammer up and she did not hit it the way she had hit the fifth nail on the pine, because a nail that is a third of the way home wants a tap and not a blow, and she had learned that on a scrap of soft pine at the foot of a bunk two nights ago at the cost of an inch and a half of split wood.

She struck it.

It went into the board about a third of the way and stopped, the way they do, and she took the hammer down and looked at it, because the one thing a person learns about a nail in a lifetime of nailing is that when it stops like that it is asking for the other half and not for more of the same.

She struck it again.

It went in the rest of the way with a sound in a room with ten people in it that was smaller than she had expected, and the head of it sat flat against the wood, and it was not crooked, and there was no way on earth of correcting it now and no need to.

She put the hammer down on the frame of the bunk.

“That is straight,” said Gao.

“Yes.”

“You are not going to say anything about it.”

“No,” said Ning Yan. “It is a nail and it is on a board and it took about nine seconds. If I say something about it then the next one will take longer.”

Gao looked at it for a second, and then went and fed the fire, which was the thing he did at the seventh hour on a night in the first week of the deep cold whether anything had happened in the room or not.

## The Second Nail

She did the second one on the board above the foot of the same bunk, at about nine inches along, and it went straight too.

She did not do it in one stroke. It took two and a half and there was a small white mark on her thumb afterwards and she looked at that for longer than she looked at the nail.

Then she brought it to the front and turned it over with her right hand and held it out where nine men could see it, and it is not a thing a person needs to show anybody.

**Licence to Practise Past Registration.** Clause Four. Her name at the top. **Condensing nine** in her own writing, with the defect under it and a new line under that which said it restored nothing.

Seven conditions on the back, small, in the county's hand.

The eight hundred jin standing as the seventh and the last thing on the paper.

Three holes in a stage column that read one, two, three, five, six, seven, nine.

She put it up above the foot of her own bunk at about head height, on two nails, at about the fourth hour in the morning in the spring of the year before this one.

It was straight.

She did not nail the other two. She put the two nails back in the cloth on the shelf at her head and the hammer on the frame and the oilcloth in her coat, and the Clay Plate went back against her ribs under the place where the oilcloth had been, and the first licence stayed rolled in the lining of her coat where it had been for two days.

Behind her the nine men went to sleep. Nobody made anything of it. A man at a fire said something about the wind and a man at the window said something about the board, and that was the whole of what the crew of the Qinghe furnace had to say about the licensed one putting a licence up over her own bunk at the seventh hour in the first week of the deep cold.

## What Was Going Up

Two days before, on the crate, there had gone out of this county four pieces of paper in a strap.

One of them was a referral with a number on it, made out of a low room at the second hour in front of twenty people, and it carried her name and her stages in order and her hours, and it said that a field man of a Warden's office could not do the thing he had been sent to do inside the bound without an instrument this county does not hold.

He had held up the other three of those four papers as well. He had sat at a table in this county and read out a hearing sheet in the middle of the winter and said, in three sentences and without being asked, that he was not lying, and he had been right about a hole in a column in the summer when nobody in nine counties had been able to contradict him.

And it had gone up a stair past every office she had ever stood in front of.

The Hall's board, and the furnace floor, and the wash house, and the county office on the square, and the quota office that had given her a second clearance and never looked at her, and the three men who had put a seal on a ceiling above five and gone quiet about it.

All of them. Past all of them, into a county that stamps and copies and sends upward to something it has never once named.

“Is there a name on it,” she said.

“There is a name on it.”

“Can you read it.”

“No,” said Yuan. “I have never been able to read that office and I have been able to read everything else in this county for two years, and I have told you four times that I cannot and I am not going to say it a fifth time to be different about it.”

“All right.”

“You knew it would go past all of them.”

“I knew,” said Ning Yan. “I have known since the middle of the winter, and I told a man in a spring that I was not going to stop him, and I did not stop him, and I put my name on a piece of paper in a room with two witnesses in it, and this is what that was for.”

## How Much Was Left

She asked it at about the ninth hour on the mat, with the room asleep and the fire down to a bed of ash and the licence on the wall two feet above her head with its head side to the room.

“How much of it is there,” said Ning Yan.

“About ten days.”

She lay in the cold and did not say anything for about as long as it takes to count nine.

That was the whole of it. Ten days. Not eleven weeks of winter, and not a quarter, and not a figure with a decimal in it — about ten days of a voice in her head, twice a day, in hours, and never idled, because those were the conditions she had written herself in the spring and she had kept them.

Ten days, and about six weeks of winter still to come in it, and the voice was going to be gone in the first week of the third month of the cold and the season was going to carry on for a month after that, with nothing in her head to carry it.

She did the arithmetic, all of it, the way she had been doing it since the first week of the year, and it came out where it always came out.

A licence nailed straight over her own bunk with seven conditions on the back and the eight hundred jin as the last of them, and nothing above Condensing nine on the front of it anywhere in the world.

A stage column with three holes in nine lines, and nine counties that had it in order.

A reading done and a referral gone up past every office she had ever stood in front of, and a district that does not hold the instrument either, and an answer space on a shelf by a door with nothing in it.

A petition with a number and a bar on it in the ordinary hand, entered on the day after the referral went up, and the bar saying that the answer to it is not this office's to write.

About a hundred and forty coppers under a board in a wash house, and oil, and a girl's four coppers a week to a thaw with no date on it, and four loads a week at nine coppers going onto a wall behind a well.

A crew of nine at the near end and a cold end at the far end and a girl at a trough and a keeper in a tower of sixty-two who is warm.

And about ten days.

There was nothing left in the calendar. There was a barred petition in it and a licence on a wall and a date by the district's hand and a road that opens in the spring, and every other day between this one and the first day of the thaw had nothing in it at all.

## What She Did Not Do

He waited.

That was the thing he had been not doing since the middle of the spring and he went on not doing it. There was no version of the night in which he asked her for anything.

Once in the spring she had said out loud to a man over a tanner's door that she was not going to stop him, and she had said it about him and not about what was in her head, and she was not going to say it about that now that the figure was bad.

She had the Ledger to order. It had been hers to order since the middle of the winter — not his to refuse and hers to spend, the standing changing hands in a winter room and neither of them having chosen it and both of them meaning it — and she did not order it.

If she had ordered it he would have read the man over a tanner's door in a breath and told her what was in his room and what he had written on the nineteenth and what he had not written, and it would have cost her more than she had, and she would not have known how much until it was gone.

She did not order it.

She lay in the second bunk in the dark in the first week of the deep cold with the fire down to a bed of ash and the licence nailed straight over her head with nothing on the front of it above nine, and ten people asleep in a room that held twelve bunks, and one light burning four miles off down in a town where the stair is carried this quarter by another man's hands.

She did not tell him to stop.
