# Nine in Her Own Hand

She came up on the thirty-fifth day with a clearance in a certified copy in her coat and a certified annex and a certified schedule in oilcloth and got wet on the four-mile road and came into a Hall with no roof at the west end of it with a table under a dry awning and three men standing at the table who had not come to the awning for eleven weeks.

Deng Chao had a sheet in front of him and a brush and the clearing cup from the Hall's own side, and Pei An had the Hall's stamp in his hand instead of Lu Kang, which was an arrangement and not an accident.

“Read it,” said Lu Kang.

## The Sheet

Deng Chao read it.

He read everything out. He had read the terms of her pending filing out loud in the winter in a corridor in front of a queue, and he read a season's schedule out loud on the twentieth day of the month, and he read a line in a registrar's bundle out loud on a Thursday in the morning, and he was going to read this one out loud because he was the one who did it and there was nobody else in the building to stop him.

“A relicensing, made under Clause Four of the Servant Advancement Settlement, by a servant-born of Qinghe who holds a licence to practise past registration at the county of Lowwater, countersigned at the third hour of a day in the middle of the spring.”

“Nine,” said Ning Yan.

He came to that before she had to say it, because she had written it on the sheet in her own hand at the top, under the heading, before she had come up the mountain, in a left-handed slant that no clerk in nine counties can read quickly.

*Condensing, stage nine, is the ceiling of this licence.*

“Condensing, stage nine, is the ceiling of this licence,” said Deng Chao, “in her own hand, and dated the twenty-eighth day of the first weeks of summer, four miles from this table, alone, on a mat.”

“Yes,” said Ning Yan.

“Not Foundation.”

“Foundation is barred on the same sheet without a second relicensing, in my hand, and I have written it there because it has to be written there and not left off.”

Lu Kang looked at the sheet.

“Show me the Foundation line.”

She turned the sheet round and put her left hand flat beside it so he could read the line and could see whose hand it was in, which was the whole of what he had wanted and the reason she had put it under his hand.

*No petition for the Foundation without relicensing. The second relicensing requires a schedule this county does not keep past the boundary. Ceiling of this licence: Condensing, stage nine.*

“A schedule it does not keep past the boundary,” said Lu Kang.

“That is on the county's own form and I have had it read at me twice by a clerk who was not trying to be kind to me.”

“Then it is on the sheet and it stays on the sheet, and it is in your hand, and it is the same sentence you wrote on a county sheet in the winter that killed our writ.” He did not say the word *writ* in any way at all. “Is there anything else on this sheet that is your own hand and not a clerk's.”

“There are two lines.”

She turned it round again and gave him the second page.

## The Two Lines

The first line was in her own left hand and it was the same eleven words she had put on a county sheet at the third desk in a corridor in the winter, in front of a brass rule and a man who had offered to show her the original.

*Aperture cracked through; stabilised at the crack; permanent.*

The second line was a new one, and it was also in her own hand, and she had written it at the fourth hour of the previous night on the loft mat with the lamp turned away, and she had thought about it for two days first.

*The defect restores nothing to the aperture. It is entered here again because this is a new document and a document carries what it carries.*

“I want the second line read out,” said Pei An.

“Read it out,” said Lu Kang.

Deng Chao read it out. Then he read the first line out again, because Deng Chao read everything twice, and the two of them sat in the room together on one page of a piece of paper in a building that had no roof, and they were the same eleven words she had signed in a corridor in the winter and the difference between them was a new sentence underneath.

“All right,” said Lu Kang.

He did not say *do not write that* and he did not say *that is not necessary* and he did not say anything at all about the fact that she had spent a day of a season writing a sentence for a registrar to read about an aperture that has not changed in a year.

“It is entered,” he said. “Both lines. Go on.”

## Three Marks

They did not do it quickly.

That was the thing she had not been ready for, and she had gone up the mountain in the spring braced for three men putting three marks down in about a minute and a half and being finished.

Pei An read the whole sheet first, at the west table, standing, holding it, turning it about a quarter turn to get the light off the wet stone.
He read the clearance and the schedule and the annex and the four clauses and the two of hers, and he checked the hour on the clearance against the hour in the quota book entry that Deng Chao had copied out and brought up in his coat, and he checked the name on the certification line and did not say anything about the order of it, which she noticed and which she had thought about since.

Then Deng Chao read it all out again to Lu Kang.

Then Lu Kang asked her four questions.

Not about her intentions. She had braced for her intentions.

“Who certified the annex.”

“The Hall's clerk, at the second hour of the twenty-seventh.”

“Who stamped it.”

“A maintaining member of this Hall, in person, standing, after he had read the whole of it.”

“Who keeps the quota book the clearance is in.”

“An office at the back of the furnace building of this sect, and I have never once been in a room with a man in it who keeps it, and he signed it without looking up.”

“Why.”

“Because he does not hurry and does not weight a figure and had been asked by three estates to do both this year.”

Lu Kang looked at her for a moment.

“Good,” he said. “A schedule that is kept by a man who does not hurry is worth more than a schedule that is kept by a man who wants something from it.”

Then he said the thing.

## The Thing They Were Doing It For

“Before I put my hand on this,” said Lu Kang, “I am going to say out loud, in this corridor, in front of two men and a clerk, why I am putting my hand on it, because in about a year one of the three of us is going to be asked why, and I would rather the answer was in a corridor than in a bundle.”

He put his hands behind his back.

“I am putting my mark on a ceiling above the one my own writ died under. Deng Chao, Pei An, this is a harder signature than anything I have put on anything in this Hall in forty years, and I want the two of you to hear me say that it is harder, and I want her to hear it, and then I want all three of us to put our marks down anyway.”

“Why,” said Pei An.

“Because in the spring we had two choices and one of them was better and one of them was lawful, and we did the lawful one.” He did not stop. “That was right. I would do it again. What I could not do in the spring was be certain which choice was which. I am certain now.”

He turned round and looked down the length of a Hall with no roof.

“There is a man in Lowwater who has paid a county clerk for a certified copy of our season's schedule and has read it at a public counter and has said two true things about her terms in a county room in front of a queue, and Sun He has written it up, and the line went up to us in a bundle on a Thursday, and none of the three of us has answered it.”
“There is no version of the three of us that is going to answer it.”

“Then you could surrender her to the office that asked,” said Pei An.

“No,” said Lu Kang. “Because the office that asked is outside this bound and the answer it wants is not an answer, it is a member of the furnace crew of Qinghe delivered with her schedule in a hand, and the moment that member is handed over with a schedule, this Hall is a book that has been opened. And then there is no furnace schedule in nine counties that anybody can rely on, including the six that are honest, and including hers in particular in about four weeks.”

He turned back.

“She is inside our schedule at nine. She is examinable in it, by any office that comes with a stamp and a reason, and every hour of her is on a sheet we keep and can be produced with a finger.”

“That is the same sentence I said to her on the twentieth day and I am not going to say it twice in a corridor, and it is the reason three marks are going on a piece of paper today and the reason nobody in this building is going to be able to pretend in about a year that we did not know what we were doing.”

Nobody said anything for a moment.

The water in the low corner had a skin on it where the wind got under the door.

Lu Kang took his hands from behind his back, and read the sheet once more himself, and signed it at the ceiling line, and then signed it again at the foot, and then Deng Chao signed it, and then Pei An, who had been holding the stamp since she came in and put it on the sheet himself and lifted it and set it back.

## The Third Time

She looked at the three marks and at her own line above them for a moment and did not say anything.

She had written a ceiling down on herself three times.

The first had been a document refusing to let her practise at all, and she had signed it at the third desk in a corridor while a man read a refusal out to her. The second had been a licence with five on it and a defect in her own hand, and she had signed that while a stamp was about to come down on it in a county room in the rain.

This one said nine.

And it was her hand. It was going to go down four miles to a man with a registrar's stamp and a queue, and the whole of what the third one bought her was four numbers she had not yet reached and a set of hours that a man with a quota book had put in a book in front of her, and everything that had happened to her body in fourteen months was still exactly where it had been.

She had written a ceiling down on herself and the third time had been higher than the other two, and the writing it down had taken was the same as it had always been.

“You have got it,” said Yuan.

“I have got it.”

“Say it.”

“I have got a ceiling of nine in my own hand on a sheet with three sponsors' marks on it and a clearance in a book four miles from here,” said Ning Yan, “and I have got exactly the same body I had in the winter.”
“The aperture is the same aperture and the arm is the same arm and the fourth stage of my life is still on no paper in nine counties, and I have been up this road twice to get a number written down and both times I have paid for it with something I cannot get back.”

“That is what it is.”

“That is what it is,” said Ning Yan, “and I am going to write nine on this sheet and I am going to sign it, and I am not going to thank any of them, because they have not helped me. They have decided that the cheapest thing available to them this year is a member they can account for, and that is not a kindness and it is not a sponsorship and it is not anything I am going to be able to repay.”

She took the brush in her left hand and signed it.

## What She Did Not Say

Nobody said anything about the book.

That was the arrangement, and it held in a corridor with a rail down the side of it, and she stood at the west table with a sheet with three marks on it and did not say one word about nine households struck in one stroke over eleven years, and neither did Deng Chao, and neither did Pei An, and neither did Lu Kang, and the clerk at the copy bench thirty feet away copied something else.

The three registers of Qinghe and of three other counties still read *reconciled* for a boy of thirteen who was taken for the kiln and who was alive, on a list, four days' road past a marker post with a white stone on it.
She had gone into the low corner of that Hall and put a schedule of a road into a clerk's hand and kept the road's reason, and it had bought her a signature and it had cost her something she had never been able to name and was never going to be able to name, and the word in that book was a lie with her hand under it.

“Go down,” said Lu Kang. “Take it down yourself. Do not send it with the courier.”

“Why not the courier.”

“Because a courier will carry it to the county door and put it on a table in the ante-room and go home,” said Lu Kang, “and there are two legs left and one of them is a man's hand and one of them is four days away, and I would rather a schedule went to that registrar in a woman's hand than in a boy's, and I have never had an opinion about that before this morning and I do not have one now.”

She got the oilcloth off the bench and wrapped the whole thing — the application, the clearance, the schedule, the annex, the two of her lines and three marks and a stamp — flat against her ribs, above the plate, in the place that had been going soft for six months.

The win was in her chest on the four-mile road down in the sun and she felt it the whole way, and it was a real thing and she let it be a real thing for about half of it.

And about halfway down, at the turn at the willows, past the weigh-station where a road four miles long began, the short breath came up at a rise she had gone up forty times, and she had to stand at the side of a cart track with a bundle of paper against her chest and wait for it.

She was twenty. It was the first weeks of summer. in the light of a morning that had cost her four marks and a clearance and a sentence she had written for a registrar to read about an aperture that had not healed by one letter.
