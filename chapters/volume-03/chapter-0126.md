# The Certified Copy

She was eight feet from the counter when he put the coin on the wood.

The queue at that hour ran out of the door and along the step and down into the wet, and she was fourth in it with the filing copy out and the county hour open and Sun He at the table on the other side of the room doing what Sun He did, which was the thing he had been doing at that table for nineteen years.

The man at the counter was not in the queue. He had come in behind her, which was not possible, because anybody coming in behind a queue in this office went to the end of it, and everybody in the queue had watched him walk past eleven people and go to the certified-copy slot at the near end of the same counter.

He put a coin on open wood. It was a county office and the wood was open wood, and there was no man at that slot, because the certified-copy slot is a slot and not a man and the clerk who serves it comes when he comes.

“Certified copy of the furnace schedule of Qinghe for the season just closed,” said the man. “The one that came down with the quarter's crate.”

“That is the one.”

“The whole of it.”

“The whole of it, with the Hall's mark on it.”

## The Reading

It took four minutes for the copy to come and the room did not stop, because nothing in this office has ever stopped, and she stood fourth in a queue eight feet from a man who was about to hold in his own hands a certified copy of a sheet with a column of her hours in it.

He paid. He took the copy. And then he did the thing that told her everything about what he was, which was that he stood there in a public office with a queue behind him and read the whole thing, from the top, at the speed of a man reading a document he intends to act on.

The header. The Hall's mark, pressed and lifted. The Hall clerk's certification, and the name of the clerk, and the hour.

Then the nine households, which took him a while, and then her column, which took him longer, because her line was complete and her hours were in her own hand at every single entry and there is a difference between a line of figures and a line of figures written by one person for four months, and he read all of it.

Then he turned the sheet round to the stage column, which is not on the schedule. It is on the filing. He had the schedule; the schedule carried the column of hours and the column of hours carried, in its own ruled column, the stage each entry was worked at.

One. Two. Three.

And then nothing, the width of two ruled lines.

And then five.

She watched him find it.

A very small thing on a very large sheet, and no reason on earth that a man standing at a public counter should stop on it, and he stopped on it the way a man stops on a thing he has been sent for.

He did not look up. He did not look at her.

He turned to the clerk who had come along from the certified-copy slot, and he spoke at the ordinary volume of a man making an observation in a room.

## The First True Thing

“This is a filing whose terms are written in stages.”

The clerk was a young man with the copying calluses on two fingers and he had not been in the room when her licence was stamped, and he said, “Sir?” in the way of a clerk who does not understand a sentence and intends to.

“The licence behind this sheet,” said the man. “Its terms are expressed in stages. Practice at or below five. No Foundation without relicensing. A ceiling entered in her own hand.” He put a finger on the schedule without touching it, the way people do. “Those are all terms about what her body is, and the county keeps the body in a column, and the column on this sheet says one, two, three, five.”

“That is what it says,” said the clerk.

“There is a space where the fourth should be.”

“There is,” said the clerk, and then, being a clerk, and having been in that office for four years and not having been here when the ruling was made: “An absence is not a breach. That is in the margin of the book from this spring. A filing fails when a term is broken and an empty line is not a broken line.”

The man nodded.

“That is right,” he said. “That is exactly right, and it is the registrar's ruling and it stands, and I am not here to argue with it. I am here to say the next thing about it.”

He put the certified copy flat on the counter and squared it, which nobody does to a county document.

“The next stage has to go into that column,” he said. “Whatever it is, wherever it is taken, on whatever schedule, at whatever hour. It has to go into that column or it is not on the schedule, and the schedule is the thing the licence is hung on.”

“A paper that is written about stages and does not carry the stage is a paper that has failed one of its own terms, and the registrar who let that by would be letting by a paper that says something about her that the record does not say.”

The young clerk looked at the certified copy for a moment.

“That is a fair reading,” he said.

“It is more than a fair reading,” said the man. “It is what the paper says. I am not being clever. I have been reading the same clause in other counties for six years and every one of them hangs the licence off a stage column for exactly this reason.”

## The Second True Thing

Eight feet away, fourth in the queue, Ning Yan did not move.

She had four things available to her. She could have stepped out of the queue and come back in. She could have said something. She could have gone up to the counter and put her hand on the certified copy and asked what right a stranger had to read a schedule she worked under.

And the only thing any of those four things was going to achieve, in a room with nine people at the counter and eleven in the queue and a registrar at the table, was that a clerk would have had to write down that a licensee objected.

That was not available to her. It was the fourth thing and it was the only one that was, and it was the thing she had been doing for eleven months.

He turned back to the counter and put the certified copy in front of the clerk, and squared the receipt against the edge of the wood, and did not hand it over.

“There is one more,” he said, “and it is not for you, and I would rather say it to the room than to the man it is about.”

The clerk waited.

“A licensee capped at five cannot be examined past five.”

Nothing in the room changed.

“That is all of it,” said the man. “I have read that schedule and I have read the licence behind it, and the licence caps her, and the cap is in her own hand and is a proper thing and is not a way of hiding anything. And a cap that is in her own hand stops an examination exactly as well as it stops a body.”

“If an office wants to look at that woman and she is at six, this county cannot give them six, because this county has licensed her to five and cannot examine past her own ceiling. I am telling the room because I would like the room to have heard it before I say it to whoever has to act on it.”

He picked up the receipt.

“That is all,” he said. “Thank you.”

## The Machinery

He did not look at her on the way out and he did not speak to her and he did not buy a second thing and he did not give his name to the young clerk or to anybody else in that office, and he went out into the wet and turned and went up the lane toward the square and the three desks.

That was the whole of what he did in six weeks.

And it did not need anything else, because he had said it to the room.

A county office writes down everything said in it. That is not a regulation, it is a habit of the trade, and it is the only reason a county can be trusted at all, and what a man says at a public counter goes into the counter clerk's mouth and then into the room, and then it goes into the room's memory, and the room belongs to the registrar.

And the registrar does not have to be told anything. He is the man the room reports to. He has nineteen years in that book and he does not hurry, and it is not his job to ask anybody what they intend, and it is not his job to stop two sentences being said out loud in his office at the third hour on the twenty-third day of the first week of summer.

He heard both of them.

She had been eight feet away and she had watched the back of his head while he heard both of them, and then he came to her, because she was fourth in the queue and it was the third hour and there was a sheet in front of him with an hour on it.

She signed her own hours in her left hand and the clerk turned the sheet so the queue could see that it was signed, and Sun He had the county book open in front of him with the brush over the column, in the ordinary hand of a man doing his duty.

And as he wrote it, without stopping, without looking up, in the voice of a man writing a number in a column:

“He is right about the stage column.”

Then he wrote the hour, and read it back, and said the next one.

## The Dread of Being Right

She went up the tower stair at the ninth hour with the filing copy in the lining and the county hour behind her and her right hand doing the thing it does about one time in three.

She lay on the loft mat with the coat for a blanket and said it out loud into the room, because she said things out loud into rooms.

“He is right.”

“Yes,” said Yuan.

“He is right about the stage column and he is right about the cap and he said both of them to a room with a registrar in it and he said them in the voice of a man making an observation.”

“He is right about both of them.”

“He is not a step ahead of me,” said Ning Yan. “He has not been a step ahead of me at any point in six weeks. He has been sitting at a desk twice a week buying nothing, waiting for a piece of paper to come down a road, and the piece of paper came, and he read it slower than I would have read it and he stayed longer over it than I would have stayed over it.”

“He is not cleverer than you.”

“He is more patient with paper than I am,” said Ning Yan, “and that is the whole of him and it took him six weeks and one coin to establish it.”

She lay still for a while.

“I want to be clear about what this is,” she said, “because I have been afraid of this since the autumn and I have been afraid of the wrong thing.”

“I have been afraid of a man who comes. Of a man who is paid by somebody with a counting room and puts money on a table and buys a body and a place and a name, because that man has a deadline and a deadline can be outrun.”

“This is a man who reads. And the reason he is worse is that he does not have a deadline. The paper moves at the quarter. The announcements move at the body's pace and the body's pace is a season, and both of those things move toward him, and neither of them is going anywhere else.”

“You have four weeks of road before the pass shuts.”

“And about three weeks of him after that before the season turns, and after the season turns the market opens on a cold day and he will be at the same desk,” said Ning Yan. “He has all of it. I have a county hour and a load a day and an arm.”

She put her arm out to the side.

“He has got a copy of my schedule,” she said. “Certified. In the county's own hand. He has got the hours and the Hall's mark and the nine households and the stage column with a space in it where the fourth should be, and he paid for it out of his own pocket in daylight in front of a queue and he took a receipt and folded it and put it away.”

“You saw him fold it.”

“I saw him fold it and square the edge against the counter first,” said Ning Yan, “which is what a man does to a document he is going to need in a month.”

## What the Room Does

Below her, the copying floor was lit, and Feng was not at the bench, and the four county copies were walled behind the fifth item where they had been since the winter, and the volumes on the high shelf were not copies and had not been struck.

Six weeks ago a sheet had come up that stair on ordinary county stationery and had asked for a word to be taken off every copy inside the bound, and it had named an office that Yuan could not read, and Feng had said he did not know which kind of man he was.

Now there was a man in Lowwater who had bought a certified copy of the schedule that the four copies were copies of, and he had a receipt, and he had said two true things in a county room that a tower keeper's whole trade is meant to be downstream of.

She did not go down and ask Feng a single question about it.

She lay on the mat with her arm out to the side and did the arithmetic she had been avoiding since the seventeenth day, and the arithmetic was this.

The two things he said in that room were going to reach three men four miles up a mountain, because the room belongs to a registrar and a registrar who hears a thing like that writes it up, and a registrar who has a sealed member is required to tell the maintaining members of that member's schedule, and the reason he is required to is that a schedule a member cannot be accounted for in is a schedule that is a liability.

And she had spent nineteen days walking a lane and giving a man in a market one true sentence and telling a girl in a wash house another one, and the man at the counter had walked out of the office with the whole of her in his hand.

None of it was a threat. Not one person in that room had done anything wrong, and that was the part of it that sat under her ribs.

And it would get to them, and there would be nothing in the world she could set down beside it, and she would be standing in a Hall with no roof explaining what she had done with a stranger's questions about her.

She got up and put the lamp out at the ninth hour and lay down on the good side, and it was not the lamp that had kept her up.
