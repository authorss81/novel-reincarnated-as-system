# Another Hand

The tower at Lowwater had four hundred and eleven volumes on eleven shelves and a lamp that had been burning for twenty-two years on the fuel of a district that paid for it in the summer and forgot about it in the winter.

It was burning. She could see it from the third flight of the quarters on a clear night, one light down in the town and four miles off, and in the third week of the month she had stood on the stair and looked at it for longer than she meant to.

She went down in the dark at the fourth hour because there was one thing to do and one person to do it with, and she was at the foot of the tower at about the fifth hour with the cold in her face and three counts in her chest from the descent.

## The Copying Floor

The copying floor was two rooms and a stair and it was warmer than any building she had stood in since the spring.

Feng Lushi was at the bench under the lamp with a sheet weighted at the four corners and a pen, and he had been coughing less since the coal came and he had been writing more, and the pile of sheets at the left of his bench had got taller across the winter.

He was sixty-two. He had been sixty-two for a long time and had got warmer for the first time in twenty-two years, which had been bought with an appointment he had said yes to on the floor in the autumn because a suspension had left him with a tower he could not feed paper through and lungs that could not stand another winter of copying cold.

He was a Listener now, for life, set-aside-able on request from without the bound. Every hour he copied went upward truly and in full, whether or not it was entered, and the county gave him a chair on the reading days and a wage at the quarter and a hundredweight of coal he had told her he would not have managed without and had not said was for the cough.

He did not ask her a question. He had not asked her one since the autumn and he was not going to, and both of them had said so to each other in one breath so that neither of them had to spend a morning on it.

“Fourth hour,” he said. “You are down early.”

“I have to be back for the ninth.”

“Then you will sit for an hour and not talk,” said Feng, “and I will put the lamp where you can see it.”

## The Bundle

At about the seventh hour a clerk came up the stair.

He was a young man from the district with a bundle under his arm and a strap over his shoulder, and he went up the four flights of the tower with it, and he put it on the third landing in a wire basket, and he came back down.

Feng did not go up.

“You did not ask,” said Ning Yan.

“No.”

“You are not carrying it.”

“I am not carrying it this quarter,” said Feng. “The county took the stair off me at the middle of the autumn and gave it to a clerk, and everything I copy goes up the bundle and the bundle goes up the basket and a man from the district takes it to the office above the county, and he comes down empty, and that is the arrangement and it is not a punishment and it is not temporary in the way a punishment is temporary.”

He turned the sheet he was on and went on writing.

“It is permanent,” he said, “because I asked for it to be written as permanent.”

## What He Gave Her

He gave it to her without being asked and without a preamble, the way a keeper gives a thing he has had time to think about, and he put it on the bench with the weights on it and turned it round so that it faced her.

It was one sheet. It was in fair hand — his fair hand, the hand that had written one line at the back of a fair volume in the spring and had it go up the stair at the quarter.

It was her stage column.

All four of the county's copies of it, one under the other, copied out in full, exactly as they stood, with the gaps in them the way the gaps stood. One, two, three. Then two ruled lines. Then five, six, seven. Then two ruled lines.

Nine lines of writing and two holes in it.

“I copied these in the last three weeks,” said Feng. “Not for the county. For the building. Because my stair went and everything I make now goes up in a bundle that I do not carry, and a tower is a thing that is kept, and there is nobody in this building any more whose keeping it means anything except the volumes and me.”

She put her hand flat on the bench and looked at it for a long time.

Two holes. Nine lines. In a fair hand, by a man of sixty-two who was warm.

“There is a fourth coming,” said Ning Yan.

“I know,” said Feng.

“You do not know that.”

“I have been copying this column since the spring and I know what a hole of that width is,” he said. “I am sixty-two and I am not stupid and I do not need to be told what a woman does on a cold end for five weeks.”

## What She Came To Ask

She had come down four miles in the dark to ask one thing and she asked it.

“Enter it,” she said. “Not as a request. Enter that a member of the Qinghe furnace crew told this tower this morning that she would make an announcement at the ninth hour tomorrow at the cold end of the Qinghe floor, and that you are to copy it into the county’s volumes in fair hand and send it up in the quarter’s bundle with everything else in it.”

“And enter it as a tower change, which is a thing that happened in the building and not a service rendered to a member.”

Feng wrote it down.

He wrote it in fair hand on the day's sheet, with the hour and the date and both the asking and the doing in it, and then he read it back to her in the ordinary voice of a man reading a sheet he has just finished, and it was exactly what she had asked for.

“Why a tower change,” he said.

“Because it is one,” said Ning Yan. “Because a request is a thing a member asks an office for, and I am not asking this office for anything. It is a thing that is going to happen in a building four miles up that hill, and you are the only person in this county who copies what happens in buildings.”

“All right.”

“And one more thing, and it is the same thing.” She put her hand on the sheet with the four copies of her column on it. “When you copy the nine into the volumes tomorrow, copy it as an announcement the member made. Not as one the tower heard. The tower did not hear it. A woman stood at a mark and said a word and nine counties had it, and the tower was not in it.”

“That is how it was written in the spring.”

“That is how it was written in the spring,” said Ning Yan, “and in the summer, and in the autumn, and I want it written that way tomorrow as well, because in a year somebody is going to read four volumes and find out where it came from, and I am not going to have it be a building.”

## The Second Thing He Said

“Two more things and then I will let you walk,” said Feng, “and I am saying both of them now so that you do not have to stand here deciding whether to ask.”

She waited.

“The first is the volumes. There are four hundred and eleven and I copy into all of them that a tower keeps, which is every volume and not one in nine. Tomorrow’s will be in ninety of them by the afternoon and in the rest before the spring. In two years a man in some county will open one at random and find your nine in it, and he will not know which of two women in this bound it belongs to, and he will be right not to know.”

“And the second.”

“The second is that you should be at the mark before the ninth hour and not at it at the ninth hour,” said Feng. “The setting comes out of the mark and the whole crew is at the near end for about eleven minutes. If you do it in those eleven minutes you will be doing it in front of nine men who are all looking at you and none of them working, which is a thing you would have to think about afterwards.”

“If you do it at the mark in the working hour you will be doing it in front of nine men who are working, and that is the same nine men and it costs you nothing.”

“You have thought about it.”

“I have been a keeper for twenty-two years,” said Feng, “and I have watched two people in this building do this and I know which one of them was still standing at the end of it.”

## What She Said When She Saw The Sheet

She had been holding her hand on a bench for about a minute with two holes in a fair hand under it, and she had not said anything, and Feng had not asked her to.

The fourth stage of her life was in the first gap. She had held that one down alone on the flats in the summer with a stone in her hand and no office in nine counties had ever heard of it, and a registrar had ruled the empty column an absence, and that ruling was still standing in this county's book.

The eighth was in the second one, and she had put that down at a stove on the thirty-third day of the quarry, and no office in nine counties had heard of that either.

Neither of those two things was in the four hundred and eleven volumes in any hand, and there was a line under both of them in a sheet in front of her written by a man who could not read what the gaps were.

“You cannot read those,” she said.

“I cannot read those,” said Feng.

“You have copied them.”

“I have copied a column,” said Feng. “A column is nine lines and two gaps and I can see that a man has left room. I cannot see what is in the room and I have never once been able to and I stopped trying about nine years ago.”

He put the charcoal away.

“That is the part that belongs to you,” he said, “and it is not in the building, and there is nobody in this tower who can look at it with you. I have thought about that for four weeks and I have come to the end of what I can do about it and so I have done nothing, which is the correct answer and is not much of one.”

That was the warm room, and it was the last warm room in the year.

## Two Miles of Hill

She left at about the eighth hour with the lamp behind her and four miles of mountain in front of her and the ninth hour two hours off.

She had never once in her life walked up four miles in ninety minutes and she did it, and the third flight cost four counts instead of three, and she stood at the top of it in the dark for about half a minute with her hand on the rail and her right arm open and the whole of the ninth standing up in her like a stone in a boot.

Below her and down the hill there was one light in the town, burning on the fuel of a district, carried up four flights of a tower stair by a clerk who came down empty.

Nine men were asleep in a room that held twelve bunks and slept ten.

In about three hours she was going to stand at a mark at the far end of a furnace floor and say a word out loud in the ordinary voice in front of all of them, and there was no door in the building to shut.
