# The Second Application

She went to the county office on the square at the ninth hour of the seventeenth day with nothing in her hand, which she had not done in eleven months, and asked a question at the side counter that was not about her filing.

She had worked it out on the loft mat and it had not fitted, and there was one place in Lowwater where the answer to a thing like that was kept in a drawer and would be read out to her if she asked it in the right shape.

“The second condition,” said Ning Yan. “Hours on the Qinghe furnace schedule, certified by the Hall's clerk, returned to this office at the quarter. No other schedule counts.”

The clerk at the side of the office had a brass rule laid along the edge of the table. He was the same one who had drawn a line through a cell in the second position of a county sheet at the second hour of an afternoon in the spring and had asked her if she wanted to see the original.

“I have it in my hand,” he said. “It is the condition. Do you want to know what counts as that schedule.”

“I want to know what does not.”

“Everything that is not that schedule,” said the clerk. “There is a list and the list is short and you are standing in it.”

## The List

“The county does not keep furnace schedules,” he said. “It never has. It reads them when they are certified and when they are returned and it has no other copy of them and no power to ask for one. There is no column in this office for an hour that a person worked somewhere else, and there has never been one, and a registrar who made one would be a registrar with a new form.”

“And nobody outside this office keeps one either,” said Ning Yan, “or a woman with a licence would have been put on a sheet in the spring and there would not be a second condition on her licence.”

“Nobody outside this office keeps one,” said the clerk. “You are correct and it is not a thing I am going to be impressed by. It is a thing nobody has ever had to write down.”

He squared the rule against the edge of the table.

“You are on the road behind the well every day of the week and you have been for thirteen days. You are paid by the load. The loads are on a board with chalk on it and the chalk goes off in the rain. There are eleven people in that lane and none of them is a clerk.”

“There are.”

“None of them that the county recognises,” said the clerk. “That is not the same sentence and it is the one that matters and it is the reason the second condition is on your licence at all.”

## The Annex

“There is a way round it,” she said.

“There is a way round almost everything and about four ways round anything,” said the clerk, “and this one is called an annex and it is on a form, and I am not going to tell you the form's name because you will say it wrongly and then you will have said it wrongly in four buildings.”

“Tell me the shape.”

“An annex is a sheet of additional hours that is added to a schedule that already exists, and it is put on that schedule by the clerk who keeps that schedule, and it is certified by whoever certifies that schedule, and after that it is on the schedule.”

“It is certified.”

“It is certified.”

“Not the place it was worked in.”

“Not the place it was worked in,” said the clerk. “This office does not keep a map of where a body was standing. It keeps a sheet with hours on it and a hand at the bottom of it. If a clerk who keeps a schedule writes an hour onto that schedule, that hour is on that schedule, and where the woman stood while she did it is not a question this office has ever thought to ask.”

He put the rule down.

“Your condition says the hours are those of the furnace schedule of Qinghe, certified by the Hall's clerk. Your hire hours are not on that schedule and no amendment to your licence can put them there, because you cannot amend a county sheet and a Hall schedule from inside a lane.”

“No.”

“What you can do is get them annexed,” said the clerk. “Taken to the Hall, written onto the furnace schedule by the Hall's own copyist, in a column of its own, certified by the Hall's clerk and stamped by a maintaining member of that Hall, and returned with the quarter like everything else on that sheet. After that they are furnace hours. Not by place. By certification.”

He looked at her for a moment over the brass rule.

“That is the whole trick and it is not a trick, and it is legal, and it has been legal since the clause was written, and the reason it is not in every licence in this bound is that no servant in nine counties has been in a position to pay for it.”

## The Price of the Annex

“How much of my own labour is in an annex.”

“All of it,” said the clerk. “It is your hours. They are counted against your household in the quota book and they are counted against the asked figure at the close of the season, and the quota clerk who keeps that book is not the clerk who keeps the Hall's sheet and does not have to agree with him about anything.”

“He does not have to agree about the asked figure.”

“He will not weight it for one arm,” said the clerk. “I have never met the man and I will tell you what his office does, because I am a clerk and this is what clerks are for: he will take the hours that are on the sheet and he will take them at what they are worth, and a woman who is paid by the load is a woman whose hours are a woman whose hours.”

She stood at the side counter with the brass rule two inches from her hand and worked out four months of her life in about nine seconds and had done it the night before and had not let herself do it in daylight until now.

Her furnace line was complete. Nine hundred and forty asked, nine hundred and forty worked, the season closed, the certificate signed in the middle of the spring behind the thaw.

A hire is not a household's labour. It is a woman with a mat on her shoulder putting a stone down in a channel, and it is not asked for by anybody, and it has no asking figure against it anywhere in nine counties.

Which means that if she annexes it, she annexes it into a book that closes square, and the moment it is in that book the number that has been level since the middle of the spring stops being level.

“That is the whole of what the annex costs.”

“That is the whole of what the annex costs,” said the clerk, “and you have got it in about the time it takes me to say it, and I would have told you the same way in the middle of the winter if you had come in the middle of the winter, and you did not come in the middle of the winter, and I have no idea why and it is not a question I am going to ask.”

## The Second Application

“And when I have annexed it,” said Ning Yan, “what do I do with it.”

“You take it to the Hall and you get it sealed by the maintaining members, and you take the clearance out of the quota office, and you bring it here and you ask for a relicensing.”

“Which is what I did in the spring.”

“Which is what you did in the winter and was refused twice and granted once, and this is exactly the same three legs in exactly the same order, and there is no second kind of this and no shorter road and no office in nine counties where a ceiling can be lifted by anybody except three men and a registrar.”

She looked at him.

“It is the same three legs,” said Ning Yan. “It is not the same order. In the spring the seal came first.”

“It comes in whatever order it comes in and all three have to be there by the time it is on my table.” He turned the rule over and set it down square with the edge. “And I am going to tell you the two things that are going to happen to you when you do it, because nobody has ever asked me and I would like one person to have been told.”

“One. You will want a new ceiling entered in your own hand. That is not this office's rule and it is in your filing from the first time and it does not change. You write the number yourself. Whoever you name writes their own name under it and takes the whole of what you have written.”

“And the second.”

“The second is the claim.”

She had known about the claim. She had written it in the log in the spring in plain text and had not looked at it since because there had been no reason to look at it.

“It is the sixth condition.”

“It is the sixth condition and it is in this office's book, and I stamped it there in the spring because I am required to stamp it there,” said the clerk. “A new document out of this office is a new stamp.”

“And a claim that is in this office's book cannot be taken off a document by a man stamping a different one. I will have to enter it again. It will be the last thing on the paper again, and it will travel with it again, and you will get exactly one more copy of the same problem in a county hand.”

“I know,” said Ning Yan.

“You know it now,” said the clerk. “In the spring you found it out at a counter with a stamp in my hand and you had four months of it in front of you. You are finding it out at a counter with no stamp in my hand and you have seventeen days, which I imagine is not as good.”

## Nothing Is Restored

She went out of the county office with nothing in her hands, which was how she had gone in, and stood on the step in the sun and made herself finish the sum all the way to the end so that she would not have to come back and do it again in the dark.

A relicensing buys a ceiling.

It does not put the fourth stage back. It does not mend a crack. It does not remove a defect line that is in her own hand on two county documents. It does not put a man in a room behind her so that she can be ill for a morning. It does not shorten four miles of road, it does not revive a writ that died on a sheet she signed herself, and it does not take eight hundred jin off anything at all.

It buys a number she may write in her own hand, above the one she wrote in her own hand in the spring, and every hour between the two numbers is an hour she has to work and a stage she has to be in front of nine counties to reach.

And the first leg is three men she has no claim on, who sealed the first application and let her sign the ceiling that killed them, and who have said nothing whatever to her since the sixth day of the winter.

## The Log

The copying floor inside Feng Lushi's tower was three flights up and it was a different room in a different building in a different county from the office she had just left, and she had made sure of that on the stair, because the two of them get confused in her head and a county office does not send copies upward and a tower does not hold the record.

Feng was at the far bench with his back to the stair and a cough that had got a little worse every week since the water went out of the air.

“I am not asking you for anything,” she said.

“You have never asked me for anything,” said Feng, “and you have taken four things off this floor that I put in front of you, and one of them was a fair line at the back of a fair volume, and I am going to be annoyed about that for the rest of the year.”

“I am not asking you for anything,” said Ning Yan, “because the next four weeks are mine and they cost me and I am not going to put any of it on this floor.”

She took the docket out on the bench at the bottom of the stair with her arm out to the side and wrote it in the wash code with her left hand.

*Day 17. Asked at the county. It is a second application down the same three legs and there is no other kind. Clearing first or sealing first does not matter to the county and matters enormously to the three men, and I know which way round it has to go and I am not going to write down why in this docket.*

*Hire hours are not furnace hours. Annex is the way round: Hall copyist writes them onto the furnace schedule, Hall clerk certifies, maintaining member stamps, goes up the quarter. Legal since the clause was written. Nobody has ever paid for it.*

*The annex puts my hire hours into the quota book and the book closes square, and the number that has been level since the spring stops being level the day I do it. That is the whole of the price and I have known it for four days and I have not written it down until now.*

*New licence. New stamp. The claim gets re-entered last, in his hand, because he is required to.*

Then she put the docket away and looked at the four county copies walled behind the fifth item where they had been since the market day in the winter, and at the volumes on the high shelf that were not copies and had not been struck, and at a man who was coughing in a room that had been quietly carrying her hours up a stair for eleven months for nothing.

“Feng.”

“Lamp is lit,” said Feng.

He did not turn round and he did not ask, and the lamp was lit.

She went down three flights and out into the lane and stood at the foot of the tower in the sun with a docket in her coat that said a thing she had not been able to write down at the fortieth day, which was that the road forward and the road behind her had turned out to be the same road, and it ran uphill for four miles through three men who had every reason to let it close.

She did not tell Yuan what the next four weeks would cost.

It was the first time since the winter that she had worked out something and kept it, and she was aware, standing in the lane at the foot of a tower with her arm out to the side, that keeping it was a new habit and that he had not asked her for it.
