# The Quiet Book

The rain had stopped and the Hall had not got a roof out of it, and there were four pools on the floor and one body of water in the low corner, and the light in the place at the eighth hour of a morning in the first weeks of summer came in sideways off the Nine Steps and lay in a long strip across the wet stone.

The three of them were under the awning at the west end.

She had come up on a cart day and got here at the second hour and had stood at the rail for eleven minutes before any of them spoke, which she had learned to do with these three somewhere around the sixth week of the winter, because a man who has been waiting an hour to speak will speak first and a man who has been waiting eleven minutes has read the whole of a room.

“Sun He has written up about me,” said Ning Yan.

“We have read it,” said Lu Kang. “It came up in a bundle on the day the crate went and Deng Chao read it out and then we all three sat here and did not say anything for an hour, and you have come up the road to find out what we decided.”

“What did you decide.”

“We have decided that we will seal it.”

She had known that was coming. She had walked four miles up a mountain in the dark getting ready to be refused for the second time in eleven weeks and she had it ready, and she felt the whole shape of the next four weeks go out from under her feet.

“Condensing nine,” said Lu Kang. “Not Foundation.”

“Condensing nine.”

“Not Foundation,” said Lu Kang, “and we would like to hear you say it in that order once more because the two of them are in one line on a piece of paper and a clerk will read both.”

“Not Foundation. Condensing nine,” said Ning Yan.

“Very well.”

## The Condition

Deng Chao put a cup down on the board.

“You have not asked what it costs,” he said.

“You told me on the twentieth day that you would not put a mark on a sheet that says nine while my hours are in two places at once, and I have been annexing them ever since, and I am going to have the clearance out of the quota office inside ten days.”

“That is not what it costs,” said Deng Chao.

“It costs a sheet with three marks on it and a clearance and a registrar's stamp, and I have been told what every one of those costs and I have paid all three, and there is nothing else in it.”

“There is one more thing,” said Deng Chao, “and we have been sitting here for an hour deciding whether to say it to you, and Pei An is going to lose his nerve in about a minute, so I am going to say it.”

“We will seal it. And the seal is for a ceiling at nine inside our own schedule, and a member inside our own schedule is a member we are answerable for, and there is going to be an office on the other side of a bundle somewhere that has a question about her and the three of us are going to have to answer it.”

“We have decided that we can live with that. We decided it about nine seconds after we read Sun He's line and we have not eaten since.”

He put both hands flat on the board.

“And what we have decided is that this seal is bought. Not given. Bought, at a price, by a woman who came up a mountain with a piece of paper, and the price is the only thing in this arrangement any of the three of us has any control over.”

“All right,” said Ning Yan.

“You do not know what you are agreeing to.”

“Yes I do.”

“No, you do not, and I want to be extremely clear about this because you are a good deal better at paper than the three of us and you will have worked it out in about as long as it takes me to say this, and you will think you have known it since the sixth day of the winter.” Deng Chao did not move. “There is a second book.”

“You have known about it since the winter and you have never once opened your mouth about it in this building, and you were not going to, and you came up here today and you are going to go down again with three marks on a sheet, and we are all three going to understand what that means and none of us is ever going to say it out loud again.”

The Hall had no roof. The light lay in its strip on the wet stone and the water in the low corner was doing what water does when nothing is happening.

“Name the price,” said Ning Yan.

## The Weapon

She had it ready. She had had it ready since the seventeenth day, written out on a docket in a coat lining in a code no clerk in nine counties can read quickly, and she had carried it four miles up a mountain and four miles down twice and had not taken it out once.

*Nine households struck in one stroke. Eleven years. Fourth line: the ledger of the maintaining members of Qinghe, per their schedule of the eleven years. Read upside down over a clerk's tray in a county office in the winter, over a debt paper that named Hou Sanliang of Lowwater as creditor on three of the nine, with striking dates eleven months and eleven days apart and the same spacing to the day.*

That was it. That was the whole of it. One paragraph, four lines of wash code, the most expensive thing she had ever read.

She looked at the three of them under the awning.

Deng Chao had his hands flat on the board and had said the word *book* in a corridor with a clerk thirty feet away at a copy bench with his back turned, and he had said it on purpose, and she understood that he had said it on purpose because it was the only way to make her take it out.

“If I use it,” said Ning Yan, “the seal is bought. And a bought signature is a purchase and not a sponsorship, and I am going to have it both ways or I am going to have neither, and I would rather have neither.”

“Go on.”

“I am going to lodge it,” said Ning Yan. “Not this week. Not for a seal. In a county office at Lowwater, on the ordinary form any applicant can fill in, and I will not put my name on it and I will not need to, because a form does not want my name, it wants a schedule and a ledger and eleven years of a column, and any one of the three of you is in all three of those.”

She stopped.

“There is a man in Lowwater holding paper on three of the nine households with striking dates that match yours to the day, and if the county ever reads his paper it will arrive at your ledger, and the county does not have to be told. It only has to read. And when it does that, the sponsorship that sealed me at the fifth stage is dead in a second office, and this awning is not the end of it.”

She laid it out flat.

“I have been carrying that since the winter. I have not used it. I am not going to use it.”

## Then Don't

“Then don’t,” said Lu Kang.

The three of them did not move.

“There,” said Lu Kang. “That is the arrangement, and I am going to say the whole of it out loud so that it is in a corridor and not in three heads.”

“We seal your nine. In exchange, nothing about the second book is lodged, copied, spoken or written, anywhere, by any of us, ever. Not at the Hall, not at the quota office, not in a county, not in a bundle going up, not in an answer to a registrar, not in a room a stranger is standing in. There is no version of the rest of this in which that comes out.”

“And there is no version of the rest of this in which you put it out, and I want to say that part as well, because you have just given it to me as a weapon and I am telling you what it is going to be used for. It is not going to be used against you.”

“It is going to be used by all three of us, every day, for the rest of this, against anybody who comes with a question. If a stranger asks me whether Qinghe keeps a second book I will say no, and I will say it in a form that can be read back and I will be lying, and you will have made me that.”

“I know,” said Ning Yan.

“Deng Chao.”

“I know,” said Deng Chao.

“Pei An.”

“I know,” said Pei An.

She stood under the awning with the rain coming through the fourth place and going round the table and she had said the whole of what she had and it had gone into a corridor and come back as *then don't*, and she was going to have to go on carrying a paragraph of wash code in a coat lining for the rest of the season, and nobody was ever going to read it.

She had never in her life held a thing that was worth more and used less.

## What It Cost

“Do you understand what you have just given away,” said Ning Yan.

“You have told us what it is and you have told us not to use it and we are going to hold you to both,” said Lu Kang, “and I am going to tell you something about the three of us that you should have worked out four weeks ago and have not, because you have been thinking of us as three men who sat in a corridor and said nothing.”

“We said nothing because we had nothing to say. We have had a book for eleven years and not one of us has ever been asked about it by anybody with a stamp, because nobody has ever been able to read it and nobody has ever wanted to badly enough. And you have been able to read it since the winter. That is the whole of our position and it has been the whole of our position for eleven years.”

He took his hands from behind his back.

“You are the first person in eleven years who can use it, and you have just told us you will not, and I am going to spend the rest of the day being sick with relief about it, and I am going to be careful for the rest of my life, because a person who has that and does not use it once is not a person I know what to do with.”

The three of them trusted her a little more.

That was the part that frightened her.

She had walked four miles up a mountain holding a paragraph that would have opened a book in four counties and closed a sponsorship in five, and she had put it on the table under a dry awning in an open building, and three men who had said nothing to her for eleven weeks had said the thing they had actually been saying all along, which was that they were afraid.

They were afraid of a paper she had promised not to write.

And in about four years, or nine, or eleven, one of the three of them would be dead, and the other two would still be here, and the arrangement she had made in a corridor would still be standing, and it would be standing between three families and one county and no office at all.

“Sign it the day after the crate goes,” said Lu Kang. “Bring the sheet with the clearance on it and the annex certified and your hand on the ceiling, and we will put three marks on it in this corridor at the west table, and Deng Chao will read it out because he reads everything out, and then you take it down four miles to a man with a stamp.”

“The day after the crate goes.”

“We cannot seal a sheet on a day a bundle is going up the same stair,” said Lu Kang. “There will be two of us on this floor and a bundle.”

## What She Took Down

She went out into the light off the steps and down through the Hall, and the water in the low corner had a skin on it where the wind had got at it, and the clerk at the copy bench was writing and did not turn round and had not turned round all morning.

At the foot of the steps she stopped, because it was the only place in that building with a rail on one side and nothing at all on the other, and she stood at it and got the docket out of the lining and read the paragraph one more time with her left hand.

Then she took her hand off it and put the docket away.

The three of them had said *then don't* and she had not, and it was done, and there is no office in nine counties where a thing that has not been written down can be un-done or read back, and she had given up the only weapon of her life in a corridor with a rail down the side of it in exchange for three marks on a sheet and a roof over a boy.

And the thing she could never get back was not the weapon. It was the not-knowing. She could never again stand in a room with those three men and not know that they knew that she knew, and neither could they, and for the rest of a very long time there would be no arrangement anywhere in this bound in which any of them could put anything down.

She went down four miles of road in the sun with a clearance to get and a crate to get into and about four weeks of a season in front of her, and she did not tell Yuan what the silence had cost because he had not asked and because she had not decided to tell him, and she noticed herself noticing the difference.
