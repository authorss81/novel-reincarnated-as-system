# Carried Down the Stair

The paper had been on the mat for four days and she had not opened it, and the reason was not caution and was not fear and was a thing she would have been ashamed to say out loud in a room with a lamp in it.

She could read. She had been reading paper since the spring and she could read a licence and a schedule and a bundle and a county's own hand at four words to the second, and she could read the copyist's wrong order without it meaning anything, because it had been in that order for eleven years and would be there after she was.

What she could not do was read a man.

That was the whole of it. A sheet can be checked by a person with two eyes and an hour. A man cannot, and there was exactly one instrument in nine counties that could take a man apart in front of her and show her the order of the pieces, and she had written four words about it in the spring on the back of a licence she had not yet nailed up, and the four words were a refusal.

## The Mat

It was ordinary paper. County-good, from the look of it, the same stock as the office stationery that had come off a copying floor at the second hour, folded twice, not sealed, not addressed, and with nothing on the outside of it that named a person of any kind.

The man who had brought it had knocked on the frame of the loft door on the morning of the hundred and twenty-ninth day and had not come in when she called out, and she had been in the bed with a fever on and had got up and opened the door about nine minutes later and the mat was empty.

So the paper had been lying on the mat for four days in a room above a copying room, and she had gone down the stair and up the stair eleven times in those four days without picking it up, and Feng Lushi had not mentioned it, because he had not seen it, because it had been put down inside a room he did not come into.

“Open it,” said Yuan.

“No.”

“You have read it four times from four feet away in this cold and you cannot tell one word of it.”

“I cannot tell one word of the man who left it,” said Ning Yan. “That is a different sentence and you know it is, and I am not going to sit here and be talked into opening a piece of paper by a thing that is pleased to be asked.”

He did not argue with it. That was the thing she could never get past with him. He had said yes he was right in about half a count, and then he had stopped, and she had been left holding a decision she had made herself on a mat in a cold room.

## The Copying Floor

She went down four flights at the ninth hour and the short breath came at the third landing and she stood there about three counts and then went on, because going down is worse than going up and she had had four days to find out.

Feng was on the bench with a lamp and about ninety pages of a district list in front of him, and he had a coat on that she had not seen him wear inside, and he did not look up for about four seconds and then he did and put the brush down square with the edge of the board, which is what he does.

“I am going to have him read a man through my eyes,” said Ning Yan.

He did not move.

“I wrote a sentence about it in the spring,” she said, “and I am breaking it, and I wanted you to hear me say so before I do it instead of after, which is the only reason I came down here.”

“All right,” said Feng Lushi.

“I want you to enter it.”

“I am going to enter it,” said Feng Lushi. “That is not a favour, that is the arrangement you have had since the day you asked me for the fair line, and you know what it is, so do not stand in my tower and make it sound like one.”

“I am not making it sound like anything. I am telling you first because you are going to find out.”

“I would have found out at the quarter,” he said, “and I would have found out later, and I would have had to decide what to write on a sheet I had already written.” He turned the brush over. “Write it up.”

## A Tower Change

It is a small thing and it took about ninety seconds, and the reason it was a tower change and not a request was that a tower change is a thing that happened in the building and a request is a thing somebody came and asked for, and she had not asked for anything and what she was going to do was not a service the tower rendered to her.

He wrote it on the day's sheet in fair hand at the top of the column where the day's work went, and the building does not stop when he writes.

*Member resident in the loft. Fourth hour. Member has directed that the thing resident with her be set to a reading of a man through her, and the setting was at the member's own word and was named by the member before it was set.*

“You have named it,” she said. “Most people would have left that off.”

“I have named it,” said Feng Lushi, “because the form has a place for what was asked for and a place for what was done, and I wrote in both, and if I wrote in one and not the other then in four months there would be a copy of this going up a stair with half of it on it and somebody would build a thing out of the half.”

He read it back to himself and then he did something she had not seen him do in fourteen months. He turned the sheet round and put it where she could read it, which is not necessary and is not allowed and he did it anyway.

“Then it goes up at the quarter,” he said, “in the order the sheet is in, and I will carry it down myself when the quarter comes, and you do not need to have anything to do with it.”

“You are off the stair for a quarter.”

“I am off the stair for a quarter,” said Feng Lushi, “which means the bundle goes down on a clerk's arm and comes back up on a clerk's arm, and it means the bundle does not go out of this building in my hand, and it means it goes out of this building.”

## The Price

She went back up four flights and sat down on the mat in a cold room with a sheet of paper she had not opened.

“Say what it costs.”

“It costs four and a half weeks,” said Yuan. “Out of about ten. It has never been cheaper and it will not be cheaper while you are alive.”

“Say it slower.”

“It costs four and a half weeks out of about ten weeks of what I have,” said Yuan. “That is the whole price and it is not partly paid and it does not come back out. When it is done there will be about five and a half weeks left.”

“That is not what I asked.”

“You have not asked yet,” said Yuan. “You asked what it costs and I have told you, and you have been sitting there for about four counts deciding whether to ask me the other thing, which is when I tell you what I will have been wrong by, and I tell you the day it is gone, because that is one of the things you wrote down in the spring and I have not broken it and am not going to.”

She sat with it.

It had been a sentence about the winter, written in her own hand on the back of a licence that was in oilcloth against her ribs, and it had said there was no version of the rest of the winter where there was that instrument, and she had not written it to be obeyed by anybody else.

“Do not ask me whether I have thought about it for four days,” she said.

“You have thought about it for four days and you have not opened the paper,” said Yuan. “I have watched you not open it.”

“You are not going to say anything about the four days.”

“I am going to say one thing about the four days and then I am not going to say anything about them again,” he said. “You kept it folded for four days. Not because you were frightened of what was in it. Because you did not want to be a woman who read it first.”

“Yes,” said Ning Yan.

“All right,” said Yuan. “Say the word.”

## The Word

She said it into a cold room above a copying room at Lowwater on the one hundred and thirty-third day, out loud, in the ordinary voice, to nobody, because there was a keeper twenty feet below her writing her down.

“Read him.”

## What It Bought

It was less frightening than she had expected and about four times as fast.

He did not go into the man. She had braced for that. She thought it would be like being turned over and read like a schedule, and instead what came was **the shape of a man's work as it lay on a shelf in a room over a tanner's**, in order, the way a column is read.

A room. A strip of sacking over the door that was not sacking. A desk with a lamp on it that was the same lamp every night.

A board on the wall with the dates on it in his own hand, and the dates going out to the edges of the paper, and every one of them a thing that had happened to somebody else and been written down at the hour it happened.

Eight entries in a column headed with a mark she could not read, and the eight were the eight remaining proofs, in order, and they were not guesses and they were not inventions, and she did not know what they were and he could not tell her in any way that would survive being written down.

A return, already sent upward, with her hours in it, and her annex, and the irregular stamp, and the stage column going down the side of it one, two, three, five, six, seven.

And the paper.

He read the paper through her eyes the way a man reads a document he intends to act on, from the top, at speed, and he went down it line by line, and every line was true.

Every line.

She sat on a mat in a cold room with a folded sheet she had not opened and listened to a man in her head read the terms of a mercy somebody was offering her, and not one word of it was wrong.

## What It Did Not Buy

“What office is he?” she said.

“I do not know.”

“Read it again.”

“I cannot read it again,” said Yuan. “It was never in the room. It is in a hand on a table on a floor above this county and I cannot get past a skin and you have not said the word a second time.”

“The eleven.”

“No.”

“The tax.”

“No,” said Yuan. “What I have is a man with a calendar and a good hand and a room he took under a clerk's misspelling, and I cannot tell you one thing about how any of it was made, and if you asked me twice I would tell you the same no.”

## Putting It Down

It ran for most of a day and then it stopped, and the stopping was not a thing he did so much as a thing that was over.

He set it down in the afternoon of the one hundred and thirty-third day, in a cold room, with her sitting on a mat with a piece of ordinary county paper four feet away from her that she still had not opened.

“I am not going to hold it again,” he said.

“You are not going to hold it again this afternoon, or you are not going to hold it again.”

“I am not going to hold it again,” said Yuan. “Not tomorrow and not on the road and not in the cold at the end of the winter, and you can put that in your own hand next to the sentence you broke if you want to write anything down about today, and there is going to be no version of this where you get it back by asking me properly later.”

They sat with that. He had said it before the thawing, once, and had put it down inside a day, and she had believed him then and had spent eleven months treating the arrangement as a piece of luck that had fallen her way.

“You have cost me four and a half weeks,” she said.

“Yes.”

“For a reading.”

“Yes.”

“For a reading of a man who came to me with a piece of paper, which I could have opened and read myself, and which I would have got wrong in a way I could not have found out about.”

“Yes,” said Yuan. “That is what it bought and that is what it cost, and if you had opened it first you would have believed it, and you are not a person who gets to know how you would have gone.”

She did not thank him.

She had it in her mouth and it was the wrong shape — it would have been the same shape as thanking a man who had put his own wages into her hand, and this was not that, and he had not done it for her, and the price had come out of her and not out of him.

“You did not do it for me,” she said.

“No,” said Yuan.

“You did it because I asked.”

“Yes,” said Yuan. “I have wanted to since the spring and you have made me wait for it and I have spent every week of it being correct about that, and neither of those things makes me a good man today. I have a worse one in me and you know his name and I am not going to say it out loud on your own floor.”

She looked at the paper.

It was still folded twice. It had been on a mat in a cold room for four days and it was going to be opened in three days in a room with a table in it, and she knew every term on it, and every term on it was true, and that was the difficulty and it had been the difficulty since before the fever and would be the difficulty on the day she opened it.

“I am not opening it tonight,” said Ning Yan.

“No.”

“I am opening it tomorrow, and I am opening it where other people are.”

“All right,” said Yuan. “Then you will want to have thought about which other people, and that is the last thing I have for you today, and I have already been paid.”
