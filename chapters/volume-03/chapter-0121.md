# The Nineteen Minutes

The county office kept its hour at the third hour of the middle of the day, and the county office was on the market square, and the lane behind the well was nineteen minutes away from the market square in a crowded town at walking pace.

She had known it for eight days. She had walked it twice in daylight to time it and had got nineteen both times and had written it down, and then she had done the only arithmetic that mattered, which was that nineteen is not a number she could recover anywhere in a day.

The man at the fourth table counted loads at the third hour.

That was the whole of the arrangement and there had never been any arrangement. A man with a board counts at the third hour because that is when he counts, and a woman who has to be in a county room at the third hour loses the tail of every day's work by exactly the length of the walk, and there is no version of the world in which a man with a board moves his hour because a woman has a county paper in her coat.

## The Fifth Load

She was on the fifth load at the twenty-first minute before the third hour and she had nineteen minutes of walk in front of her.

The mat went over her right shoulder, because that is the shoulder it goes over, and it is the shoulder that answers about one time in three, and this was one of the three. It was not a decision she made. It was a thing she had found out at the ninth hour and the first night hour for eight months and it did not consult her.

Twenty-six minutes at the water with one arm, and she had done it in twenty-three four days out of five.

She got the mat down at the edge of the lane on the bank side, where it would not go into the water, and she put her left hand flat on the top of the stones and stood there for the length of a count and a half.

Forty people in the lane. The man at the table. The man at the water. The foreman at the oil end who had started saying *the licensed one* on the fourth day and had said it eleven times since.

“It will be here at noon,” she said.

Nobody argued. It is a thing people who work stone understand: a mat set down on a bank at the third hour is a mat that will be picked up at noon, and it is not a thing that requires an answer from anybody.

She walked.

## Nineteen Minutes

The square was busy and she went through the middle of it because the middle was two minutes shorter than the two sides, and by the time she was on the step of the county office she had been walking for twenty-one minutes and the clerk had the attendance open and had eleven people in front of her and was going through them at the rate of about one every two minutes.

She was not late.

She was, however, in a queue, at the third hour, on the eighth day of the first week of summer, with the filing copy out of her coat before she had got to the second person in front of her, which is a thing a person does who has never once had to explain why they are early.

The clerk took it at the table and read the hour and read her name and read the licence number off the first licence against her face, in the ordinary way, out loud, because that is what this counter is.

“Third hour and the twenty-second minute,” said the clerk. “Attended.”

“Attended.”

“You are nineteen minutes past the opening and that is inside it and I am entering the hour and not the minute, because the book has an hour in it and does not have a minute in it, and if you want the minute I will enter the minute in the margin and nobody will ever read the margin.”

“No,” said Ning Yan. “Enter the hour.”

That was the whole of what the county hour was. It had cost her four loads and eight coppers and the walk and the twenty-one minutes, and it consisted of a clerk entering an hour in a book in a book that already had the hour in it.

She signed it in her left hand and the clerk turned the sheet so the queue could see that it was signed, which is not in any regulation and is done anyway, and then he went back to the next one.

## The Arm

“Your arm is going,” said Yuan.

She was in the lane at the second hour with a mat on her left shoulder and forty feet of clear track in front of her and a full channel at the end of it.

“What.”

“Your right arm has been at about half since the water and it is going to be gone inside a minute, and when it goes it goes all at once and it does not come back for the length of a count, and I want you to put the mat down before it does and not after.”

There was nothing wrong with the information. That was the thing she had to work through in the next eleven seconds, standing in a lane with a mat on her shoulder and a full channel at the end of it, and there was not one thing wrong with it.

He had read her at the ninth hour and the first night hour for eight months. He was always able to read her. He could not read anything else, and he could not read her without her standing in a room, and he had spent about four and a half weeks of a channel doing it once and had refused on the same day in a way that has never been unmade.

He was not spending anything. He was not reaching. He was reading a woman standing in a lane with a stone mat on her shoulder, which is what he does when she is near him, and he was telling her.

He was telling her because telling her was not deciding.

“You cannot do that to me often,” said Ning Yan.

“I do it whenever it is true.”

“No,” she said. “I did not ask you to stop. I am telling you what it does to me when you do it, and I am doing it once, and then we will not do it again today.”

“Your arm is going.”

She put the mat down in the lane, in the middle of it, where forty people could walk round it, and it sat there for the length of a count and she stood beside it with her right arm out to the side and her left hand hanging.

Then it came back. It always came back. It came back the way a hand comes back after it has been asleep, slowly, and the first thing she did with it was put her fingers on the mat and find that they were there.

“That is the fourth time this week,” said Ning Yan.

“It is the ninth time this week.”

“I have tested it twice a day since the autumn.”

“You have tested it twice a day,” said Yuan. “I do not sleep.”

She stood in a lane in Lowwater at the second hour of the ninth day of the first week of summer and looked at a mat she had put down in the middle of a working track, and there was nobody within thirty feet who could have told her what had just happened, and there was a man with no hands forty feet inside her head who had watched her put it down before she knew she was going to.

Nine times.

Every day she tested it twice, and on the other times she found out at the water channel, or at the bottom of the tierce, or in the night when it went in the cold, and she had spent eight months building a life out of knowing in advance and he had quietly removed the entire project without asking her and without spending anything.

“Do not tell me the next one,” she said.

“You cannot have that.”

“I know I cannot have it,” said Ning Yan. “That is why I am asking. And you are not going to give it to me, because the third of your three is not to hide anything from me and there is no version of that where you sit on something this sharp for me.”

“No,” said Yuan.

## The Loss

She got the mat to the water at the fifth hour and it took twenty-six minutes and it was the nineteenth day she had been paid and the fourth time in five days that the right hand had gone on a load.

The man at the table counted her two loads and did not look up.

“Two.”

“Two.”

“You were on the fifth.”

“I was on the fifth.”

“You put it down in the middle of the lane.”

“I put it down in the middle of the lane.”

The man at the table wrote one number beside her name and then, after a moment, wrote the second one, and put a small line between them, and did not say anything about it, and wiped the board at the end of the day the way he wiped the board.

Four coppers.

She stood at the end of a water channel in a lane forty paces long and worked out what a day had been worth, and it was six loads and twelve coppers, and four of the six were in a channel at the bottom of the lane on a bank with the stones still in the mat, and she had left them there at the third hour to be in a county room at the third hour.

The county room had contained an hour and a queue and a clerk who did not read margins.

Eight coppers. Half a day. Every day of it, for as long as the season's road is open.

That is what a licence costs a woman who has no one standing behind her, and the county office does not know that it is charging it and neither does the man with the board, and the only thing that has changed since the spring is that the charge now comes out of a number instead of out of a column.

She wrote it in the docket in the wash code at the ninth hour with the lamp turned away.

*County hour, third hour, day 8. Walked it. 19 minutes each way and it is 21 when the square is full. Hours are on the Qinghe schedule only and the county hour is not on any schedule I can see, and no schedule of mine can be made to hold the walk.*

*Four loads gone, eight coppers, and it will be four loads gone every day the road is open and I am not going to ask for the hour to be moved, because the hour is the registrar's table and the table does not move for a woman with a licence.*

*No surety stands for me. If I am ill there is nobody. If the arm goes in a lane at the second hour there is nobody. I go or the hour is a gap, and a gap in the hours breaks a filing, and a broken filing is the whole of the licence gone and the eight hundred jin standing on nothing.*

She stopped the brush there and read it back and did not put the last line in, because the last line was not true yet and she was not going to write a thing in a log that was not true.

## What the Licence Is

She lay on the loft mat with her arm out to the side and the licence squared on the boards where she could see the edge of it in the lamp.

It had survived its own first test.

She said that out loud, into a room, at the ninth hour, in the plain voice of a woman who talks to a man with no hands out loud twice a day and does not stop to do it, and there was nobody in the room and nobody in the building below who could hear it.

“You said it survived.”

“It survived,” said Ning Yan. “I went to the third hour and I was attended and it is entered and there is a line in the book for the eighth day of the first week of summer with my name against it, and nobody has written the word breach anywhere near me in eight days.”

“You left work to do it.”

“I left work to do it,” she said, “and I am going to leave work to do it every day until the road shuts, and it will cost me a third of everything I earn, and there is not one hour of it that the county will ever know about.”

“You could stop going to the lane.”

“I could stop going to the lane and I would have a licence that says I may take paid work outside the crew and no work at all,” said Ning Yan. “That is the paper I have. It is a door and a roof over a hole, and I have to walk through it to prove the door works.”

He was quiet for a moment. She lay in the lamplight with her arm out to the side and felt the shape of the arrangement the way she had felt it since the sixth day of the winter, the one that was not new and was not going to end.

“I am not going to ask you to stop either,” she said, “and we are not going to talk about that, because we did it in the spring and I meant it and nothing has changed it.”

“Not yet,” said Yuan.

“Not for a long time,” said Ning Yan.

At the bottom of the lane the mat was standing on the bank where she had left it, with the stones still in it, and eleven people had walked round a mat all afternoon without one of them having been asked to do anything at all about it.
