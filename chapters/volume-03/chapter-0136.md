# The Lawful Request

She was on the fourth stair with a slate frame under her arm when he came up the last flight.

He was carrying nothing. That was the thing she noticed first and did not understand for about a minute. A man who has come six times to a shut door comes up it with something in his hands or he comes up it with his hands behind him, and he came up this one with his hands behind him and no paper at all, which she did not understand and which turned out not to matter.

“Feng,” he said.

Feng was on the copying floor with a sheet in front of him and the lamp turned away and his back to the stair, the way he sat. He turned round.

“Two things,” said the man on the stair. “One is a request and it is in writing and it is stamped, and I am leaving it with you and not with a clerk, because the request is for your stair and a stair is not the county's.”

“Show me,” said Feng.

He did not come up. He came up two steps and put a folded sheet on the top stair and stepped back down, and he did not read it out and did not stand over it, and he stayed four steps below with his hands at his sides.

Feng came and got it.

Ning Yan did not move. She had one hand on the rail and the slate frame under the other arm and she had been in the stairwell for about a minute before either of them had come, and there was nowhere on four flights that she could go that was not a flight of stairs.

The man looked at her.

“Good evening,” he said.

“Good evening,” said Ning Yan.

Then he went down the four stairs and out into the lane at the pace of a man with nowhere in particular to be, and the lamp in its cage on the fourth landing went on burning, and Feng stood on the top stair with a folded sheet in both hands.

## What Was On It

She did not ask to see it. She stood where she was for the length of a count and a half and then came up the last flight because she was going to see it either way and doing it without asking was the only version of doing it that did not put anything on him.

It was one sheet. Ordinary stationery, the county's own size, with the county mark at the top in the ordinary place, and below that a stamp she did not have a name for, in grey, pressed lightly.

*To the keeper of the Lantern Register at Lowwater. Request is made for the paper this stair carries upward for the quarter, in the form in which it is carried, at the quarter next following.*

*This is a lawful request. It is answered in writing. An answer that is not given is a refusal.*

*Answer at the quarter, in the form the stair carries.*

“Four hundred volumes off the warm shelf,” said Feng.

He said it to himself more than to her, in the voice of a man reading out the size of a thing.

“All of it.”

“All of it, and the working sheets, and the quarter's bundle, and the column.”

“Read me the margin.”

“There is no margin.”

“There is a margin on every form this office sends out,” said Feng, “and on this one it is in a hand I have not seen and it says one thing, and it says *in the form in which it is carried*, and that is the whole difficulty in nine words.”

He turned the sheet round.

The margin was in a small hand. Six words.

*Including what is not entered.*

Ning Yan read it twice.

“That is not an ordinary request,” she said.

“No,” said Feng.

“That is the most complete request I have ever seen written down.”

“It is complete,” said Feng. “It is complete and it is stamped and it names the stair and the quarter and nothing else in nine counties, and there is not one word in it that is wrong, and I have been a keeper for twenty-two years and I would not have thought of the margin in a hundred years.”

## The Thing She Did Not Do

There was a thing she could have done and she stood on a top stair and worked out exactly what it was, in about four seconds, with the sheet in front of her.

She could have gone down four flights and across the square to the county office on the county's own paper and said: there is a keeper of sixty who copies cold all winter, and a man at a first desk has asked him for his work, and he will hand it over.

Not in those words. Nobody says anything in those words. She could have said it in the ordinary way — that she was a licensee with hours in the county book, that she had a lawful interest in what a stair carries about her, that a member is entitled to know what is written upward. Every clause of that would have been true and every clause of it would have been a reason, and a request carries a reason and goes in a book, and the county writes down reasons.

And Feng would have been off the stair.

Not dismissed. Worse. Asked for the volume on his own bench, in a room with two men in it, on a morning, with a clerk writing.

She put her hand flat on the rail.

“No,” she said.

Feng looked at her.

“You have not said anything.”

“I was going to say no to something in my own head,” said Ning Yan, “and I have never once in my life done that on a stair and I am not starting at sixty.”

“It would have worked.”

“It would have worked and it would have gone in a book with my name on it in the first line and yours in the fourth.”

Feng went back into the copying room and put the request on his bench, squared with the edge, the way he did with everything.

## The One Question

She asked one and it was the only one she had, and she had gone up the four flights with it already formed.

“Can you refuse it.”

Feng did not answer for a while. The tower was very quiet in the way that a building is quiet when it is full of paper.

“No,” he said.

“Can you delay it.”

“No. It says at the quarter next following and the quarter does not read an answer late, because a quarter that reads answers late is a quarter that reads them whenever it likes.”

“Can you answer it partly.”

“No,” said Feng, “and if I tried it would be the worst thing I have ever done in this room, because a partial answer to a request for the paper a stair carries is not a smaller truth. It is a bigger lie.”

She stood in the doorway of the copying floor of the Lantern Register tower in Lowwater and understood the whole of what was about to happen, and there was no part of it she could get out of.

“Then it is the whole thing,” she said.

“It is the whole thing,” said Feng. “Everything the stair carries for the quarter, in the form the stair carries it, including what is not entered. And that includes the volumes, and the working sheets, and the column, and the bundle, and a line I wrote at the back of a fair volume off the warm shelf in the spring because I could not think what else to do with it.”

He did not look at her when he said the last part.

“I wrote that line about you,” said Feng. “It is the only line about you in nine counties that is written down anywhere, and I wrote it in a fair hand off a warm shelf at the back of a volume, and it is not in the column and it is not indexed and it is exactly the kind of thing that a man who has thought about this for six months would write down.”

“Yes,” said Ning Yan.

“I am telling you because you are going to find out what is in the answer in about a quarter and I would rather you found out from me.”

“No,” said Ning Yan. “You answer it and I will not read it before it goes, and if I read it before it goes then I am a member of the public who has read a thing she got written about her, and a keeper who shows a member what a stair carries is not a keeper either.”

Feng looked at the brush on the bench for a while.

“You are the first member I have ever had in twenty-two years,” he said.

## The Lane

She went down the four stairs and out into the lane and across the square to the hiring end of it, and the man at the first desk was there, and he was reading a notice about the water rate with a sheet of his own under it, and he bought nothing while she watched for about the length of two counts.

He had a certified copy of her furnace schedule in a folder somewhere. He had had it since a day in the spring. He had a receipt in his own hand, folded square, that he had made a clerk write out in front of a queue.

He had bought nothing else in all the weeks since, and he was going to go on buying nothing else, and she understood with a clarity that was almost physical that the request on the top stair had cost him the price of a sheet of ordinary paper and a walk up four flights, and that everything else in this had come out of the county for free.

She went and got the escort contractor's slate.

He was at the weigh-station end of the Lowwater road with a string of six empty carts and a boy holding the near horse, and he was a thick man of about fifty with a knee that had been badly set in a cart wheel and he hired by the day and by the train and had eleven men who stood about a road doing nothing at a time.

“Guard's hire,” he said, before she had got ten words out. “I have had it on the board two weeks. Can you walk?”

“Yes.”

“Can you carry a load up a grade on the long side?”

“Yes.”

“I am not asking whether you can. I am asking whether you can at the end of a day and I want your answer for the end of a day.”

“I have been doing it at the end of a day for nine months,” said Ning Yan.

He wrote her on the board in chalk.

“Five weeks to the close of the season's road,” said the contractor. “Down to the fourth bridge and back with the trains. Twenty coppers a day and a quarter of the train's share. The road shuts with the weather and not with me.”

“Twenty coppers and a quarter.”

“A quarter. The train pays out by the load and there are eight of us on it and a ninth on the bridge.”

“And if the road shuts.”

“If the road shuts you get the day and no share, and that is the whole of the bad weather clause and I have never had a man argue with it.”

She looked at the board.

Eight men and a ninth on the bridge.

Her licence said paid work outside the crew, under countersignature, and twenty coppers and a quarter of a train on the Lowwater road was paid work outside the crew, and it went on the furnace schedule by certification like every other hour she had ever worked, and it would be stamped by a man with an irregular mark on it in a Hall with no roof.

“No,” said Ning Yan, “I will not argue with it.”

The contractor looked at her for about a count.

“Most people argue about the weather clause,” he said.

“Most people have not had a season’s road close on them once.”

He chalked her a second mark under the first and did not charge her for a slate.

## What She Did Not Say to Him

She did not say it to him at the ninth hour on the eighty-third night, lying on the mat with her arm out to the side and the lamp turned away.

“There is a thing that is going to happen to a man of sixty in this tower in about a quarter,” said Ning Yan, “and I found it out tonight, and I found it out because a man who has never once asked me anything stood on his fourth stair six times and did not ask me a question until he had written one down.”

“You could have stopped it.”

“No.”

“He is going to be suspended.”

“Yes.”

“You knew that before you got here,” said Yuan.

“I knew it the moment I read the margin,” said Ning Yan. “I have been sitting up this stair for about two hours working out whether I was reading it right and I was.”

She lay with it.

“He has a column at five and four hundred volumes and a cough and twenty-two years,” she said, “and the whole of what he has ever done for me in fourteen months is written at the back of one of them in a fair hand, and in about a quarter a clerk is going to read that line in a county room in front of a man with clean hands.”

“I am not going to tell you it was the right thing.”

“I know you are not.”

“I am going to tell you that he wrote it at the back of a volume off a warm shelf in the spring because he had four hundred volumes and a cough and a foreign clause on his licence and nothing else to do with any of it, and that a man who does that is not a man who is going to be asked to write out everything he has ever done with his own hands.”

“I know that too,” said Ning Yan. “That is not why I am frightened.”

“Why are you frightened.”

She lay on the mat in the dark with the lamp turned away and the request folded on a bench four miles below her and one quarter between them.

“Because I asked him a question on a stair and the answer was *no* and there was nothing in it,” she said, “and because a man who cannot lie is worse than a man who can, and because there is no version of the next three months in which I am not going to be the reason for something that happens to that man.”
