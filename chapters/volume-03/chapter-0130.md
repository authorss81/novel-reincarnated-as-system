# Level With the Asked

The quota office was at the back of the furnace building and it had four tables in it and one window, and an anteroom in front of it with four benches, and a frame on the back wall with twelve unsigned sheets in it that she had looked at once, in the thaw, and had entered in a log in a code, and was not going to look at again for as long as she lived.

She came up on a cart day on the thirty-first and sat on the second bench for two hours and eleven minutes.

That was the anteroom's whole function. Men sat on the benches because the man inside did not hurry and because a man who is waiting outside an office is not inside it, and there were three other men on the benches that day and one of them had been there since the second hour.

The window was open. It was the only window in the building that was open in the middle of the day and it had been open since the thaw and Shen Xiu had had it propped with a piece of a broken slate because a room with a shut window in the summer is a room where a clerk makes mistakes and he had made enough of them.

## The Annex

She put it on the table with her left hand.

It was the Hall's own duplicate, certified at the Hall, stamped by a maintaining member, four weeks long, and the asked figure was at the top of the column in the Hall clerk's hand and the worked figure was under it, and they were not the same, and the difference was the whole of the argument.

Shen Xiu read it. He read it the way he read everything, at the speed of a man who has been reading the same kind of thing for years and is not impressed by the container.

He did not look up for a long time.

“You have asked for a hundred and six hours in a season that has seven weeks left in it,” he said.

“Yes.”

“You have worked thirty-one.”

“Thirty-four, at the hour this morning. The figure on the certified sheet was the figure on the certified sheet.”

“It was.” He set it down square. “You are capped at five and the season has seven weeks in it and there is a stone end in Lowwater that pays by the load and a road that shuts with the weather, and there is no version of your life in which you work a hundred and six hours of annex before the books close.”

“And you are not a woman who goes into a book with a number she cannot reach, because I have had this conversation with a man in the east who did and I had to carry his gap for two years.”

“I cannot work a hundred and six.”

“No,” said Shen Xiu. “You cannot. And the number on the sheet is the number on the sheet, and the number on the sheet is a hundred and six, and it came down here off a Hall clerk's pen about four days ago, and I am required to certify a number or refuse it.”

## The Second Number

He turned the sheet round.

“Clearance is two numbers,” he said. “You know what the two are.”

“Yes.”

“Your line. And the book.”

“And the second one is not mine.”

“It has never been yours,” said Shen Xiu, “and it is not yours now, and it will not be yours this afternoon whatever you have come up a mountain for, and I am telling you where it stands because you have not asked me for it and because the last time we sat in this room you did not ask me for it either, and I would like the two omissions to be understood to be the same omission.”

She said nothing.

“It stands at four thousand one hundred and eleven hours out,” said Shen Xiu, “and it is out on five estates, and none of them is yours, and it is out on five estates because of a season that ended in the autumn and not this one.”

“I am telling you the figure and not the names because I do not give the names and I did not give them to you in the spring either. It is not yours to have. It will be level or accounted for at the close of the year and it is not going to be level this month and it is not going to be level because of anything you do.”

“All right.”

“All right,” said Shen Xiu. “Now. You have a hundred and six on a sheet and thirty-four on it. Tell me the number you can stand behind.”

“It has to be level at the close of the season.”

“It has to be level at the close of the season,” said Shen Xiu, “and the season closes when the last week of it closes and not before, and I do not close it early for one arm, and I do not close it early because a woman came up four miles on a cart day with a sheet.”

He looked at her for the first time.

He had not looked at her once in the whole of the winter and the spring and the thaw. He had signed clearance for her in the spring looking past her, and had entered three hundred and eleven hours in a column of his own, and had never once in any of those months put his eyes on her face at a table.

He looked at her now for about a count and a half.

“Your hours are not the only hours in that annex,” he said.

It was the first question he had ever asked her.

“No,” said Ning Yan.

“Whose hours are in it. Yours alone, or the crew's.”

## The Answer

“Yours alone.”

“Yours alone, worked outside the crew, paid by a stranger, annexed onto a Qinghe furnace schedule by a Hall copyist and certified by a Hall clerk and stamped by a maintaining member, and the hours are not signed by anyone and are counted at the load and not by a foreman, and there is a document in the county saying that a sheet the Hall keeps is furnace hours by certification and not by place.”

“That document is this office's business.”

“It is the county's business and you have the answer to it and I have given it to you in the right words and you may write it down.”

Shen Xiu wrote it down. He wrote it in the quota book on the facing page, in his own hand, at the ordinary speed of a man entering something, and he did not look at the frame on the back wall while he did it and neither did she.

“Then it is one name,” he said.

“It is one name.”

“And it is not a household.”

“It is not a household. It is a woman with a mat on her shoulder putting a stone down in a channel at the end of a lane, and there is no household anywhere in that arrangement and none of the nine in that furnace column has a hour of it.”

“Which is why the three of them will not seal a sheet that says nine while it is like that,” said Shen Xiu.

She did not answer.

“I am not asking you how I know,” he said. “I have run a book for twenty-two years and a schedule of Qinghe has been certified by that Hall for a hundred and forty years, and there is exactly one reason a maintaining member will hold three marks off a sheet, and I have seen the other reasons. Say nothing.”

“Say nothing.”

## What He Would Not Do

He would not weight it.

That was the whole of the afternoon, and she had known it would be the whole of the afternoon about eleven minutes after she came in, and she had still come up four miles to be told it.

“Not at a third of a load,” said Shen Xiu. “I have had three offices ask me for a rate this year and two of them had a reason on paper and one of them did not, and I did not do it for any of them. The fourth estate pays you twelve coppers a load and that is a rate. The eighth estate pays a girl four hours and works her six and that is a rate.”

“You are not getting a rate out of me for an arm that opens about one time in three, because in about nine weeks somebody upstairs will want to know why a furnace line has an annex in it at two thirds of a rate, and the answer will have to be in a form I can read out.”

“I never asked for a rate.”

“You came up a mountain with a hundred and six on a sheet and thirty-four worked, and you had worked out in Lowwater that the only way through this room was a smaller number,” said Shen Xiu. “You did not come to ask for a rate. You came to hand me a number I could sign, and I am going to make you find it yourself, because if I pick it, it is mine, and it will be in my book for the rest of my life.”

He turned the sheet round and put the brush on the table in front of it.

“Take the brush and put your own number on it. Not your left hand and not a clerk's. Your own, in your own hand, and then I will write under it what I think of it, and then it goes in the book with two names on it and neither of them is mine alone.”

## The Number

She took the brush in her left hand and wrote a number on the certification line.

It was fifty-eight.

That was what she had worked out on a loft mat on the twenty-eighth night with a docket in her coat, and it was not a round number and it was not a hopeful number.

It was six loads a day on the days the road was open and three hours at the water channel a day at a rate that was a third of what a two-armed woman gets out of the same channel in the same hour, which is what she had actually been doing since the fourth day of the first week of summer and had not admitted to anybody.

Fifty-eight hours in the seven weeks that were left.

Shen Xiu read it.

“Where does fifty-eight come from.”

“Six loads and about forty minutes at the water in a day, and the road is open and it shuts in the weather, and I have counted the weeks.”

“You have counted the weeks.”

“I count everything. It is a fault.”

He wrote under her number, in his own hand, and read it back because he read everything back in that room.

*Fifty-eight hours asked by the applicant and certified as the figure the applicant can stand behind. The figure is her own and is entered over her own hand and is not weighted by this office. The line will stand at the close of the season as asked and worked or it will show a gap, and a gap is a gap.*

Then he put the book down and picked up the brush again and signed.

*Second quota clearance, season closed, granted. Annex certified to the furnace schedule of Qinghe, additional hours of one member, paid outside the crew, annexed by certification and not by place.*

He signed it at the fourth hour of the afternoon on the thirty-first day of the first weeks of summer, on the third table, in a room with four tables and one window, and he did it without looking up and he did it in one stroke, which is a thing he has never done for anybody.

## The Signature

She read it three times.

It was there. It was the second clearance and it was in the quota book of the furnace building of Qinghe Sect in a hand that could not be mistaken for a Hall clerk's or a registrar's or hers, and it had cost her a number she had chosen herself in front of the man who would have had to carry it, and fifty-eight hours of a lane and a channel between the second hour and the end of the season.

And he had signed it in one stroke, and he had not asked her for anything, and he had not looked at her while he did it.

“You did not look at me,” said Ning Yan.

“No.”

“You signed it without looking up.”

“No,” said Shen Xiu, “because a signature is made on the paper and not on the person, and a man who looks up while he signs a book has stopped doing the one thing the book is for.”

He put the brush down and squared it.

“Go down the road,” he said. “They are waiting on you and they have been waiting on you for four days, and they are waiting on this, and there is no version of a ceiling at nine in this bound that does not start with a line in this book.”

She stood up.

“Shen Xiu.”

“Twenty years ago I sent a report on a boy and it was four days early and it cost him a place he should have had,” said Shen Xiu, without looking up, “and I have never once told anybody that, and I am telling you it now because you are going to go down four miles with a clearance in your hand and a ceiling to write and you are entitled to know that there is a thing under this desk that would have me put back in a corner.”

She did not ask about the report.

She did not say thank him.

She said, “I have not forgiven you,” which was true, and which had been true since the eighth day of the winter in a room with four tables and a frame on the back wall, and she said it in the ordinary voice of a woman closing a door she had not opened.

“I know,” said Shen Xiu. “I have not asked.”

## Two Numbers

She went out into the corridor and stood in the anteroom among four benches with a clearance in her coat and fifty-eight hours in front of her, and it took her about nine seconds standing on the step to understand what had actually happened in that room and what it had cost and who had paid it.

Two numbers. Her line and the book. And the second one was not hers and was never going to be hers and he had put the figure on the table in the middle of it without being asked, because she had not asked, on purpose, for the fourth time in eleven months.

And there had been a question. One question, in a room where he had never asked her anything, and it had been the right one, and he had asked it about the annex and not about her, and that was the difference between a man who has decided something about you and a man who has decided what a piece of paper is.

He had asked whether the hours were hers or the crew's, and she had said hers, and a man who works a book for twenty-two years is going to know what a member's hours being in one column and not another means in his sleep.

That was the part that sat badly.

Not the signature. The signature was a line in a book and he had made it in one stroke and she had the whole of it.

Something else: he had not weighed it.

Not for her, not for anyone, not for the fact that she was the only member in eleven years to advance on a schedule that had nine households struck out of it, and when she had been in that room in the spring she had believed, without ever having tested it, that a man with a quota book would eventually move a figure for an arm, and he had told her in the thaw that he would not, and she had gone away from it not believing him.

Down the four miles of road she worked out the whole of what the clearance had bought, because it was the first leg and it had to be done first and three men in a corridor had made her understand why before they had said it.

Three men who could not account for a member in a book were carrying a liability and had spent two volumes refusing to create one. Three men who could account for her, exactly, to the hour, in a column of their own with a stamp on it, had a member on a schedule that any office with a reason could examine.

She had gone up the mountain in the spring and been sealed first and then cleared and then refused.

She had come down it now and been refused first, by a man who would not look at her, at a number she had written herself in front of him — and the refusal had put fifty-eight hours and a signed line into a book that three men upstairs could read with a finger, and had turned her from a liability into a schedule.

Three marks on one sheet, in a corridor at the west end of a building with no roof, and after that a registrar who does not hurry.
