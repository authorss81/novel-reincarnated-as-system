# The Notice

The sheet on the shelf by the door was not an order. It was a notice, and the difference between the two is that an order has somebody's standing behind it and a notice has a rule's, and a rule does not have to want anything.

It said that the winter return for this county would be read at the second hour on the nineteenth of the month, that a member whose hours were on it was to stand in the room while they were read, and that a member who was not in the room would have her hours read without her.

There was a number at the top and a date at the bottom, six days old, and the date was the day a clerk had written it out, and no human being had put it there at all.

She read it four times. She had read it four times on the first morning and she read it four times again standing up, because the first time she had been in a hurry and the second time she had not been.

## The Line Underneath

Under the line about standing in the room there was a second line, and the second line was the one that had been written by a registrar and not by a rule.

*A member attending may be shown the sheet before it is read, and may ask for an hour not to be read to be entered as read and not as absent.*

She read that six times too.

That was Sun He. Nineteen years at that table and a rule with a door in it, put in writing, in the ordinary hand, under a notice a clerk had copied out without understanding it, so that any member of this county who wanted to stand in the room could do it knowing in advance exactly what the room would have to do with her face.

He had written that line for her. She was nearly certain of it, and she could not prove it, and it did not matter, because a line under a notice is either for one person or for everybody and a registrar does not write lines for everybody.

## The Room She Promised

She had said it in the crew quarters, four days after the winter schedule went on the Hall board, and she had said it out loud, and the reason she had said it out loud was that she wanted it said to somebody.

*It is in a county office on the square four miles off, waiting to be read in front of a room, and I am not going to be in that room when it is.*

She had been in this county office about four hundred times in eighteen months. She had been made legal in this building and hunted in this building and refused an instrument in this building in front of two witnesses, and it was the safest room she knew, and she had told a man with a certified copy and a receipt that she would not stand in it for a reading of her own hours.

“You said it in a room with nine men in it,” said Yuan.

“I said it in a room with nine men in it and I said it to myself and it was the same as saying it out loud, because I said it on purpose.”

“You said it in a room with nine men in it to me, and none of them heard you.”

“No,” said Ning Yan. “And that is not why it was said.”

## What Going Cost

Four miles down in the cold and four miles up, and a morning in four that the crew would have to cover, and the third flight at the bottom of the climb with three counts in it at the best hour of the day.

And the wash house, where four paid hours a week were hers to be at or not to be at, and the girl of twenty-four would have to hold the trough alone for a morning, and the eighth estate would want a word about it.

That was the whole of the cost and it was four or five coppers' worth of a life and a morning of a woman's bad leg, and she wrote it out in figures on the back of the slate she used for hours, because that is what she did, and then she wrote one more figure under it.

Zero days of loading. The yard that sells stone by the load was four days east and the season's road shut on the one hundred and seventeenth day and it is not going to open before the thaw. Da Shan gets his stone off a bank up the east road at nine coppers and is charged for it by weight whether she is buying or not, so the four loads a week going onto the fourth table come out of a tin, and no morning in four changes that.

The cost of going into that room was a morning and a woman's bad leg.

The cost of not going was a column with three holes in it, read into a room that decides.

## What She Would Do In It

She worked it out on the walk up the four miles, in the order a person works out a thing they are frightened of, which is to decide what you will do before you get there and then do that.

One. She would stand in the room.

Two. She would sign her own column herself, when it came to her, and she would not let a clerk do it and she would not let a man with a certified copy see it before it was signed.

Three. She would not explain the fourth. She had held that one down alone on the flats with a stone in her hand and a registrar had ruled the empty column an absence, and there was no office in nine counties that had ever heard of it, and she was not going to tell it in a room at the second hour.

Four. She would not explain the eighth either. Same reason. Same silence.

Five. She would not name the thing in her head, and if a man asked her what it was she would answer him the way she had answered him in the summer, in the ordinary voice, and if he wrote that down it would be his line and not hers.

Six. She would not thank anybody. Not the registrar, not the clerk, not the man at the back of the room who bought nothing, and least of all the man in her head who was not being asked for anything at all.

That was the whole of it and it took about a mile and a half of a four-mile hill, and she had the last of it settled before she got to the wash house road.

## Telling the Girl

She went down to the wash house before she went up, because there was a morning in four in the account and it was not hers to spend.

The girl was at the trough with the last copied list in front of her and a quarter in her coat, and she looked up when Ning Yan came in and did not ask what was wrong, which is a thing that people who are paid by the week learn to do very quickly.

“The nineteenth,” said Ning Yan. “First hour to fourth. I will be off the trough and you will have it alone.”

“Then I will hold it alone,” said the girl.

“It is four paid hours and you are on a bad leg and it is a cold morning and there is nothing to copy.”

“There is nothing to copy,” said the girl. “That is the whole of my week as well.”

Ning Yan stood at the trough for about four seconds and worked out that she had come down here to give a person an excuse and had been handed a fact instead, and that the fact was that there was nothing on that board this month for anybody to be paid for.

“Write to the eighth estate,” she said. “Tell them the wash house held on the nineteenth on a member’s attendance and that the hours are the same hours and are not shortened, and that if they shorten them I will enter it at my next county hour as a shortening of a working member’s paid time by a third party, which is a line on their sheet and not on mine.”

“That is a threat.”

“It is a filing,” said Ning Yan. “It is a thing I will do on purpose and I would rather have it on their sheet than have them shorten your hours and have you say nothing.”

The girl looked at the trough for a while.

“All right,” she said. “I will write it. And I will tell the three of them this week, because you are not going to, and because that is what I am for.”

“That is what you are for.”

“It is four coppers a week,” said the girl, “and what I am for is standing at a trough on a bad leg in the cold, and you have been doing the other thing about nine months and you have never once got the two mixed up.”

She had not. That was the whole of what the girl was for, and it was the reason Ning Yan had paid her in advance and it was the reason she was going to be careful about it now, and she went out of the wash house and up four miles with the receipt of the filing in her coat and four coppers a week on a board that had nothing on it.

## Telling the Crew

She told them at the ninth hour on the floor, standing at the near end where the grate was, with eight men and her own shift signed for and the cold end forty feet behind her.

“I am off the floor for a morning on the nineteenth,” she said. “The county is reading the winter return and a member whose hours are on it stands in the room while they are read. Gao, that is your morning and it is not mine, and I would take it back if I could, and I cannot, because the sheet says a member and I am the member.”

Nine men looked at the floor.

“Say it again,” said Gao, “and say how long, because a morning on this floor is a morning of nine.”

“A morning. First hour to fourth. I will be back for the fifth and I will have the cold end for the rest of it.”

“That is a morning of nine and a half,” said Gao, “for a piece of paper.”

“Yes.”

“Then it is a morning of nine and a half,” he said, and turned round and went back to the grate, and that was the end of it, and there was no further question about it from any of them, which was what she had asked them for in a month when a word she had put on the front of their own schedule had put two men out of work in a lane.

## The Week Before

The reading was on the nineteenth. The attendance was on the sixteenth, three days before it, and that was the standing order Sun He had entered under a refusal in a book in a press under a counter, and she had been coming in once a month since the middle of the summer for a morning and a signature and about eleven minutes of standing in a room that wrote down what was said in it.

On the sixteenth she would be read and she would sign and she would go home.

On the nineteenth the whole column would be read, in order, in a room with a queue at the door.

She went up the hill and the third flight cost three counts and she let it be three, and she lay in the second bunk with nine men breathing and the fifth week of the cold end two days off the end of it, and the ninth stood up in her like a thing waiting for the last door to open.

Three days to the attendance. Three days after that, the room.
