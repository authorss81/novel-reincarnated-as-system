# A Tower He Cannot Feed

She spent the whole of the ninety-sixth day looking for one honest reason to tell a man of sixty-two not to take a hundredweight of coal, and she did not find one, and she went about it properly.

She started at the bottom, which was the road.

The clerk came up at the third hour with a satchel and got the quarter's bundle off the shelf by the door and went down four flights with it, and the bundle was a quarter's bundle and it was heavy and there were four hundred and eleven volumes on the shelves behind him and eleven on the bench and four on the working rack, and everything the stair carried for three months was going down those stairs in a satchel in a clerk's hand.

Then there was the lamp.

That was the thing that got her, and she did not see it until the fourth hour of the afternoon and she went back and looked at it twice.

The lamp in the cage on the fourth landing had been burning since she was a servant in a crew quarters. She had never once known a day when it was not. She had thought about it as a thing a building does, the way a furnace is hot and a river runs, and she had never asked what it was for or who paid for the oil.

He was leaving it burning an hour past the hour it was needed. That was not a kindness to her. She went and asked him about it at the sixth hour, in the ordinary voice, and he said, without looking up, that a keeper's lamp burns while the keeper is in the building.

It had burned about six hours a day for twenty-two years while there was nine hours of copying in it.

## The Arithmetic of a Winter

She did it on the lane, at the second hour, with a mat on her shoulder and a queue behind her, because that is where she did all her arithmetic now.

A keeper of sixty-two, eleven years of a cough, four flights, copying cold from the sixth hour in autumn through the whole of a winter and out the other side.

Twenty-two years he had done it. Not one of those twenty-two winters had been different, because he had never had a fire and he had never had oil in the room he sat in and he had never once had a hundredweight of coal come to a door.

The coal was a hundredweight. It would be about four weeks, if he burned it the way a man burns it who has been cold for eleven years, which is to say badly and at night.

Four weeks out of a winter. He would still be cold for eight of the twelve.

And she worked the other half of it, which was the half she did not want to work, which was that he had already worked eleven winters like the one coming and had come out of every one of them, and was here, standing, copying, with a column at five and four hundred and eleven volumes on his shelves.

There was no arithmetic in this that made a hundredweight of coal into a small thing.

## What She Went and Found Out

She went to the carriers' shed at the third hour and asked about coal, and a man told her that a hundredweight came up the Lowwater road four days before the cold and went to a door and was the cheapest thing anybody bought all year, and that a cart of it at that time of year went up in about eleven days, and that the road shut with the weather and not with him.

Eleven days.

That was the whole of the argument, and it took her about four minutes at a shed door to find it and she had not found it in a day of looking, because she had been standing in a tower and a lane thinking about a man's lungs and not about a road.

A hundredweight of coal comes up a road in about eleven days, and the road shuts when the weather shuts, and it is cheap, and it is a door.

So there was no reason. Not one.

She went and stood at the fourth bridge at the fifth hour and watched two trains down and one up and did not hear the second question, which was the one she had been walking toward all day.

## The Second Question

The second question was the one she could not get around.

Feng Lushi was sixty-two and had a cough that had got worse every year for eleven years, and there was a hundredweight of coal and a district wage and a chair with a fire in it in a building four miles from this one, and he would be sixty-three in the winter and sixty-four in the one after that.

He was not going to die this year. She had looked at him at the sixth hour and the ninth hour for fourteen months and she knew what a man going to die in the winter looked like and he was not it.

And the whole of the cost of the thing — the part that was written on four lines of a sheet and read out in a plain voice in a copying room — was that he would sit in a county room at the second hour on the county's reading days and report in full, in order, whether or not it was entered.

She had worked that out on the loft mat at about the second hour and she had not liked it and she had not been able to make it smaller by working at it again.

## What She Told Him

She said it at the sixth hour on the ninety-sixth, on the copying floor, with the lamp behind him and her slate frame still on the bench where she had left it at the second hour.

“Feng. You said you would give him an answer at the ninth hour.”

“I will.”

“I am not asking you not to take it.”

He put his brush down.

“I know you are not,” he said. “You have had a day and you have not once come up this stair and told me to refuse it, and I have been sitting here since the sixth hour working out how many times a person is supposed to be told to refuse a thing and I could not get the number.”

“I have come up the stair twice.”

“You have been in this room twice,” said Feng. “That is a different count and you know it is a different count.”

She put her hand flat on the bench.

“There are two things,” said Ning Yan, “and the first is worth nothing. The coal comes up the Lowwater road four days before the cold and it takes about eleven days to come at that time of year and it is the cheapest thing anybody buys, and the road shuts with the weather and not with him, and if the road shuts before it comes then there is no coal and that is the whole of the bad case.”

“Thank you.”

“The second one is not worth nothing and I am not going to pretend it is.”

He waited.

“You are going to sit in a county room with a fire in it for the rest of your life,” said Ning Yan, “and every hour I put in a column of a furnace schedule is going to be in a report that goes up to an office above this county, in order, because a man at a first desk asked a keeper for his paper and a keeper could not lie.”

“Yes,” said Feng.

“I know you know.”

“I have known since the eighth hour of the day you came up that stair and I did not ask,” said Feng. “I did not ask because I am sixty-two and there is a hundredweight of coal in the third term of this and I am not able to be proud in the fourth.”

## The Four Miles He Walked

The man came up at the ninth hour on the ninety-sixth and knocked on the frame of the door with the back of a knuckle, and Feng was not on the bench.

He was in the county office on the square.

He came back up the four miles at the second hour of the next morning with his coat buttoned to the throat and the sheet still inside it, and she met him on the second landing and did not ask.

“The county's book says four lines about a Listener's appointment and so does his sheet. It has a wage at the district rate. It has oil and a hundredweight of coal. It has a chair on the county's reading days at the second hour with the county's fire. It has the reporting line.”

“Anything else.”

“It has a line at the bottom that says the appointment is not entered in the county's own register, because it is not a county appointment, and that the county will hold the appointee's attendance on the reading days and will not enquire further.”

“So there is nothing in the county's book against it.”

“There is nothing in the county's book for it and nothing against it,” said Feng. “I walked four miles to find that out and I am going to tell you why I walked four miles.”

“You are going to tell me why.”

“I have been a keeper for twenty-two years and I have never in twenty-two years signed a piece of paper that I had not read against two other books,” said Feng. “That is not doubt. You are allowed to think it is doubt and it is not. It is the whole of what I am and there is nothing else in me.”

## What the Girl Did Not Say

She had, in fact, said one more thing on the wash house step, on the ninety-fifth, with her back to the water.

“He is not going to ask you for anything,” said the girl. “That is what a washerwoman who hauls for the coal trade told me, and she wanted it said. A man with a chair and a fire is a man who does not want anything off anybody, because everything he has then comes up a stair without him asking.”

“I know,” said Ning Yan.

“No, you do not, because you have never in your life had a thing that came up a stair without you asking.”

She went in with her slate and her leg wrong and did not look back, which she never did, and which Ning Yan had learned in fourteen months not to read as anything at all.

## The Threshold

She went up the four miles at the ninth hour on the ninety-seventh, and she came into the copying room and Feng Lushi was on the bench with a sheet in front of him that was not a working sheet.

The lamp in its cage on the fourth landing had been burning all night, which was new, and which she was not going to ask about.

The appointment was beside the brush, folded once, squared with the edge.

“Your hand goes in the third line,” he said.

“I know it does.”

“I read the whole thing twice before I read it to him and once after, and I want you to know that I read it three times, because you would have found out if I had not.”

“I would have.”

“And I want you to know the other half of it,” said Feng. “I read it three times because I was looking for the place where it lies, and there is a place where it lies, and I did not find one, and there is not going to be one, because it does not lie anywhere. It is written exactly where it is written.”

He squared the sheet with the edge of the bench.

He did not pick up the brush.

“Ning Yan,” said Feng Lushi, “I want you in this room for this. Not at the door. In it.”

He put his hand flat on the sheet.

“I wrote one fair line about you at the back of a volume off a warm shelf in a spring I did not expect to see the end of. I am about to put my name under a line that says I will copy all of you for the rest of my life. I would like the woman who is written in it to be standing in the room when I do it.”

She came in and stood at the end of the bench.
