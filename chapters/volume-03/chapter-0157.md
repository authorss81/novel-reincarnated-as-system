# Nothing Chalked

The four weeks were up in the only way a thing like that is ever up, which is that Da Shan wiped the frame at the fourth hour and did not chalk anything on it, and then stood at the side of it for about a minute with his back to it, and then went and had a smoke.

There were four men at the fourth table and none at the first.

She stood at the side of the board for about four minutes herself, working out whether she was going to ask him anything, and then she went and got the mat and put in four loads because the fourth table was working and she was the only woman at it and the man at it did not have to say that out loud.

Twelve coppers a load, four loads, forty-eight coppers, and the light gone off the cut.

## What Was Left in the County

There is no paid work in this county for a licensed woman with one arm until the thaw. That is not a hardship and it is not an opinion. It is what happens to a county when the road shuts and the building work stops and every man in Lowwater who was paid by the day goes inside for the winter and waits for the road.

The water channel behind a well is not work. It is stone coming down off carts that are already going somewhere, and when there is no stone coming down it is not a job, it is a place to stand.

She had about nine weeks of savings in a lining and it was not nine weeks of savings, because she had been putting a third of every load into a tin at the wash house since the thaw and the tin had come to about two hundred and thirty coppers, and twelve coppers a load at the quarry for six days, at the five loads a day she was putting in since the ice, would be about three hundred and sixty a week if the block kept running.

That was the whole of the arithmetic of a winter and it came to about six weeks.

After six weeks there was the furnace floor, and the furnace floor was not paid, and the tithe, and whatever a person can sell.

## What She Did Not Say

Da Shan had a board, four tables and a wall of frames, and no other trade, and the board had been down to four men on a bad day and nine on a good one all through the cold, and two of those tables had been the fourth and the third since the thaw, and both of them had been the fourth and the third because a woman with one arm had put nine loads a day on the fourth and made it worth two men.

She had been working three miles east for eighteen days and she was going to be working three miles east on the next morning.

She did not tell him.

She worked it out on the road that night at about the eighth hour, with the wind coming off the cut, and she worked it out properly, the way she had worked out that she would not tell nine men about a refusal, and the shape of it was the same shape.

If she told him, then Da Shan was a man she had left, in the cold, with four men on a bad day, and she would owe him a version of it.

And if she told him for nothing, then she had told a man at a board in a lane that there is no work here this winter. The wrong man hearing that, in a lane, in a county this small, is a thing she cannot take back.

So she put in her four loads in the morning and took her mat and went, and she did not tell him, and she did not ask him for anything, and she has thought about that since and has not been able to make it into anything better than what it was.

## The Tower

She slept in the loft on the two nights a week when the quarry ran short and the four miles in the dark was not worth it, and she went up the four flights with three counts on them and came down with eight, and on the second night she sat on the mat and found that the room was warm.

Twenty-two years.

The keeper of the Lantern Register at Lowwater had gone twenty-two years without a fire in the room he worked in, on a stipend from a tower.

There was a hundredweight of coal at the foot of that stair in the first week of the cold, and a district wage that came four times a year, and she had gone up the four flights about nine times since the autumn and had not once said anything about it. There was not one thing she could say to him that was not a smaller thing than the thing he had done.

He was on the bench at the second hour with his coat off.

“You are warm,” said Ning Yan.

“I am warm,” said Feng Lushi, and did not look up, and wrote about nine more lines, and then said, without any change in the voice at all:

“The county's reading days are the ones you came in on. The chair is at the second hour on those days and the fire is lit at the first and the fire is the county's and I am permitted to use it and I have been permitted to use it since the autumn.”

“I know you have.”

“You did not come in on one.”

“No,” said Ning Yan.

He wrote for about four seconds.

“You did not have to,” said Feng Lushi.

“No,” she said. “I did not have to, and I want to say something about that and I have decided not to, and you are not going to get it out of me tonight, and that is because I do not forgive you and I have not started, and it is also because you are sixty-two and warm, and a man who is sixty-two and warm has about four good winters and I am not going to be the one who spends them.”

He put the brush down and looked at her for the first time.

“That is fair,” said Feng Lushi.

“It is more than fair. It is a discount,” said Ning Yan, “and it is the only one I am going to give you in this tower and I would like you to notice that I have said the word out loud instead of letting you work it out on your own, because I am tired and it is cold and I am going to be here two nights a week for six weeks.”

“It is noted,” said Feng Lushi, and picked the brush back up.

## What It Is to Be Paid

The odd thing, and the thing she could not have said out loud to anybody at a furnace floor, was that she had been **paid** for eleven months.

Eleven months of a slate with a name on it and a number against it every day, eight coppers, then twelve, then eighteen, and the whole of that had been a thing she had walked toward. She had built a day around it. She had put a county hour at the third hour in the middle of it on purpose so that the day had a shape and a floor in it and somewhere to stand at the end of it where a man wrote a number down.

And there was no number.

There was no number at all. There had not been a number for about four days and there was not going to be one behind a well for four months, and the tin had two hundred and thirty coppers in it and a crew at the ninth hour and a hand that was going to cost her a third of every load from here to the thaw.

That was the shape of it. It was not grief and it was not fear and it was duller than both of them, and it was going to go on a great deal longer.

## The Cold End at Qinghe

The furnace does not stop in the cold. That is the one thing about a furnace crew in a hard winter that is on the side of the people who live in it: the colder it gets the more the fire wants feeding, and by the second month of the cold the Qinghe floor runs an extra pair and the grate is loaded twice in the cold hours and a crew of nine is a crew of nine in a room where the heat comes off the stone.

She was on the cold end because she was on the cold end, and being on the cold end in the cold is not a punishment, it is just where you are.

Nine men. Twelve bunks. Ten sleepers, and the tenth was a boy of nineteen who had been on the settling floor since the thaw and had got a place on the crew in the autumn and did not talk much.

The extra pair went on in the second week. It went to the strongest four and her, because she was on the grate every night of the first month of the cold and had been since the thaw, and a crew that has worked out that a woman with one arm can hold a bar on a grate for four hours does not ask her.

That is what it is to be good at something for a year. Nobody says anything about it and it is put in a roster.

## Six Weeks

Eighteen days in and the hoist was about four men short, because the cold had taken two off and the block at the top was iced, and about two hundred and thirty coppers in a tin was not getting any heavier.

Bo wrote the number at the rope. Five loads before the light went.

She put in five loads and the hand held, and the hand was going to be a problem at about the six-load mark for the rest of her life, and she had worked that out in the nine days after the ice and had not told anybody and was not going to.

Six weeks of that.

Six weeks of five loads and twelve coppers and four and a half hours on a furnace floor at the ninth hour and a flight of stair that costs three counts up and eight down, and then six weeks after that with nothing at all in it, and then a thaw that was not going to come until the road came, and the road was not going to come until the weather did, and none of that had a date on it that anybody had ever written down.

She had worked out in the spring that there were no paid work and no road and no date on any of it, and she had worked it out again now with two hands instead of one and about two hundred and thirty coppers, and the second time round it was worse by about a third and she had done the whole of it in about nine minutes.

Nine weeks, and not one of them carrying a date she could put on a wall.

A winter on top of that.

She went up the third flight in the dark and counted her breath, and the count was three, and nine men were breathing in a room that had held twelve three winters ago.
