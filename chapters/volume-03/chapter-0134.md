# The Annex Is Work

The word irregular went onto the front of the Qinghe furnace schedule at the second hour of the forty-eighth day, in the Hall clerk's hand, with the Hall's mark under it, and by the second night hour that day four miles down the road it had been read by nine men.

The crew reads its own schedule. That is not a courtesy; the floor is checked against a mark and the marks are written down by whoever is standing nearest, and a word in a hand at the top of a schedule gets read on the floor the same afternoon because there is nothing else to read.

“Gao,” said one of them, at the ninth hour, at the furnace mouth. “It says irregular.”

“It says irregular,” said Ning Yan.

“Your column.”

“My column.”

“Gao, that is the Hall's mark and not hers.”

“It is the Hall's mark and not mine,” said Ning Yan, “and I asked for it, and if you want to know what it is for I will tell you in one sentence and then I am going back to the cold end.”

The man called Gao waited until he was sure, the way a man waits when he has decided to spend something.

“All right.”

“It says that the hours I am putting in a column of that schedule are mine and not the hiring board’s, and that they go up at the quarter on a sheet with my hand against them and the Hall clerk’s under them. That is the whole of it.”

She put her left hand up, palm out, the way she had put it up in front of a licensing clerk in the spring.

“Everything above five on my licence is written in that hand. And the figure in that column is short. And the short is in my hand. I would rather the county had the true one than a rate out of a book that was written before I had a licence.”

Gao looked at her for a while.

“Then it stays,” he said, and went back to the fire.

Nobody said anything else about it, which she had expected, and which was the entire reward for having said it out loud, and which was more than she had expected, and which she was not going to think about.

## The One Going Soft

The first licence was still nailed up crooked over the foot of her own bunk with a line of rain gone down it in the thaw, and the crease across the defect line had gone soft in a way you could see with a thumb.

She got up onto the bunk at the eighth night hour with the lamp at the foot of the stair and looked at it for a while.

Four feet of oilcloth-backed county paper. A stamp. Six conditions on the back in a registrar's ordinary hand, and the sixth of them eight hundred jin, and the crease going soft exactly where she had known it would go soft because she had watched it for a month.

It said five on the front of it. It had said five for four months and it would say five until the paper came off the wall, which it was not going to do tonight.

The second one was in her coat against her ribs, in oilcloth, above the plate, in the place that had been going soft in a different way for six months. It said nine. Seven conditions. The claim the seventh and the last thing on the paper.

She did not take the first one down. She was not going to have two pieces of county paper on one wall, and she was not going to have a new one up over a soft one, and she was aware that there is an arrangement where a licence is nailed straight above a servant's bunk in a room with nine men in it and every man in the room can read it, and that she was not doing that arrangement this week.

## What Nine Lets Her Do

She did it on the mat in the tower loft, on the forty-ninth night, with a slate frame and a stub of chalk, and it took about twenty minutes and she got it down in three columns.

The first column was what the relicensing gave her that the first one had not.

A ceiling of nine, which is a permission and is not a body. Hours in a column, which is work and not a wage. A claim travelling on the licence, which is protection and is exposure. A licence that any office with a stamp and a reason may read in order, which had not been true of the first one at five, and which she had got on purpose and would have got again.

The second column was what it took.

An hour of the furnace day every day, for a slate frame and a stamp. Four loads a county hour. Four miles twice on a cleared day. The irregular entry on the front of a schedule that three men read and nine men had read.

The third column she had to stop and think about, and it took the longest, and she wrote it at the top of the slate in bigger letters than the other two because she wanted it to be the first thing she read in the morning.

*What nine lets her do that five did not.*

It came out in about four minutes.

The scope line. She had read it in a county room in the spring without understanding it and had gone away thinking it was worth a hire.

It was worth more than a hire.

A member licensed to practise past registration may be **taught past her stage**, under countersignature, at that county. That is a sentence in the licence and it has been a sentence in the licence for four months and she had never once gone and found out what it meant.

It meant that there was, in nine counties, a lawful way for a woman to be taught.

## The Arithmetic of a Pupil

She sat with it.

A servant-born who breaks in the open may be taught past her stage under countersignature. It did not say by whom. It did not say at what cost, or out of whose hours, or for how long, or what a master of a furnace crew would be paid.

What it meant was that there was a door in it and she had been walking past the door for four months with a licence in her coat.

And then she did the arithmetic, which took her about eleven minutes and which she would have given a great deal not to have done.

A master who taught a servant-born past her stage would have to enter it. He would have to put it in his own hand on his own book and send it to a county that would read it in order. He would be teaching the member the eight hundred jin stands against, whose stage column reads one, two, three and a gap, and whose fourth stage is on no paper in nine counties and cannot be put there.

And if the county read it, the county would know the name of a man who had put his hand to her.

And three men at the west end of a Hall with no roof had put their hands to her in the middle of summer, on a sheet, for a ceiling, and had bought it with a silence that she had agreed to, and one of the terms of that silence was that she was not going to go and make one of them carry anything else.

“It would be cheaper to teach me,” said Ning Yan.

She said it out loud on the mat, to nobody, in the ordinary voice.

“It would be cheaper by a very long way. There are three men on a mountain who have already put their marks on a piece of paper about me and every one of them would know exactly how long it took me to hold a thing in one arm, and one of them has read the whole of a schedule and one of them has read out a sheet in a corridor twice, and I would learn stage six in a month instead of a season.”

She put the chalk down.

“And there is not one of the three who would put his name to it twice.”

## What She Decided

She lay on the mat with her arm out to the side and worked the last of it out with the lamp turned away, and the last of it was not about the three men.

It was about the fact that a master teaches and what a master teaches with is an order. You cannot hand a sequence across. It has to be built in the body that is going to use it, on the work the body is going to do it on, over months, by somebody standing there telling you when the order is wrong.

And there was nobody going to stand there.

So she lay there and decided to build it herself, out of load, out of the work, out of the ordinary repeated thing she was already doing eight hours a day for twelve coppers a load and a stamp on a sheet — and to do it on the work, not on anything else, because the work was the only thing she had that repeated enough to put an order into.

She did not call it a decision out loud. She lay on a mat in a loft four miles down a mountain and made a plan for the next six weeks with a stub of chalk, and the plan had one column in it, and the column was *loads*, and she wrote the number she had to hit beside it in her own left hand and did not tell anybody, including the man in her head, who did not ask.

That was the whole of what the relicensing had bought her in eight months, and she lay there and let it be a small thing.

It was not small. She had spent four months of tithe in an enemy's ledger to get a number she could not use, and now she had a licence and a ceiling and a column and a word on the front of a schedule, and the next six weeks of her life had to be built out of the only thing in nine counties that she was allowed to be paid for.

“I want to say something,” said Yuan.

“Say it.”

“If you build it on the work, then the work is what holds it up,” said Yuan. “Not the licence. The work.”

“I know.”

“I want to be the one who said it first.”

“You are not going to be anything,” said Ning Yan. “Go quiet. I have got six weeks to do.”

She put the chalk on the ledge and lay still with her arm out to the side.

Nine counties had been told she was licensed at nine. She was going to find out what that was worth by doing six weeks of somebody else's work with a bad hand, and the number she was going to hit was written in chalk on a ledge above a mat in a loft, and there was not one other person in nine counties who knew it was there.
