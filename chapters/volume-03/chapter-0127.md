# Under a Misspelling

The lodging house above the tanner's let four rooms and had let four rooms for nine years, and it kept a sheet on the door because the tallow render and the lamp oil went out per lot, and a lot had to be entered against a name, and the woman who took the sheet at that door could not read and had never been able to read and wrote every name on it down from what the person standing in front of her had just said.

Ning Yan had walked past that door for twenty-five days. It was on the lane between the tanner's and the well and there was nothing to stop on it.

She stopped on it on the twenty-fifth.

The sheet was a strip of sacking hemmed at the top and hung outside in the shade, and it had a line for each room and a column for what had been drawn against it, and there were four rooms and four lines and three of the names on it were ordinary names that had been on that sheet for years.

The fourth line said *Chou Zhyan*.

She read it four times, standing in the lane at the ninth hour with her hand flat against the wall, and worked out in about eleven seconds what she was looking at, because she had watched a clerk make this exact mistake every day for eleven months.

The doubled consonant. You get that when a person hears a name once, out of a mouth, in a room, and writes it down from what is in the head rather than from a form, because there is nothing in the room to copy a name out of. A name that a person wrote himself, in one go, from memory, in a lane, to a woman who cannot read, gets the doubled letter in it, because the tongue put it there first and the hand copied it honestly.

She had watched the Hall's copyist do the opposite of that for a year. He had a form in front of him and no memory behind it, and he therefore wrote her name in an order that had come from a piece of paper sent up the road by an office that had gone and found out what to write, and he had been asked for a corrected sheet once and had never been given one.

Two clerks. Two mistakes. One of them had gone on the whole of her county sheet; the other had just gone onto a strip of sacking on a door in Lowwater, and he had put it there himself, and a woman who cannot read had taken it and charged a lot of oil against it.

## What She Did Not Do

She did not go up the stairs.

The stairs were outside on the left of the tanner's door, four of them, unroofed, and a man who lodged there would be four paces from a lane forty people used a day, and there was nothing on earth stopping her from walking up them and standing in his doorway and saying *I know*.

She stood in the lane with her hand on the wall for the length of a count and a half and worked out what it would be.

He was not a man who could be frightened off a stair. He had said two true things in a county room in front of a queue and had then gone home, and if she had gone up to his door he would have stood there and been pleasant about it and asked her how she had known, and then she would have had to say.

And she would have had to say *because a sheet on a door is a county's business if a county ever asks*, and the whole of why he was in Lowwater was that he did not have to be in Lowwater, and she would have given a patient man a reason to be in Lowwater.

She took her hand off the wall and went to the lane behind the well and picked up a mat.

## The Second Cough

She went up the tower stair at the ninth hour that evening and he was on the fourth one.

It was the same cough. It came up from the tower door and it stopped on the fourth stair the way a man stops to look at something, and she had heard it do that four times since the middle of the spring and had gone past it four times without turning round.

This time she stopped where she was.

He did not come in. He was standing on the fourth stair with his hands behind him, looking up the last flight at the copying-floor door, which was shut, and the lamp was going behind it, and the whole of his attention was on a shut door.

Then she said a word.

He turned his head about a quarter of the way, the way a man does when he has decided that the whole of his attention is available, and he said good evening, and he came down the four stairs and out into the lane and stood at the bottom of them in the light of the tower, and it was a completely ordinary thing and there was not one second of it that was wrong.

“Good evening,” said Ning Yan.

“Good evening.”

“You are not from the Bureau,” said Ning Yan.

“No.”

“That is true,” she said. “I have never once thought it was not.”

He did not say anything to that. He said, “There is a light on your stair that is not the keeper's,” which was true, because the keeper's was at the copying floor and this one was in the cage on the fourth landing, and then he said good evening again and went down the lane toward the square with his hands still behind him, at the pace of a man with nowhere in particular to be.

She went up to the copying floor.

## Feng

Feng had a sheet in front of him and the lamp turned away from it and his back to the stair.

“I am not asking you,” said Ning Yan.

“You have not asked me,” said Feng, “and you are standing in the doorway telling me that you have not asked me, which is a thing a person does about four times a year in this building.”

“He has been up your stair.”

“He has been up my stair.”

“Four times.”

“Five times,” said Feng. “The fifth was this evening and he stood where he has stood every time and looked at my door and did not knock and did not ask and did not give his name, and I have been standing here for about a minute and a half.”

She stood in the doorway of the copying floor of the Lantern Register tower in Lowwater and looked at a man of sixty-odd who had been a keeper for twenty-two years and who had a consumptive cough and an unindexed column with five in it and four county copies walled behind the fifth item.

“He did not ask you for the stair’s paper,” said Ning Yan.

“He did not ask me for anything,” said Feng. “He has not asked me for anything since the middle of the spring and he has stood on my stair five times, and I want you to hear the shape of that, because you are about to work it out and I have worked it out four days ago and I would rather you had it in one piece than in eleven.”

“All right.”

“A man who wants paper from a keeper asks for it,” said Feng. “He asks for it in writing or he asks for it with a stamp or he asks for it standing on the fourth stair the way a man asks when he has been sent and cannot make you produce it. This one has never asked. He has come to a stairwell five times and looked at a shut door.”

“He is waiting to be asked into the building by a person who has no reason to want him in it.”

“That is my reading and I would keep it to yourself.”

## The Room Is Not the Point

“He has got a room over the tanner's,” said Ning Yan.

Feng put his brush down.

“Under a name that is not his,” said Ning Yan, “written by a woman who cannot read, off his own mouth, in a lane, with the doubled letter in it because that is the mistake you make when you have got no form to work from. He could have given any name in nine counties. He gave one that a clerk would have made, because a clerk's mistake is not a mistake a person looks at twice.”

Feng looked at the wall for a moment.

“Your Hall's copyist writes your name in an order that came off a road,” he said.

“Yes.”

“I have watched you watch him do it for a year and I have never once understood why you let it stand.”

“Because he has no other version to work from,” said Ning Yan, “and there is nothing to be gained by a man being told he has the right one if he cannot get it.”

She stood in the doorway with her arm out to the side.

“That is what he is doing over the tanner's. He has put a clerk's mistake on a strip of sacking where nobody can check it, and in about nine weeks a man from a room upstairs will come to that door and read the sheet aloud and nobody will know anything about it and nothing will be asked.”

“That is not a room,” said Feng. “That is a lodging.”

“He is not in Lowwater to sleep,” said Ning Yan. “He is in Lowwater to have an address that is not an address in a town where he has been standing at a desk twice a week since the snow without ever having slept in it. The room is the cheapest thing he can buy in this county and it costs him a name nobody can read.”

## The County Office

She went to the county office at the ninth hour on the twenty-sixth with the filing copy in her coat and stood at the side counter with the clerk and the brass rule, and asked a question that had nothing in it at all.

“Is anything coming up from outside the bound for my schedule.”

“No,” said the clerk. “Nothing has come up for your schedule since the slip in the spring and that was for the county sheet of your advancement and it is a different sheet and it is spent.”

“That is what I thought.”

She was about to go when she turned back, because she had come four miles down a mountain and up a tower stair twice in three days to find out one thing and it was free and it was on the wall.

“Sun He has written up about me.”

The clerk's hand stopped on the rule for about a second and then went on.

“Yes.”

“He wrote it up after the third hour of the twenty-third.”

“He wrote it up on the twenty-fourth,” said the clerk. “In the morning, at the opening, before the queue. Nineteen years and he has not done it before for a licensee, and he has done it for two estates in his time, and both times it was about a member and not about a schedule.”

“Then he has told the maintaining members of Qinghe.”

“He has been required to tell them,” said the clerk. “A schedule that a member is on, and a registrar has a matter in hand about that member, and the maintaining members of the schedule are the three men who keep it, and the registrar's office writes to them.”

“It goes up with the quarter's ordinary bundle. It is not a letter. It is a line on a return in a bundle and it will be read by a man in a corridor and it will be read in a week and not sooner, because that office does not hurry.”

She stood there for a moment.

“And what is the matter in hand.”

“A licensee capped at the fifth stage of Condensing, on a schedule of Qinghe, with a certified copy of that schedule in the hands of a stranger, and an observation made in this office at the counter,” said the clerk. “That is what the line says.”

“It says what a registrar is required to write down when a stranger buys a certified copy of a schedule a member works under and says two things about her terms in the room. It does not say you are in any trouble. There is no column for that and he did not invent one.”

“Thank you,” said Ning Yan.

“It was four words,” said the clerk. “You have been in here three weeks.”

## What It Was

The terror of it was that there was nothing in it.

She went up the tower stair in the late light with the filing copy in her coat and the short breath coming on at the third landing and had to stop for a count, and stood at the top of them thinking about the shape of the last eleven days.

No ropes. No ditch. No counting room, no man with a tube, no two men on a wall with a date and a shelf mark. There was a strip of sacking on a door over a tanner's, and a certified copy in a folder somewhere with a receipt folded square against a counter, and a line on a return in a bundle going up to three men who will read it in a week.

That was all of it.

And every single piece of it was lawful, and every single piece of it was answered in writing by an office that copied everything upward to something it never named, and no one anywhere had done a wrong thing and no one anywhere could be complained to, and the whole of it was a woman in a lane being described by an office in words, in the correct order, to an office that is allowed to describe her.

“Yuan.”

“I am here.”

“I am frightened,” said Ning Yan.

She said it out loud on the stair, at the ninth hour, to nobody, in a tower four miles down a mountain from the last place she had said it, and the lamp in its cage was burning on the fourth landing because it was always burning.

“I am frightened and there is nothing to run from and no one to be angry at, and a man in a good coat is not coming down this road with a date on him, and a man over the tanner's is going to be at that desk in the cold when the market opens.”

“You are frightened of the calendar.”

“I am frightened of the calendar and I have never been frightened of a calendar in my life,” said Ning Yan. “I have been frightened of men and of roofs and of a man of sixty-one being kind at me. I am frightened of a piece of paper with my name on it going up to a man in a corridor, and there is nothing to do about it, and in three weeks there will be three marks on a sheet because of it.”

She got to the loft and put the coat down and lay on the mat with her arm out to the side.

She did not go to the room over the tanner's. She did not ask Feng a single question about the five times a man had stood on his fourth stair, and she would not ask him about the sixth or the seventh, and there was going to be a seventh, and the whole of what she had learned in Lowwater in four weeks was that the most dangerous thing in this county was a person who was not doing anything to you and was not going to stop.

Four miles up the mountain there were three men in a corridor with a dry awning at the west end of a building that had no roof, and a bundle was going up to them with a line on it in a registrar's hand, and they would read it in a week.

She was going to be in the room when they decided.
