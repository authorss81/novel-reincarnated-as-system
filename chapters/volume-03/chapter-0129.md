# The Order of the Crate

Day 0 had been the last day of the thaw, which was the day the season's books closed, and four weeks off it was the twenty-eighth, and the crate went up on the twenty-eighth and not on the twenty-seventh, because a quarter is a quarter and a crate that goes up on the day before is a crate that goes up in the wrong week.

She had known that since the second of December and had been counting it in a calendar on a loft wall since the ninth day of the winter, and she had not said the date out loud to anybody since the day she got the licence, because a woman in Lowwater who says a date out loud is a woman who is counting something.

The twenty-seventh was the corridor. The twenty-eighth was the crate.

## The Annex

The Hall copyist wrote her hire hours onto the furnace schedule at the second hour of the afternoon of the twenty-seventh, in a column of its own at the side of the sheet, and she stood at the table while he did it and did not say a word, because a copyist writes what he is given and the only thing she could have done on the twenty-seventh was improve his handwriting.

The column was four weeks long. It was not continuous yet — it could not be, there was no such thing as an hour that had not been worked — and it was short, and the asked figure was on the top of it in the Hall clerk's hand, and the clerk looked at the two of them for the length of a count.

“How much are you asking.”

“I am asking for what is on the sheet.”

“That is not a number, that is a habit,” said the Hall clerk. “The asked figure is the number the sheet carries at the close of the season and the worked figure is the number on it now, and they are level or the quota office will not certify them, and the quota office is not mine. I will write the number you give me and the man with the book will find out in about a fortnight whether you are a liar.”

She gave him the number.

The clerk wrote it. The clerk certified the column with the Hall's mark and the hour. And Lu Kang came along the west table and read the whole of it, standing, at the speed of a man who has read a document like this eleven thousand times, and his eyes went down her line to the bottom of it and back up.

She watched him get to the place she knew he would get to.

At the top of the column, in the certification, the copyist had written her name in the wrong order.

It had been the wrong order since the twelfth day of the winter, in a copyist's hand, off a version of it that came down a road from an office that had gone and found out what to write. She had let it stand at a table a year. She had watched a man be told to bring a corrected sheet and had never been given one.

Lu Kang read the certification line. He read the name in it. He went on down the sheet to the closing line and then read the name again.

He did not say a word about it.

He took the Hall's stamp off the wall behind him and set it on the sheet himself and pressed it and lifted it and set the stamp back, which was what he had done in the spring and which was the only thing on that schedule that had ever worked.

## The Stair

She came down the four miles on the first cart day of the twenty-eighth with the certified schedule wrapped in oilcloth under her coat against the skin of her ribs, above the plate, in a place that had been getting softer for six months.

Lowwater in the light before the hour was a town that smelled of wet stone and tallow and the tanner's, and she went up the lane behind the well first, because the man at the fourth table had a board and she owed him a morning, and she put six loads to the water and took twelve coppers and was at the back of the wash house at the ninth hour.

The girl had a sheet squared against the edge of the bench.

“That is it,” she said, before Ning Yan had got the coat off. “The county’s request, on my hour, on the return sheet, second week of the quarter. It goes up the tower stair at the fourth hour and it goes up with everything else and it is not a separate parcel, because a separate parcel is a thing a man can ask about.”

“Have you looked at it.”

“I have counted it and I have squared it,” said the girl, “and I have not read it, and you are not going to ask me to.”

She put it down the end of the bench, squared, in a lane where water ran the whole length of three troughs.

“And I am not going to be thanked,” said the girl.

“No,” said Ning Yan.

“Good,” said the girl. “Now go up the stair, because the fourth hour is the fourth hour.”

## The Tower

Feng had the crate out on the copying floor and it was a crate of paper the size of a small chest, and there were eleven bundles on it, and the lamp was going, and he was stacking.

He was stacking it in an order.

That was the thing she had come down the road to see and she saw it before she had got to the bottom of the stair. He was putting the ordinary quarterly bundle in first, the way he had done every quarter for twenty-two years, and the ordinary quarterly bundle was the largest thing on it, and a clerk reads what comes in the crate in the order the crate is stacked.

Which meant that her schedule would arrive at the bottom of the quarter’s bundle, under a clerk’s receipt from a district office in the east and an extract of two household deaths, and a clerk does not dig.

“Feng.”

He did not turn round.

“The certified schedule is not in that crate,” said Ning Yan.

“It is going in the crate.”

“It is going in the crate at the bottom.”

“It is going in the crate where the crate puts it,” said Feng, “because the crate has been stacked the same way every quarter for twenty-two years and I am sixty-one years old and I have four clerks and a cough, and the whole of what I know about a quarter is that a quarter is the same shape as the last one.”

She stood on the stair with the oilcloth under her coat.

She did not ask him to change it. That was the thing she noticed about herself in the next moment, standing on the third stair of a tower with her arm out to the side.

She did not ask, and it was not because she did not want to, and it was not courtesy. It was that she had worked out, somewhere between the fourth mile and the bottom of the stair, that a keeper who has stacked a crate the same way for twenty-two years is the one person in this county whose hand nothing is going to shake, and that the only move available to her was to be a woman who tells a keeper how to do his job.

So she said the other thing.

“Feng, I want the schedule on the top and I want a line on the top sheet that says what is in the crate and in what order, in my hand, because a clerk at the far end of it cannot ask a question.”

He turned round.

“That is not a thing anybody does.”

“It is a thing anybody can do,” said Ning Yan, “and it is the whole of what is standing between a county and a number, and I am not asking you to stack the crate differently. Stack it the way you have stacked it for twenty-two years.”

“Put my schedule on the top of it and let me write the list, and the crate goes down the stair in the same order it has always gone down the stair, and if anybody upstairs ever asks why the furnace schedule is on top of a county bundle you have got a clerk's answer for it, which is that the applicant asked and he is allowed.”

Feng looked at her for a moment.

Then he said, “Move the eleven,” and went back to the bundles.

## What She Wrote

She wrote it at the bottom of the crate on the back of a sheet he gave her, and then copied it onto a clean one and put the clean one on the top of the stack, in her left hand, in a slant no clerk in nine counties can read quickly.

*Contents of this crate, in the order stacked, this day of the quarter, four weeks off the last day of the thaw.*

*First. The furnace schedule of Qinghe for the season closed, certified at the Hall, stamped by a maintaining member. Nine households. The applicant’s column is in it entire and unbroken and begins at the first entry of the season’s tithe book, which is the day the book opened, and there is no date in her line that has been left out and no hour worked that is not on it.*

*Second. The certified annex to that schedule, additional hours worked by the applicant outside the crew, entered in a column of its own at the side, with the asked figure at the top and the worked figure beneath it.*

*Third. The quarterly bundle of the Lantern Register, Lowwater.*

*Fourth. A return sheet entered at the wash house on the applicant’s own hour, recording a written request from this county office for the season’s furnace schedule and its hours.*

She put the dock on top and got the oilcloth off her coat and put the schedule in, on top of the eleven, and then came back and put her own list on the very top of it.

The certified schedule was the whole relicensing. That was the thing she had not understood until she was standing on a copying floor in a tower with four clerks and a crate: everything else in this arrangement was three men in a corridor and a man with a quota book and a registrar who did not hurry, and every single one of those was a person who could say no.

And the only piece of it that could not say no was a piece of paper with a continuous record of four months and a season and a four-week annex on it, going down a road inside a crate, to a county office that had never in its life held one.

“The line in your hand,” said Feng, from the bench. “They will not be able to read it.”

“They can send it to a man who can.”

“They will not send it to anybody,” said Feng. “A county does not chase a schedule. That is why a county is safe.”

“Then it does not matter,” said Ning Yan.

## The Stair, Going Down

He did not offer to carry anything.

That was in the log afterwards and she wrote it down because it was the single strangest thing that happened to her that day and she wanted to be sure later that it had happened.

She got the crate up onto her left shoulder in the room and she got it down four flights of a tower stair with it on her shoulder, and she stopped twice, and at the second stop the arm came open and she changed it across and went on.

Feng stood at the top of the stair with his hand on the rail and coughed, twice, in the way of a man who has something in his chest and is not going to spend it, and he did not come down.

“You do not have to,” said Ning Yan.

“I know I do not have to,” said Feng. “I have watched you carry a crate down that stair in the spring and I have watched you carry a schedule up it and I have watched you carry both of them in the same coat and I am not going to be the man who took the weight off you once and then had it in a room.”

That was a thing he had thought about before she came to the bottom of the stair. She understood that afterwards and not at the time.

The lane, the tanner's, the square, and four miles of road with a paper crate on one shoulder and an oilcloth under it, and the road open, because the pass is a winter thing and the cart days were counted and there were a good many of them left and the weather had not turned yet.

She put it down at the county door and went in and told the boy at the counter it was the quarter's crate from the Lantern Register and he said the carrier would come for it at the fifth hour, and she said it would not, because she was taking it up the county's own stair to the copying floor.

He looked at the crate and then at the counter and then said, in the way of a boy of seventeen doing his first year, that nobody had ever brought one up the county's stair before.

“Nobody has ever needed one before,” said Ning Yan.

She carried it up.

## What Was In It

On the fourth floor of the county office there is a room with a table in it and a clerk who takes what comes in a crate and puts it on the table in the order it comes.

He put the eleven down first, then the girl's sheet, then the annex, then the schedule with her list on it, and he read her list, and he read it twice.

“This is yours,” he said.

“Yes.”

“There is no order on this form.”

“There is no order on this form,” said Ning Yan, “and that is why I wrote one, and I wrote it in the room the Hall copyist could not read and I wrote it again in the room you can read, and they are the same four lines.”

He read the third line and the fourth and then went back to the first.

“Continuous,” he said. “From the day the book opened.”

“Continuous from the day the book opened.”

He went and got the previous quarter's return and put it beside her schedule and ran his finger down her column from the first entry to the last, the whole length of the season, one finger, four months, and it took him the better part of a minute and a half and he did not find anything, because there was nothing in it to find.

That was a true record. Not an excellent one. Not a certified one, not a stamped one, not a thing any office in nine counties would praise. A true one — nine hundred and forty hours of tithe and three hundred and eleven of a season's schedule and four weeks of an annex, every hour in a body of a woman who had done all of it, in her own hand at every entry, without one gap in it anywhere in the whole length of it.

He squared her schedule on the table with the rest of the quarter's return.

“This will be on the registrar's desk in the fourth week,” he said.

“And the registrar reads it in the first week after it.”

“He reads it when he reads it,” said the boy. “He does not read it because it has been read to be read.”

She thanked him and went down four flights of stairs and out into a Lowwater evening, and she had carried a paper crate up a hill and down a hill and up a county's own stair in one day with one arm and a quarter's work in her coat, and she had got it in on the twenty-eighth and not on the twenty-seventh.

The whole relicensing stood on a sheet with a continuous record on it, and the sheet had been read by a boy of seventeen who ran a finger down it and found nothing, and in a week it would be on a table in a room with nineteen years of county book in the lines of a man's face, and there was nothing in it that anybody could put a finger on.

That was not a triumph. It was the first time in six months that being legal was a thing she could physically do.

Up the mountain there were three men who had read a line in a bundle and had decided to seal, and she had told them the price of it in a corridor and they had said *then don't*, and the clearance that had to come before their marks was in a room with four tables and one window, and a man in it had never once looked at her.
