# What He Can Read

She walked out past the cart turn on the twenty-second at about the fifth hour and kept going, and that was the first time.

Past the bad flat, along the whole of the far end where she had laid two marks in lime in about four minutes on the twenty-first, and then half a mile further along a cart track that goes to a yard with stone in it, and then up onto low ground where the drift is six inches and does not ring.

The board is at the top of that rise, and the board is the last working point on nine days of road.

She had never been past it, and everything past it is somebody's ordinary country, and a woman who has been on nine days of working ground has no business past the post at the end of it.

## What Is On It

She had seen the column on this course twice and could not read a word inside it, and she stood in front of the far one for about two hours and did not read a word inside that one either.

It is the same plank and it is the same narrow column at the end of it and it is the same strip of oiled cloth pushed up behind it out of the weather. What is different is that she is standing on the right side of it.

She counted the lines, because she could count them and because counting them is all she has ever been able to do with it.

Nine years of them, and a strip about four inches wide every nine or ten days, and nine days of this month on it.

And in the middle of the month, between a line dated the thirtieth of the month before and a line dated about the ninth of this one, there is a strip with a date on it at the left and nothing at all on the rest of it.

Four inches wide.

It is the width of every other strip that has ever gone into that column, including the ones that are nine years old and nobody can read, and the width of a thing that was copied off something and there was nothing to copy.

She stood and looked at it for about as long as it takes a man to read a line.

Then she did the other thing she could do, which is the thing she has been able to do since a month that is four months back, and it came to the same place it has come to every time. She looked at the dated line above it and the dated line below it and she knew they were readings, because a reading is a number of yards worked at a place on a day by a man, and she has been reading that kind of number for four years in nine counties and cannot be shown one by accident.

The strip is hers and it is a hole the width of a stage.

## The Man Who Keeps It

He was about fifty-five and he had a coat on for the wind and a knife and a rule, and he came up the rise from the yard at about the sixth hour and stopped about nine feet off and looked at her and then at the board.

“There is no one to tell that you are here,” he said. “Say it if you came to ask it.”

“I did not come to ask it.”

“Then stand there and do not tell me what you are reading, because you cannot read it.”

That is the whole of the first conversation. He gave her a knife and a spare strip of cloth when she was holding her right hand in a way that made the tool look like a thing to be done one-handed, and she said thank you, and he said it was nothing, and then she stood at the board for about an hour and he worked on a post about forty feet off and neither of them spoke.

He did not ask her where she came from. He did not ask her what her name was. He did not ask her what she had been doing on the ground past the cart turn for fourteen days, which every man within nine days of road had watched her do.

## What He Says About The Ground

He came to the board at about the seventh hour to put his day up, and he did it in about four minutes and she watched him do all of it.

He wrote the date at the left and he wrote a figure at the right and he tore a strip off square against a rule and pushed it behind the plank, and none of it was a reading of ground and all of it was about ground.

She asked him one question, and it was the first question she had asked anybody past the last stake.

“You keep nine years of that and you do not read it,” she said. “Why.”

He put his thumb on the plank.

“Because it is not mine and it is not a book,” he said. “I get a strip off a cart and I put it up and I have done that since before I came and the one before me did it too, and if I stop it is a hole in nine years and a hole in nine years is not a thing a man does.”

“Then what is it.”

“That is the question I have asked for nine years,” he said, “and the answer I have got is this, and it is what everybody on this reach says, and it is enough to work by.”

He turned and looked out along the ground the way a man looks out along ground he has walked every working day of nine years.

“The line is not stone,” he said. “It was never in the same place on both sides of itself and it does not move in a night.”

“It is a line of work. It is kept by people going out on it with bars in their hands, and where they go and go on time the line holds, and where they do not it leaks. And what comes out of a stretch that has not been kept is drift.”

“The thick of it is on the worst-worked ground?”

“The thick of it is on the worst-worked ground and the thin of it is where nobody works,” he said. “My end of it is the thin end, because there is nothing this side of the rise for it to come to.”

He said it the way men say it here, which is as a fact about weather.

“Ninety parts of that is what the men on your side of it say,” he said. “I have been out nine years and I have never seen the other ten.”

He put his hand flat on the post beside him.

“I am not going to stand at my own board and tell a woman about a thing I cannot show her on a strip of cloth,” he said. “If there is another ten parts of it then somebody will find them out by doing it and not by asking me.”

## What She Did With It

She stood there and put it against everything she had measured for four months and it did not move.

She had kept the ringing against what nine counties have heard in order, over four years. The loud stretches are the worst-worked. The quietest ground on nine days is at the far end.

That is now a thing two men on two sides of it have said to her, in the ordinary voice, about their own work, and neither of them is a liar and one of them has never heard a stage go up in his life.

She said it to herself once, standing at the post, in the plainest words she had.

The residue of everything the bound is told runs along it and pools where the ground is soft, and this is a fact about ground that a man who has been paid by the day for nine years can tell you, and it is nine parts of something, and there is a tenth part that nobody has ever stood in front of.

And she stopped there and did not build anything on it, because the only control in the world is herself and she is the one thing being measured, and a woman standing at a post at the seventh hour on the far side of the working reach building a theory about the ground out loud is a thing that would be noticed.

She put the tenth part where she keeps the three empty dates.

## What He Can Read

It happened on the twenty-sixth at about the fifth hour, on the rise, with the bar across her knees, and she had not asked him anything about it and had not asked him for four years.

She had asked him about the office on the first month she came out here, and on the seventeenth of a month two months back, and twice before that, and he had told her four times that he could not read it, and had said that the fourth time that he was not going to say it a fifth time to be different about it.

He said it in one breath.

“It says the Standing Office of the Second Account.”

She did not move.

“That is a middle office,” he said. “There are offices above that one and I have never seen one of them and I am not going to tell you what I think is over the top of it. That is the whole of what is there and I am going to stop.”

He stopped.

She sat on a rise in a country where nothing is announced and did not ask him for one notch more, and she did not ask him what the Second Account is a second account of, and she did not ask him whether that office is the one that put a countersignature on a schedule read aloud out of a wage-book by a clerk in a room she was not in the middle of.

She wanted to ask him that one more than any of them and she did not, because he had stopped, and because a man who has said four times that he cannot read a thing and then reads it and then stops has told her everything he has, and asking him a sixth question would be asking a person for something they have not got.

## Six Weeks

He gave her the other thing on the twenty-eighth, at about the sixth hour, standing at the post with his coat on, in about four sentences and without being asked, in the ordinary voice, and she wrote it on the back of the ticket with the flat of a second finger and it took eleven minutes.

“You have about six weeks of me,” he said, “and no more than that, and there is nothing in this country that makes it longer except a base that is not finished yet.”

She had a column of marks on the back of a dead weight ticket that she had kept since the tenth of a month that is two months back and it was four marks and three spends and a day of standing and one job.

She wrote the six weeks underneath it in her own hand and put the ticket back in the oilcloth above the Clay Plate.

Twenty-one days to the ninth.

The ninth is on the twenty-ninth, and she is at the eighth, and there is a base under her that is one stage short of finished and a hole in a column on a post that a man who has kept it for nine years is going to keep for nine years more.

And she has a voice again, for about six weeks, and there is a word for what that is worth to her that she did not write down and has not said out loud and is not going to.
