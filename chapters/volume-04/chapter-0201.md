# The Office's Hand

The third leg was four days and two walks from her and she had to be in a room to ask for it, and the room was not the camp.

It was in the west end of the valley: a long low building with a slate roof called the schedule office at Stony Ford, with a stove in the middle of it and about eleven people in it, and one of them was a clerk called Ren Shu.

She had expected an office. It was not an office. It was a room where a schedule was made, and the making of a schedule is a thing men do to each other while somebody else is reading aloud from a book.

## Ren Shu

He was about fifty and had the hands of a man who has spent thirty years turning sheets and not touching anything heavy.

He read the request. He looked at her sheet with the three marks on it. He went out and came back with a wedge of paper about the thickness of a county almanac and put it on the table with a lamp on it.

“The wages come up off the west-going carrier on the seventh,” he said. “The book is with the contractor. I take what is in the book. I read it to the room and the room sets the schedule and the room sends the schedule up, and the schedule goes up with the sheet on it and the sheet is what it is for.”

“Who reads it?”

“I read it.” He turned round and called a name into the back of the room. “Do not go over there.”

She was not going to go over there. She had not been going to go over there.

“It is a matter of practice,” Ren Shu said, turning back. “The book is a contractor's book. The room is the schedule office's. A reader is the only person in this building who stands in both. You are not the reader and you are not in the book, so you do not stand in it while it is read. That is not about you. I do it the same way when it is a man from the furnace side.”

## The Reading

It took under an hour and she was in the room for all of it and was not in the middle of it.

He read from a wedge of paper. He read the dates and the stretches and the hours in the ordinary voice, and eleven people wrote, and there was a fire going and a man moving coal into it at some point and nobody adjusted for that.

Nobody adjusted for that is the sentence that tells you what the room is.

Eleven people write while a man reads. They cannot all have been listening to the reading, because eleven people cannot listen to a reading and write eleven columns at the same time. So they did not listen. They wrote. They caught what they caught.

That is the mechanism. That is how a number gets from a contractor's book into a schedule that an office past that county reads in order with dates on it, and it is not a secret and it is not an outrage and it is the way every working thing in the world is done: a woman in the middle of a table who catches three figures out of four hundred and writes them down, and nobody ever audits the three.

She had spent four years being the only person in the room who checked the figures.

Her name came up at about a third of the way through.

She heard it.

She heard the county, and she heard the stretch, and she heard a number of hours, and she heard a number of coppers, and she did not see the number. She was at the near end of eleven tables' worth of a long room and he was reading to the middle of it, and the wedge of paper was on a stand at the far end, and nobody turned it round.

She did not see whether the hours he read were the hours that were in the book.

There was about nine seconds in the middle of it where she understood that she could have asked.

She could have walked to the middle of that room and stood beside the stand and read the wedge. Nobody would have put a hand on her. It would have been discourteous and it would not have been against anything, because nobody in that room is under any instruction about a woman standing at a stand.

She would have had a number.

She did not do it, and the reason she did not do it is not courage and it is not standing. The reason is that a number she had read herself would be worth nothing to her at all, because a relicensing needs a schedule, and a schedule is not a number a woman saw once, and there is no version of this in which what she read in that room came back to her in the form of a stage.

Everything that happened from here happened because a man in this room said a number out loud.

So she stayed at the near end of the table and said nothing, and she was never afterwards sure whether that had been the cleverest thing she did that season or the most expensive.

That was the whole of it, and she went over it a great many times afterwards, and it came out the same every time.

She cannot check one of them. She was hired on the fourth of the month by a man who wrote her hours in at the start of the day. She worked. She was paid six coppers a day by that man for a fixed number of days. There is no point at which the number that got read out of the wedge of paper in that room was anything she ever saw.

He read her name and her hours and about a second and a half of everything in her life, and he read it correctly or he did not.

If he had been wrong by an hour, she would never have known, and neither would the foreman, and neither would the eleven men, and the man at the trestle would have gone on writing what he wrote in his own book and the two books would not have seen each other.

## The Hand

The countersignature went on in an office's hand and not a man's.

She knew it because the hand did not vary. It was the same two strokes on every sheet in the pile, and it was not neat, and it was not fast, and it did not rise at the end of anything, and she had signed a column in a hall with no roof in eleven minutes with the flat of a second finger, and she knew what a person's hand looked like when a person was there.

There is no person in that.

She was looking at a hand that was going to do this about four hundred times a year and it had been going to do it four hundred times a year for longer than she had been alive, and it was not anybody.

Above the seal on the sheet there was a line of writing, which is the office's name, and it was written at the top of the page in the same unhurried set of strokes, and she did not get to read it, and he did not read it out, and she did not ask.

## The Thing It Said

The relicensing was granted in principle, with the ceiling at Foundation the first stage, and it was dated from that valley and not from Lowwater, and the paper it was written on was one she was not going to be able to hold again.

He said the words to her the way he said everything, which was twice and flatly.

“Your licence had nothing on the front of it above nine,” he said. “It has something on the front of it now. The schedule behind it is mine to set and the counter goes up above mine and above that is not mine either, and it goes when the schedule is signed, and the schedule is signed at the end of the month on the days you put in the book.”

“How many days.”

“That is not a question I answer.” He did not raise his voice or change anything about his manner. “It is on a wedge and it goes up on the seventh, and after the seventh it is not a number anybody in this room can change, and there is nothing in that number for you to check. That is the arrangement. I am not going to tell you it is a good one and I am not going to tell you it is a bad one.”

She did not ask a second one.

A person asking a question in that room is a person being told what she may have. She had been told what she may have about nine hundred times in four years and she was not going to spend one of the ten days she had left on being told it again.

She took her sheet with the three marks on it and it had a seal on it and a line of writing above the seal, and she walked out of a stove-warmed room into a cold valley with nine days of her own back behind her and nothing whatever in front of her except ten days of a wage-book somebody else kept.

## Awe

There was a part of that which she had not expected and which she turned over for most of a mile.

Three volumes of her life had been about having her own hand on every document. Eleven minutes at a counter with the flat of a second finger in front of a queue that did not shuffle. A column of nine lines and three holes in it, signed herself. A defect re-entered in her own hand under a sentence she wrote at the fourth hour on a mat.

Everything she had ever got, she had got by putting her own mark on it in front of somebody.

And the thing that lifted the ceiling was a document she never touched, in a hand that is not a person's, on a number a stranger read out of a book she was not shown.

She wanted the ceiling. She had wanted it since a hall with no roof, and she had paid for the wanting in a bargain a fourteen-year-old girl made in a lane in the spring of the third year, and she had this morning got it.

It is not hers.

She walked a mile in a cold valley being awed, and being horrified, and being those two things at the same time about the same object, and both of them were correct and neither of them was about him.

## What The Bar Had Been

She walked about a mile on the flat and then sat down on a stone, which she had not planned.

A registrar in a county nine days west had written four words in the ordinary hand on a bar, and had written a fifth line slower than the rest, and it had taken her a fortnight of cold to get the shape of what she had.

*The answer to this application is not this office's to write.*

He had been right about that in a way she had not been able to think her way out of for a month.

This county holds no schedule of work done past its boundary and cannot certify one.

That was never an obstacle. She had gone round it nine days in about four minutes an hour.

It was a description of where she was standing.

She stood in a valley with a stove in the middle of it and ten days owed from the seventh and a hand that was going to sign a number she was not going to be shown, and the registrar had written the whole of her situation on a bar in the ordinary hand without ever meeting her and without being wrong about one word of it.
