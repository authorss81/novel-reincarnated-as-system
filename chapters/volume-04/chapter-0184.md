# One at a Time

The ticket was off the end of a stack of dead weight cards that a man had put under the frame of the Hall's crate to keep the crate's foot off the floor, and there were about sixty of them in it, and every one of them had an hour and a load on the front and a quarter written by a man with a chaw in his mouth in the spring.

The cut had run out on the thirty-first day past the last day of the thaw and the cards had stopped coming.

She took four of them down the hill in her coat and put them on the mat in the second bunk under the oilcloth and lay down on them in the cold, and at about the ninth hour on the sixth day of this month she turned one of them over.

The back of a weight ticket is the back of a weight ticket.

There is an hour at the left and a load at the right and a stroke under the hour that a man puts in when he has counted the hour himself, and there is a card number at the top that goes up to a tally board in a hut three miles east that does not exist any more.

And under all of that there is nothing at all.

## The Standing

Her own terms from the spring were still standing. Four of hers and three of his, entered on a sheet at about the fourth hour on a loft mat, referred to since and never written out again by anybody including her.

The first of hers capped what the channel was spent on at hours.

That was the whole of the problem, and it had been the whole of the problem for about a year and she had not known it was a problem until she had something to ration.

She had been keeping a rate. Twice a day, in hours, never idled, for a year and a half, which had been her own invention and had nothing behind it, and she had kept it the way a person keeps a habit that has turned out to be load-bearing and then discovers that the habit was the only thing holding the shelf up.

The first of her four terms said the channel was for hours.

She was about to break her own standing term, on purpose, in a cold room, with nine men asleep, and to write down that she had broken it, in her own hand, on the back of a piece of dead work.

## The Division

She did it with a pencil stub she had ground down herself in the autumn, because a ticket takes a pencil better than a brush and a brush is not a thing you keep in a coat.

She wrote the days down the left in a column. She wrote nothing across the top of it.

Then she sat on the mat in the cold and did the sum, which was the first time in a year and a half that she had tried to do this sum at all.

She was going to put most of him on a word in two days, which she had decided on a mat and had not said to anybody, and that left her a small remainder and a number of sentences a day out of it.

She wrote five marks across the row for a day.

Then she looked at it, and did not put a stroke through the row, and sat there on the mat in the cold with a pencil stub in her hand and waited to be told she had it wrong.

“I would not do that,” said Yuan.

She waited.

“You are not buying a day,” he said. “A day of me is whatever a day of me is at twice a day and you have never once measured it, because you never had to. Five is not a fifth of anything. Four is what you can justify, and four is more than you have been spending, which is the whole of why the number is awkward, and I am not going to argue it with you.”

He said it in one sentence.

He did not say it again, then or afterward, and she did not ask him to, and the column on the back of a dead ticket had four marks in a row on it and stayed that way for the rest of the winter.

## The Rule

She wrote the rule out under the column in the ordinary hand she used for anything that had to be true in the morning.

Four to a day.

One mark for each sentence and a stroke through the day when it is spent, and the day is spent when the four marks are on it.

Not to be spent on what I want to know at the moment I want to know it. Not to be spent because it is the answer to something. If a thing can be got out of a run list or a board behind a well or a man at a trough, it is got that way and not this way.

And then, because she was eighteen and had written a rule for a man who could not read it and could not be bound by it, she added the last line in the same hand and the same size, which was the only part of it that was not practical.

And it goes on the page and not out loud, because the day it goes out loud is the day it is over.

She put the ticket inside the oilcloth against her ribs, above the plate, with the licence, and lay down in the cold on three dead weight tickets with a rule on the back of one of them.

## The Loneliness Of It

Nobody in this county knew she was doing it.

That was not a complaint. It was the first thing she had ever built that no office in nine counties could enter, no clerk could copy upward, and no district could put in a crate with a strap over a man's shoulder, and she had spent nineteen months learning that a thing with a hand on it goes somewhere and a thing without a hand on it does not.

This one had her handwriting on it and it would never be read by anybody but her.

She was in the middle of a world where the shape of her was written down in four counties and read out in nine, and there was a certificate and a referral and a member's sheet and a licence nailed over her own bunk, and a date in a counting room a mile away that had got in there by a hand that was not that room's.

And there was a scrap of card against her ribs with four marks on it and a stroke through a day and a rule in her own handwriting.

He could not read it. That was the point and it was also the cost, and the cost was that she had just invented the only discipline in the world that nobody could check her on, including the two of them.

## The Yard Behind The Hall

She took it out to the yard in the morning to find out whether she could do this in daylight.

The yard behind the Hall has a bench along the wall that has been there since before she came and a scrap of soft pine on the end of it left there by a man who repairs the Hall's shutters and does not clear them off, and about the seventh hour there is nobody in it, and the sun comes off the wall onto the stones for about two hours in the third month of the cold.

She got four marks onto the card in about nine minutes and she put it away before she had done the fifth row, because a man came round the corner of the Hall with a bar on his shoulder and she could not have been in the yard holding a piece of quarry stationery with a column of crosses on it and a rule in her own hand at the seventh hour on any account at all.

That was the discovery, and it was a stupid one, and she had known it would come.

There is no version of this in which anybody ever sees the card, and the reason is not that the card is secret. The reason is that a woman who has been in the paper of nine counties for a year cannot be seen in a yard in daylight holding a book in her own hand, and there is no way to be the two things.

She had kept a book for a month at the counter in front of a queue and had made a clerk wait for eleven minutes and had been perfectly comfortable about it, because that book was required of her.

This one was not required of her by anybody. This one was a discipline she had invented at the ninth hour to keep a man alive, and the first thing she had learned about it was that there is nowhere in this county she is allowed to keep a book.

She would do it on the mat, after the ninth hour, with the oilcloth over it and nine men asleep and the fire a bed of ash.

That was going to cost her something every single day for as long as it lasted, and the something was four marks and a stroke and about nine minutes in a cold room on her own, at an hour when she ought to be asleep, which is a thing she had never once in nineteen months made herself do.

## The One Sentence That Was Funny

“You are going to be very bad at this,” said Yuan.

“I know.”

“No, I mean that as a criticism and not as a comfort,” he said. “You are going to be extremely bad at it, because you are going to try to save all of it, and I am going to want to spend every one of them, and we are going to spend a month in a room the two of us arguing about a number on a piece of card.”

“That is the arrangement.”

“That is the arrangement,” he said, “and I am worse at it than you are, and the reason is that I want to spend them and you want to save them, and neither of us is going to be the one who gives in first, and you know which of us will.”

He said it in one sentence and it was the only thing he said all night that was funny.

It was not funny.

She lay in the cold with the rule against her ribs and four marks in a row on the back of a dead ticket and a voice in her head she had never once been able to count without spending, and she worked out that she had just made a rule about how to spend him, out loud, on a mat, at the ninth hour, with nine men asleep.

Nobody in the crew would ever have found out. That was the arrangement and it was the reason she had chosen a thing with a man's handwriting on the front and her own on the back, and she lay there and worked out that she had built the only room in the world that nobody could come into, and that she was going to have to live in it, and that the two of them were going to be the only people in it for as long as it lasted.

She did not tell him to stop.
