# Bread At The Store

There is one store between the last stake and the cart turn and it is a room with a counter in it and shelves and a woman behind the counter who has been there every day of her life.

It is the only place past the last stake where anybody sells anything, and she has been buying bread in it for two months, at the seventh hour on the way back from the trestle, five coppers, and the woman has never once asked her anything at all.

## What Five Is For

Ten days is fifty coppers of work and she had about ten coppers in her hand on the twenty-first of that month.

That is the whole arithmetic and it is the only one that has to be done, and she did it on the first of the ten days and then she stopped doing it, because a woman who stands at a bread counter working out whether she can eat is a woman who has started counting and counting does not stop.

Five coppers in the morning and five coppers at the bread counter in the evening, and the number in her hand was the same both times.

There was a tin behind a board in a corner of a wash house nine days west with about a hundred and forty coppers in it, and it had been gone since the evening of a day in a month that is four months back, and there was not one thing in the world to go back to.

## Oil And Salt

The woman behind the counter had a name on a board and Ning Yan read it about nine times and never once used it.

“You want the oil,” the woman said, on the twenty-third, “or you want the salt, or you want the bread, and you have been standing there for a bit with the bread in your hand doing the thing where you add up what you have got.”

“I am.”

“The oil is four coppers and it will go further if you do not cook it. You cook it, it goes in a pot and the pot is the pot, and there is no pot out here that is not somebody's.”

“I do not have a pot.”

“No,” the woman said, and put the pot back under the shelf, and then, after a moment, brought out a flat pan and put that back too.

“Two months and a bit,” the woman said.

“You are the only person who comes out of that line without a pot. I have thought about it.”

“You have not asked me about it.”

“No,” the woman said, and went and looked at a shelf, and then came back and put the bread down on the counter on the other side of the salt, which is where a woman puts bread for a person she has decided something about.

“You are going to be out here in the second month of the cold,” she said. “Everybody out here is. It is not a thing to be asked about.”

“I know.”

“You have not eaten since the fourth hour and you are going to eat now, and you can do that here, because people eat in a store all day and I have stopped counting them.” She put her elbow on the shelf. “That is not an offer. That is a woman telling you to stop standing in the middle of my floor.”

She ate the bread on the step outside with her back to the wind, which is where she has eaten it for two months, and it was better than it had any right to be, and she gave the woman five coppers for it and the woman took five.

That was the whole of it. Forty seconds and a shelf, and a thing about a pot that was not asked about and was not a kindness, and she has not mentioned it since and neither has the woman.

At the seventh hour on the twenty-fourth, and the twenty-fifth, and the twenty-sixth, and the twenty-seventh, and the twenty-eighth, and the twenty-ninth, and the thirtieth, the bread went on the counter at five coppers and the woman's hand went straight to the tin.

## The Eleventh Hour

She worked eleven hours a day and it was eleven hours on the fourth and it was eleven hours on the thirtieth, and there was nothing in between to change it.

The worked stretch runs about three hundred yards and it rings the whole way. It is the thinnest drift of anything she stands on and it is somebody else's mark on the plank and it is a foot of it in the worst of the run at the inside end, and it goes over a boot in one place in nine.

Four men work it with her. Two of them are on the course's old ground by the look of their boots and neither of them has said where they came from and neither of them is asking.

The four of them have got a rhythm in it that she is not in, and after about four days she stopped being slowed down by it and started being slowed down by something else, which is that she can do four times what any of them can do at any moment and has to choose to do exactly what they are doing.

She chose it every day for ten days. It took her until the sixth to understand that choosing it was the entire content of those ten days and that she had been doing a full day's work in a full day's manner with a body eight stages past the strongest thing on nine days, on a stretch a man of thirty with a bad shoulder can keep up with.

## Two True Things At Once

The sixth stage came on the thirteenth and by the twenty-second she could tell what it had done to her and it was two things and both of them were true.

The first is that she is the strongest body on nine days of road and it is not close.

She put a bar into drift that has gone over a boot and got two feet of it up and turned it and put it down again on its side, four times, on a worked stretch, in the fifth hour, in front of four men, and the fourth one she did without shifting her feet, and the four men looked at each other about it in the way men look at each other about a thing they are going to have to carry with them for the rest of the season.

The second is that she is worse at the work than she was at the fourth of that month, and she is going to be worse at it for the rest of the season, and that is also not close.

The base underneath her is heavier. A heavier base is a colder base and a slower base, and she is at the fourth count of breath everywhere now including on the flat and including on a grade, and three is gone and is not coming back, and the bar wants more of her than it wanted a week ago and takes longer off her than it did.

And she is paid five coppers a day for the second of those.

The four men know the second of those without being told. The man with the bad shoulder gets a hand up faster than she does and she has never once been able to explain to him why, and he has never once asked, and it is the only thing about her that they can see and it is the thing that costs her the work.

## Cold That Is Not A Price

She is not hungry. She has not been hungry since the first of the Foundation stages, which is the better part of four months, and that has been strange every single day of it and has stopped being strange, which she noticed and did not enjoy noticing.

The cold is the other thing.

For four years every cold she ever stood in was something that had been done to her — a season's road, a shop, a licence she did not own, a room with a man in it who wanted something — and she could put a hand on where it came from, and the hand could be a person and the person could be asked.

This one has no source.

There is a fire at the trestle about eight feet from where she sits and four men are at it and she pays five coppers a day to be allowed to be within eight feet of it, and she is colder at the fire than she is in a hut with three boards in it, and that is a thing about a base under her and not about the fire and not about them and not about a decision.

It is weather. It is the stone of her running cold at four counts.

On the twenty-sixth she sat at that fire for about an hour and a half, as close as she was allowed to get, with bread in her hand that she had not finished, and could not get warm, and eventually took herself eight feet back out into the dark because the men had started talking about a job in a voice that had turned to somebody she had not been asked about.

## Past The Cart Turn

On the twenty-ninth, at about the sixth hour, she did a thing she had not done before.

She walked about a quarter of a mile past the trestle with the bar over her shoulder and stood on the rise and looked at the ground past the cart turn.

That is all. She did not put a bar in it and she did not go over the rise onto it and she stood there for about eleven minutes and then walked back and put the bar on the wall of the hut and went and slept.

The drift on the far side of that rise is thick on the low side, about a foot and a bit, because that is where the work was worst and the crews have said so for nine years and are correct.

And it is quiet. It has been quiet the whole time she has been on this ground, and it is the quietest ground on nine days, and she stood on the rise on the twenty-ninth and looked at it and understood in one plain sentence why it is quiet.

She did not say the sentence out loud and she did not put it anywhere else.

There is nobody out there.

That is the sentence. There is not one person on that three-quarters of a mile to be announced to, and the ground is quiet because there is nothing there to be told anything.

She had worked it out on the third of a month with a bar in her hands and a woman with a cart standing nine feet off, and she had not built anything on it then, and she did not build anything on it now, and the rise she was standing on was the same rise she had been standing on every evening for a fortnight, looking.

The not-going cost more than the work had.

That is the whole of the twenty-ninth and the thirtieth. Ten days, fifty coppers, bread bought, a pan put back under a shelf, a bar on a wall, and eleven minutes on a rise at the sixth hour looking at ground that nobody hires on and that she is not going to be clever at.
