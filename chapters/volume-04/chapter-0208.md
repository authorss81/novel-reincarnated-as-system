# The Counterfoil

She had never been paid anything and kept anything.

Every wage she had ever taken in four years went into a tin, and every tin went into a board in a corner, and every board got lighter, because that is what happened to money when a person was spending it on passage and fire and bread. She had been paid a quarter forward for stone once, for work she had done, and it had gone into fire.

On the twenty-seventh a man at a trestle gave her four strips of paper and told her to keep them.

## What A Counterfoil Is

“When the book pays, the worker keeps a stub,” the tender said, and put them on the plank, four of them, squared. “That is what that is. It is not a form. It has got the date on it and the hours and the stretch and the rate and the master's mark, and that is all it has got, and it is the only paper on this course that says you worked here on that day at that stretch for that rate.”

He did not say it was valuable.

She picked them up.

A county seal is a thing you get at a counter with a reason and a stamp and a clerk who is tired. A licence is a thing nine counties can produce a copy of. A certified copy is obtainable in a room in Lowwater with a stamp and a reason, and she had one in a Warden's book somewhere and a receipt for it, and both of those were real and both of them were a copy of a thing that somebody far away said had happened to her.

A strip of paper off a contractor's book, about nine inches by two, with a bar painted on the corner because that is where the fourth stretch from the trestle was.

She had never seen one before in her life. She had been inside a room with a hundred and fifty clerks' offices' worth of documents and not one of them was any of the things this was.

## Four Strips

She had carried five documents in her life with a county's hand on them and she had thought about every one of them.

None of them is worth anything on its own. A burial line. A surety transfer that reads *Stands to me*. A no-surety request stamped at her own asking and never asked for again. A countersignature and six conditions. A relicensing with a ceiling of nine written in her own hand.

Every one of those is a thing that could be produced by anybody in nine counties with a stamp and a reason. Every one of those is a copy of somebody else's word about her.

A counterfoil is not a copy of anybody's word. It is a strip of a contractor's own book with a stretch painted on the corner, and it exists because a man went into a wedge of paper and read a number out loud, and it is the only object in the world that can say that a woman did a day's work at a bar on the sixth of a month at six coppers.

She had never in four years been able to produce a single thing that said she was anywhere. There was not one paper in nine counties that had her body in it; proof had failed on the body in the second year and had never been gone back into.

Four strips, nine inches by two.

## The Price Column

On the stub, under the hours and the rate, there is the price column.

That is the fourth column and it is the same width as the stub and it has a space at the right-hand end of every line, and the space is where a word goes.

She stood at a trestle holding four stubs and understood it in about two seconds, having understood the other half of it on the twenty-seventh of the month before last.

**A reason goes in the price column. The price column and the reason column are the same column. The hours go in it.**

She had known it in theory for a long time. She had known it since a man holding a book opened at a page with forty entries in it, and she had stood there and worked out in four seconds that the two were one.

She knew it now for certain, and in her own hand, on four pieces of paper, in a trench in the ground where the ink had not properly dried.

## What She Holds

She lined them up on the plank at the fire that night, in the order they had been paid.

On the fourth of the month, nine coppers' worth of her, at the cross, six coppers.

On the fifth, six coppers, at the bar.

On the sixth, six coppers, at the ring.

On the seventh, six coppers, at the ring, and the ring was the day she stood at a trestle for four seconds with a word in her mouth and did not say it. There was a mark on that stub for a ring on a day, and a stroke of a pencil beside the space where a reason would go, and it was a stroke of a pencil and not a word, and she did not know who had put it there.

She left the space empty.

It went up the road with the man. She did not know what happened to the stub. She did not know whether the book and the stub were supposed to agree, or whether anybody at any point on either side of anything had ever checked.

She had four of them, and she kept them in the oilcloth above the plate, with a ticket that had three marks on the corner and a ticket with two lines and a ticket with three lines.

## What She Has

She worked it out on the twenty-ninth, sitting at the west end of the camp with her back to a cart wheel, in the last of the light, in about ten minutes, and she wrote nothing down because there was nowhere to write it that was not a dead weight ticket and there is a limit to how many things a person can put on a piece of card and still be able to read the card.

She was relicensed above Condensing nine.

There was a ceiling on the front of a document above nine now, and there was a seal on it, and the office that had set it was one she had never been inside, and the number under it was one she had not been in the room for.

She was in debt of an attendance.

It was on a member's sheet in a county nine days west, in a column of its own. It did not come off for hours and there were no hours. It would be in a file in Lowwater in a fortnight and it would be in a file in Lowwater in a year, and there was no word in it that said why, because the word went in the hours column, and that was the price.

She was working on a line of work kept by hand, by people paid by the day, who were right.

She had not got an answer to that and was not going to get one and was not going to argue about it. Eleven men, a foreman with a daughter on it, a tender with a board, a girl of nineteen. They were right about the drift. She had been inside the ring and seen the proof, and she had four weeks of a cold in her that started on the ninth of the month, and she had nothing at all to set against any of it.

And she was holding the only book in the world that could say she was there.

Not the one with her name on it. Not a copy of the one with her name on it, readable in order in nine counties with a stamp and a reason.

Four strips of paper, nine inches by two, with a bar painted on the corner.

## What Is Not Going West

She did not go west this week, and she was not thinking about going west.

She had been thinking about going west for eleven days, on and off, at the back of every day. There is no point in a person carrying that on past the point where it stops being a question and starts being a habit.

What she had instead was a set of strips.

She could not buy a seat at the fourth bridge with four pieces of paper about nine inches by two, and no carter on that road has ever in the world heard of a contractor's counterfoil and would not know what to do with one if she held it out and asked.

The west-going goes on the turn of a board in a hut in the back of that camp and there was one of them on the twelfth and there will be another in about three weeks.

## Three Hundred Yards

She asked the foreman one question in four days and it was not about the work.

“Have you walked it?”

Cui Bao put the book down and thought about it, which she had not expected him to do.

“Not this season,” he said. “I have not walked it end to end since the spring. Most men have not. You cannot walk it in a week and you cannot walk it and work it.”

“How long is it.”

He put his thumb on the map on the back of the board that everybody in that camp had seen a thousand times and that nobody read, and he ran it along, and there were numbers on it, and he read them out the way a man reads a thing off a board he had never once studied.

**Nine days.**

She looked at it for a while.

Nine days from a county she had left on the first of the month. Nine days she had walked, most of it. And she had been in about three hundred yards of it — the cross, the bar, and the ring — and she had seen about four hundred yards of the worst of it, and the rest of it was the rest of it.

She was eighteen years old on a line at the far end of nine days of road, and she had seen three hundred yards of it, and the number was written on the back of a board in a room, and she was the only person who would ever go and look at the other eight days of it, however many miles of ground that turned out to be.

## The One Small True Thing

She thought it once, on the twenty-ninth, at the cart wheel, and she did not say it and did not write it, and she never said it to anybody.

Nobody on that course asked her a single question in four weeks.

Not one. Not what she was, not where she was licensed, not what the paper with a seal on it was, not why she had been on the ring before the light, not where her money came from, not why she had not been at a county office this month.

Four weeks, with four stubs, and the count of questions asked of her by any person on that course, including the foreman and the tender, was none.

Every office she had ever stood in front of wanted one thing from her. Her name, or her hours, or her hand, or her signature, or her body, or her head, or eleven minutes of a column, or a decision.

This is the first place anybody has wanted her hours and nothing else.

She had a cold in the middle of her chest that would not go for four weeks, and about ten coppers, and eleven men who were right about everything, and four strips of paper in her coat, and she was not going west.
