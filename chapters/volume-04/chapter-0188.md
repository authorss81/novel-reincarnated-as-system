# A Man With a Family

The year came round on the second of those three days and there was nothing set aside for it.

That was the whole of it, and it came round the way it comes round, which is not on a date and not on a day of the month and not on anything she could have put a mark against in the autumn.

She noticed it at about the seventh hour on a bench in the wash house with the lamp on and the rain going on the slates above her, and she noticed it the way you notice a step that is not there any more.

She had kept one back every year since the middle of the last summer. One of the day's four, held out from the ration on purpose and for a year, and spent once, out loud, in a room, on the sentence that said she had not forgiven him.

Two years now. The first one in a warehouse off a lane with two witnesses in it. The second one in a county office on the square in a quieter voice, also with two witnesses, and after that she had written down in her own hand that she was not going to say it again for a while.

A while had run out.

She sat on the bench in the wash house with her hands in her coat and let the day come round, and there was nothing in the day's ration because she had spent that day's reserved sentence five nights ago on a number.

“Eight days out of about ten.”

That is what she had put it on. That is what a sentence she had been keeping for a year and a half went on, and she had done it on purpose, out loud, at the ninth hour, with nine men asleep, and she had not felt bad about it at the time, and she felt very bad about it now.

She could not say it.

That is the shape of it. She did not decide not to say it. She found, at about the seventh hour on a bench in a wash house in the third month of the cold, that the sentence she had been saving up for it had already been spent on a price, and that a person cannot say a thing they have already said about something else.

## The Lane

There is a lane off the west side of the square in Lowwater with a wall at the end of it about the height of a man, and it is the lane where she said it the first time, out loud, to a man in a winter's coat who was not the man it was about.

She went down it at about the second hour on the middle of those three days.

She stood at the wall.

Nine minutes' walk the other way, down the same lane and out onto a street that has a name on it, is a counting room with a door and a list on the inside of the door of who is not admitted.

She had a name.

She had most of what was left of him as well, and she could have spent every sentence of it in about an hour inside this county's boundary, standing in a lane in this town, four minutes' walk from a room where the name of the man she had just bought was, possibly, on a sheet somewhere, if a schedule-man leaves anything behind when he puts a date into somebody else's book.

It is not possible. That is the thing and she had known it before she got to the wall.

A schedule-man goes into a counting room with a date and comes out with the date entered, and he does not have a conversation, and he is not admitted to a room with a list on the door. If he had left anything behind, the counting room would not be holding anything she could reach.

And even if he had — she stood at the wall in the cold and worked it out honestly — even if she stood in that lane for the whole of what was left of him, and even if he came out of that counting room at the second hour and walked down nine minutes' worth of street to where she was standing, she would be a woman with one arm standing in a lane telling a stranger with a family what she had bought him for.

Which was the end of it, and she had come down the lane to find that out and had found it in about four minutes.

She came back up the hill.

## The Ticket

She wrote it on the back of the dead weight card, at about the ninth hour on the second of those three nights, with a pencil stub, in her own hand, in the ordinary way she writes her own hour on a Hall sheet.

Under the column of marks. Under the rule.

**Luo Ping.**

And then under that, because she was writing on a thing she had made for a discipline and not for a record, and she wanted it to be readable at the ninth hour in the cold by a person who is tired:

*schedule-man. 39. Wife and boy, eleven, past this county's boundary. Not visited. Not written to.*

That is what a person puts on a card. It took about forty seconds.

Then she put the card in the oilcloth against her ribs, above the plate, above the licence, under the layer of a thing that has been against her chest since the sixth day of the winter before any of this, and lay down on three dead tickets in the cold with nine men asleep and the fire a bed of ash.

She did not go and find him.

She had a name and a household on it and she could have spent the rest of him tonight, and she kept it, and the keeping was a decision and not a postponement, because the road on the shelf in the county office had a date on it and the road was east and the boundary was east, and everything in her head that was worth having about Luo Ping was past this county's boundary.

## What Replaced It

Not forgiveness.

She was very clear about that at about the ninth hour on the second of those nights, on a mat, and she said it out loud to nobody in the ordinary voice, which she had been doing for eight days now and which was the only new habit she had picked up in a year.

“I have not forgiven you,” she said, “and I am not going to, and I have just spent eight days of you on a name and I am going to spend the rest of you on the road and neither of those is the same as the other thing.”

He said nothing.

That was correct and it was what she wanted and she had said the sentence to nobody, and the whole of the difference between this year and the two before it was that she had said it out loud in a room with nine men asleep in it and nobody had heard it and nobody was going to.

## The Third Of The Three

On the third of them she went down to the frame and worked a shift and signed her own hour off the end of it with the flat of a second finger, and Gao held the board, and nothing was said about the quarterly, and the word on the front of the schedule was still on the board in the clerk's hand where nine men had left it.

At about the ninth hour she came back up and lay down and put four marks on a dead card for a day in which she had asked him two things, both of them about a cold end she had already worked out for herself, and both of them bought with a sentence she did not need.

That is what the ration looks like now and it is not what she built it for.

She had built it to stop herself buying information with him. She had been spending it on things she knew the answers to, at a rate of about two a day on a floor and two more in a cold room, and the card had four marks in every row of it and a stroke through each day, and there was not a single mark on that card in front of a question that mattered.

He had not said a word about that. She checked on the second night and he had not said a word about it at all, and a man who said nothing at all for three days about a thing he was living inside was either being careful or was waiting for her to notice.

She had noticed. She was going to keep spending.

## The Relief

The relief is the part she did not expect and had not been able to put down since.

It was not that she had forgiven him. It was that the yearly thing was over.

For a year and a half she had stood up, once a year, in a room, and said a true sentence out loud, in the ordinary voice, as though the account were being kept in his name and somebody were receiving it. And there had been no room and there had been no somebody, and the account had been hers, and the saying of it had done nothing at all except prove, to an empty room, that she was still angry.

And on the seventh hour of a bench in a wash house, on the day it came round, she found out that she did not have to do it any more.

Not because it was settled.

Because the sentence had gone on a price, and a person who is spending a man on purpose does not also owe him an annual performance of being owed.

That is what replaced it. Not forgiveness, not absolution, not the closure of anything at all. A purchase, on a chosen day, at a stated price, with the price said out loud first — which is the exact form of every other thing she had done to him since the middle of the summer.

And then, about nine counts later, the cost of the relief arrived, and it was this:

She had just agreed to spend him.

Not for a year. Not once a year in a room. She was going to spend him on days, in the amounts, in the order she decided, for as long as he lasted, and the discipline on the back of a dead card with four marks in a row was not a discipline she was doing for him.

She lay in the cold and let that sit.

The account did not close. It was not the same account it had been on the seventh hour of a bench two days ago and it would not be the same account in the spring, and the year that ran from a morning in the middle of the last summer was not over, and nothing in it had been forgiven in, and nowhere in it did anything say settled.

What changed was that she was no longer keeping it for him.

She was spending it on herself, out loud, at a price, on purpose, and the only thing in this room that was worse than the relief was that she had felt it the moment she sat down.
