# The Board

Cui Bao put her on the board on the twenty-eighth of the month because the man with the broken thumbnail was four days out at the far end of the working reach, and readings come in whether or not there is anybody standing in the camp to write them down.

There was one, which was her.

“You write what they tell you,” Cui Bao said. “You do not decide what is on it.”

It was two planks on end props under the eave at the west end of the camp, ruled into a margin and a field, and the margin carried about sixty painted marks stacked down it in the order they had been painted, which is not the order they are worked.

She had walked past that thing every working day for five weeks.

She had looked at it, on the way to the trestle and on the way back, the way a person looks at a wall.

## What Was On It

The margin was the working reach. She had that in about the time it takes to pick up a bar, standing at it, and it took her until the second morning to work out what the rest of the thing was for.

The field was ruled across into lines, one line for a day, and the day was in the left-hand cell, and then the field went on across in cells, one cell for each painted mark, nine cells to a line for the days when the crew worked the near end and more for the days they did not.

A man came in off a stretch at the second hour or the third and said what he had done at it, and she put the figure in the cell, and the figure was yards. A cell with a figure in it was a stretch attended. A cell with nothing in it was a stretch that had not been.

The whole of the arrangement was that a hole was visible.

There were eleven men and a girl of nineteen who came past that board every morning before the light was off the hill, and they looked at it and went out, and about nine of them went to a cell that was empty and two went to one that had a figure in it and had been filled again on a day when the stretch was not due.

Nobody upstairs of that board existed.

Not a clerk, not a county, not an office with a stair. There was a man of about fifty with a book, and a man of about thirty with a second book, and twelve people who get up in the dark, and about sixty painted marks, and that was the entire coordination of a nine-day line of work.

## Eleven Minutes A Line

It took her eleven minutes to write a day.

She wrote with the flat of a second finger, which she had been doing for three hundred and some county hours, and which is slower than anything a person with a hand writes with by about a factor of three, and a day's line on that board is twenty cells and a date.

Ma Sheng wrote one in four.

She did the arithmetic on the second morning, standing at the board at the fifth hour with her right arm open the way it opened, and the arithmetic was that a day written the way she wrote it came out at about one hour and three quarters, and the board was read before the light was off the hill, and the light was off the hill at about the seventh hour.

So the day's readings went up at the seventh hour, at the earliest, on about two days in three.

## What Was Done About It

Nothing.

That is the part of it that stayed with her for the rest of the season and the part she went over afterwards in a bunk with three boards in it, at night, with a cold under her ribs that a working season had not taken out of it.

Not the foreman, not the tender, not the man with the broken thumbnail, who came back on the fourth day, read four days of what she had written standing up in the light, and said one thing about a cell he thought should have been a hundred and ten.

The girl of nineteen came past it twice a day and did not slow down for it.

Nobody offered to take a line off her, and nobody was unkind, and that is not the part that was hard.

The hard part was that it never came up at all.

A place where people are paid by the day does not stop the line for a slow hand, and a person who is paid by the day is not going to be the one who stops it. If a day's readings cannot be written in the time there is to write them in, then the day goes up late, and every man who comes in after it says his figure into a board that is a day behind him.

So they went out at the fourth hour and worked, and she wrote, and on about two days in three the sixth hour came round and the twentieth line was still not finished.

That is all of it. It was arithmetic, and it was not cruelty, and it was never going to be mentioned again by anybody at that camp.

## Three Mornings At The Fourth Hour

What she did about it was the only thing available, which was to stand there until her hand knew the shape of it.

So she was at the board at the fourth hour for three mornings, which is an hour before anybody came in, and she copied the shape of a day onto the back of a dead weight ticket.

She copied the date in the left cell. She copied the marks in the right order down the page so that her hand would go to the right cell without her telling it to. She copied the figures the way a man says them, which is that he does not say them at all, he says *the whole of it* or *a half* or *twenty off the north end*, and the person with the pen turns that into a number.

By the third morning the ticket had the whole shape of a day on it in a hand that was not hers.

And on the third morning the tender came in at the fourth hour and read back the day before, all twenty cells, out loud, slowly, in the way a man reads a thing he is about to be responsible for, and he came to a line she had written and read it and went on to the next one.

He did not change it.

That was the entire review. There was no other sentence about it and there was not going to be one, and she stood on the other side of a board with a dead weight ticket in her coat and understood that she had passed something that nobody on that course was going to congratulate her for.

## What The Board Was Doing At The Fourth Hour

The first morning she stood at it and simply watched, which she had not done anywhere in four years.

At the fourth hour a man comes in. He is wet to the thigh and he has been in the ring since before there was light. He puts two fingers on the plank about a third of the way down and says *two hundred and forty, whole of it*, and she writes two hundred and forty in that cell, and he goes and sits down by the fire.

At the fifth hour another one, from a different end.

At the sixth hour the foreman reads the board. He reads it standing up, from the left, with a thumb going along under the lines, and every now and then he stops and puts his thumb on a cell and says a number out loud, and eleven men who are already on their feet move to the cells he said, and go out.

Nobody upstairs of that board existed, and no man who came in that morning knew that he had been chosen by a thumb.

She had been in rooms for four years. She had stood in a hall with no roof with nine lines and three holes in it, in a county office where the ceiling on her licence turned out to be a permission, in a tower where a keeper who wanted to be asked would not ask, and in nine counties that had each read out whatever she did and had it in a copy by the time she got back to where she slept.

And the whole of the thing that was holding nine days of work together on that plank was sixty painted marks and a rule that a man attends a stretch on the day the stretch is due.

She argued none of it, and she was given no answer, and she did not look for one.

The tender said one thing about the board on the second morning of the three and it was not advice and it was not for her.

“A man who works a stretch puts fresh marks on it as he goes,” he said. “That is in the book and it is not in the board. The board is what happened. The marks are what he did about it, and if he did not do it, the marks are last season's and a man reads them at the fourth hour and knows before he gets there.”

He put a thumb on the plank.

“That is the whole reason there is a board. Not so anybody can count. So that a man standing at it can see at a glance which places did not get their day.”

The foreman was right about the schedule. The tender was right about the drift, and he had been right about it at a trestle in the second week, and she had stood inside the ring and seen the proof and had not said one word.

The girl of nineteen was right about the pace. She did the same eight yards in eight minutes that Ning Yan took forty to do, and she was right about that too, and there is no argument available against a person who is simply faster at a thing.

All four of them were right and all four of them had no reason in the world to be wrong, and she was the strongest body on that ground and the slowest person at the only object there was.

## The End Of The Board

She squared her last line on the thirtieth, which is the last day of the month the road had been shut on, and she put the chalk in the groove it lived in and stood back.

The board is longer than the working reach.

That is the thing she had not known at the start and could not have guessed at the start, because the crew does not work the last eleven feet of it and there is no mark painted for the last eleven feet and no cell ruled under them, and the whole camp walks past that end of the plank twice a day.

At the far end of it, past the last mark anybody on this course attends, there is one more column.

It is narrower than the rest and it is ruled separately and there is no heading over it she could read, and it is not ruled the way the readings are ruled — the spacing in it is wider apart, and it does not begin at the top, it begins about a hand's width down, which is what a column does when something has been going into it for a long time.

There was nothing in it.

She looked at it for about as long as it takes to count something and then she went and got her bar and went over the wire.

About ten feet of board at the end of a nine-day line, and a column in it that no person in that camp filled, and it was not empty because it was new.
