# The Last Cart West

The board where the carriers' schedule lived was in the back of the same hut as the wedge of paper and it had been there since she came, and she had looked at it every day for a week without reading it.

She read the board on the day before the sixteenth, and that was the first time she had read any of it.

Ma Sheng put the fire in the stove and said, without turning round, “It is going east for four days and then it is not going east for anything until the month turns,” and then he read her the west-going line out of it out loud, in under a minute, the way a man reads out a thing he has read every week for nine years.

He did not ask her why she wanted it.

He did not say she should go and he did not say she should not.

## What The Board Says

The west-going goes on a schedule that is not the county's and is not anybody's county. A carter comes east with whatever there is and turns round at the last place it turns and comes back, and the coming back is eighteen days from the going, and in the middle of it there is whatever there is in the middle of it, which is two weeks of nobody.

“That is the carrier that went west eleven days ago with the month's sheet on it,” Ma Sheng said, and put a thumb on the board. “It is the one that would have carried a letter. It comes back about the third of the next month, and it will carry whatever anybody gives it going east, and the going east is the other side of that sheet, and the going east has three more days on it this week.”

He put his thumb down.

“The next west-going is not that one. The next west-going is a man who runs it on his own book because he has a house on the far side of this valley and he does not take passengers at all, and he leaves when he leaves, and he will not leave this week.”

She looked at the board.

It was a good board. That is the thing about a board that somebody has kept for nine years: it has four columns and a rule down the middle and about three years of entries going back in a hand that changes about once a year.

She looked at it for as long as was polite and then she stopped looking at it.

## The Other Reason

There was a second thing under the first thing and she had it in her head before he finished reading, and the board was not the reason she came to that hut.

Eighteen days east and back. The carrier that went west eleven days ago with the month on it is the last one, and the next one is a man who runs his own book and will not leave this week, and the one after that is eighteen days off at the earliest.

Tomorrow was the sixteenth of the month.

It was never going to be reachable. Not by a cart and not by a cart that went faster. Nine days east and eighteen days on the road and a turnaround that does not care who has a reason, and the sixteenth is in the morning and there has never been a version of the last nine days in which a woman who left a county on the morning of the twenty-fourth of a month was going to be standing on a square at the third hour of the sixteenth.

She had not counted it. That was not an excuse and she did not use it as one. She had not counted it, and the reason she had not counted it was not that the road was hard or that the tin was thin or that a county had written something on a bar in the ordinary hand.

She had not counted it because on the morning of the first of the month, at first light, with a bag with four things in it and a flat of something warm in her coat, she had looked at a baked flat wrapped in a cloth and a dressed stone with nothing written on it, and the count had not been in her head, and she had walked off the top of the hill.

That is the sentence. It is not a reason. A reason would be a road or a purse or a season, and there are four separate days in it where she could have turned round at a shoulder in the road and gone back and had that morning four miles early and stood on the square at the second hour on the second instead.

## The Tin

The tin came out of the sixth day lighter than it had gone in.

Six coppers a day, and a place at the fire that was a third of it, and two cakes that were another third, and a woman who was not going to be able to put anything by at the rate of one day.

What she had brought — about thirty coppers, spent on a seat at a bridge, two days of food and four more days of bread — was gone in twelve days, and what was in the tin that morning was four and a half coppers, and she had to make the half up herself.

The days since then had gone through the tin at six coppers in and six coppers out, which is not a thing a person does on purpose. It is a thing a person does when the fire is a third of the wage.

So there was no way west.

That was arithmetic and it was the arithmetic of a purse and not of a county and not of a boundary, and it was given to her four days ago at a bridge by a driver counting coin into a fold of his coat.

She had known it since then. That was the part that stayed.

She had known since the first, or at any rate since the second at a bridge, that a seat was a thing she had been holding and not a thing she had, and she had gone on east anyway, because the only thing east was the hours and the hours were the only thing east was.

Thirty coppers and a seat at a bridge.

It is an absurd amount of money to be nine days from a counter.

## What He Gave Her And Did Not Know He Was Giving Her

Ma Sheng read the west-going line out of a board to a woman standing in front of it, because she was standing in front of it and because it is on the board.

That was the whole of why he did it. It took under a minute and it cost him nothing and to this day he did not know that he had done anything.

He was the first person in eleven days who had done something for her for nothing.

He was not doing it for her sake. He was doing it because he keeps a board and somebody was standing in front of his board in the ordinary course of a morning, and a man who keeps a board reads it out to whoever is standing there. He would have read it to a carter. He would have read it to the girl.

He brought her a second blanket that evening, without being asked and without a word, and she did not thank him and does not know to this day why she did not.

## The Word She Said Out Loud

She spent one of the day's ration on it, at the board, at the last of the light, with nobody within forty feet.

“The sixteenth,” she said.

That is what she bought with it. Four words of a date, said out loud, once, in a cold hut nine days east of a county, to no one at all.

She did not say it to him and he did not hear it, and it had cost her a voice and it bought nothing.

She put the four and a half coppers on the plank at the fire and the tin came out lighter again.

## The Evening

The men were in the ring by the fire and it was the same ring it had been every night, and the argument had got to a different subject, and one of them was telling a thing about a man called something she still did not catch, who had done a thing on a stretch that had a number on it.

She sat with her back against the cart wheel about six feet off and listened to nine hundred words of the inside of a place where nothing was wanted from her, and this time she was counting them, because she had a date and she was putting it against the outside of the days the way you put a mark against a wall.

She got up at about the second hour.

She did not go over the wire. She went and stood outside it in the dark and looked at the ground for a while without any idea what she was looking for, and the thing she was looking for was not in the ground.

She got about four hours that night in a bunk with three boards and a mat on it and a second blanket she had not asked for, and she woke twice, and both times what woke her was the same thing: that she had a date tomorrow and no plan at all for the date, and had never had a plan for it, and it was the sixteenth and she had known the day and the night and the hour at which it was.

That is what she had against her. Not a road. Not a county. A morning on a hill with a flat of bread in a coat.

Tomorrow was the sixteenth.

There was no cart, and there was no coin, and there was no counter she could be standing at by the third hour, and the sheet with her name on it was nine days behind her and going west on a carrier whenever a carrier next went west.
