# Luo Ping

The room at the back of the camp where the week's days got written into somebody else's book was a hut with a counter in it and a stove and a window, and there was a man in it doing the work when she came, and she had come nine days east to ask a man one question.

He was about thirty-nine and thin in the wrists and there was a wedge of paper squared on the counter in front of him with a date at the top of it.

She stood in the doorway.

“You will be wanting a name,” he said, without looking up. “I am going to ask you one thing first, which is whether it is going to take the whole of the evening, so that I can put a lamp on it.”

“It will take a moment.”

“Then you can stand in the doorway.”

She asked him whether his name was Luo Ping.

He put the nib down, and turned round, and looked at her properly for the first time, and it was a long look and it was not unfriendly.

“It is,” he said.

## Sixteen Years

He said the rest of it himself, which she had not asked for and did not stop.

“I am a schedule-man. Sixteen years. I go into four or five offices in a season and I am sent, and I put the dates into their book in the order they want them, and then I am paid by the day for the days it takes me and I go to the next one.”

He turned back to the counter and squared the wedge.

“Nobody has ever once asked me whether a date ought to have gone in. Sixteen years. I have been in rooms with money on the table and I have been asked what I think of a date by a woman in a good coat and by a clerk and by a man who wanted me not to, and never once has anybody stood in a doorway and asked me whether I thought it should have gone in.”

He said it in the ordinary voice of a man describing a job.

“I will tell you what I am, because you have a licence and you have come a long way and I would rather you had it right than have you have it wrong. I am not somebody who has an opinion about the dates. I am somebody who has a hand that is good at the order they go in.”

She spent one of the day's ration and it bought that.

## Two Questions

The second one cost her the other, and it bought less than the first.

She asked whether he had been in a counting room in Lowwater.

He said: “I have been in every counting room between here and there four times in nine years. Which one.”

She stopped there.

She had two more in her, and she had them ready, and she worked out standing at the counter of a hut in a cold country that she was not going to ask them and that the asking was the whole of what she had come nine days to do.

Because he had been in a counting room in Lowwater in the spring, and a date had gone into a book in that room against an eight hundred jin claim standing against a woman, and the date was not a figure, it was a day, and he had written the day.

And a county does not put a date on a thing it cannot find.

So the county had been able to find her, or the county had been given her, or somebody had handed over a name in exchange for the price of a day's travel by a man who was under no instruction to look at anything.

She did not ask.

If she had asked, he would have answered, and he would have said that he had been sent, and that he had written what he was sent to write, and that he did not know who had sent him on that occasion and would not say if he did.

He would have told her. That is what the sixteen years are. He would have told her the true thing in the ordinary voice and it would have told her nothing at all, and she would have had to carry it west, or put it down somewhere, and there is no room on a dead weight ticket for it.

She said: “That is all.”

## What She Took Out Of The Room

She took out of the room that a man who keeps other people's schedules does not stop keeping them because you know his name, and that she had wanted a man who could be caught and there was not one on this course or on any other that she would find in a season.

That is a correct amount and it is less than she came for.

She did not tell him what the date was against.

She did not tell him it was a claim, or that it was eight hundred jin, or that there is a document in a coat lining nine days behind her with her name on the front of it and the claim as the seventh and last thing on the paper, or that the back of it has a bounty written against it somewhere in an office the county cannot climb a stair to.

He was a man at work and she was a woman who could ruin him in about nine words and get nothing whatever for it.

And she did not tell him she knew about the household either.

That was the thing she was actually careful about, and it was the only part of the two days she would want written down anywhere.

Nine days west of that camp on high ground there was a woman and a boy of eleven who did not know that their husband had put a date in a room in Lowwater for money. He had not said so and she did not say so, and she did not say so because saying it would have been a way of making him small.

She thought about it for a while on the way back, which was a thing she had no right to do, and then she did not think about it again.

## Afterwards

She wrote it on the back of the second dead weight ticket that evening, outside the camp, with a pencil stub and her left hand flat on her knee.

Two lines. The first said that a man who keeps other people's schedules is a trade and not a person, and that finding him had not answered the date. The second said that the claim stands.

Then she put the ticket in the oilcloth with the sheet's three marks on the corner of it, and she was aware that she was now carrying three pieces of paper against her ribs that all said the same thing from three different hands, and that not one of them was the piece of paper that would have mattered.

She had wanted the piece of paper that would have mattered.

## His Hands

The thing she noticed last, standing in a doorway, was his hands.

They were the hands of a man who writes for a living and has never in his life had to lift anything that was not paper. The nails were short and the first joints were bent a little the way they bend in a man who has held a nib since he was twenty-three. One thumb had a small black stain in the crease of it from four hundred years of a kind of ink.

She had the heel of her left hand gone, and she signed a county column with the flat of a second finger in eleven minutes.

They sat about eleven feet apart across a counter in a cold hut and neither of them looked at the other's hands.

## What It Answered

Nothing.

It answered nothing.

The claim stood. The date was in the counting room in Lowwater on the seventh and last condition of a licence she was carrying against her ribs, and it had not been answered and had not been torn off.

Hou Sanliang was in Lowwater, unpaid and patient, and had never been in a room with her and did not enter one that day and was not going to enter one this season.

And the thing she wanted — who sent him, and why, and what was on the piece of paper that made a man write a date in a room in a town he had no business in — was not available to her, and he did not have it, and he would have told her so.

So she walked away with less than she came for, standing in a cold country with six coppers a day in a tin that came out lighter every evening, and the discovery she took with her was the plain one.

A schedule-man is not a person who can be found.

It was a trade. A very good trade. There were thousands of them and every office in nine counties had four or five, and they all wrote the same hand.

A woman could spend eight days of the only voice she had left to find one, arrive at a hut, be told the truth and go away with nothing — and it would turn out that she could have got here without finding him at all.

The road was nine days and the road did not need him. He was on the road because the road goes where his work goes.

That was the whole of what nine days bought, and she had known it before she got to the door.
