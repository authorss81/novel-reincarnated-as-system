# His Own Request

The copying floor inside the tower was the only room in four miles that had been warm every night since the coal came, and she went down into it on the third morning with a sheet of paper in her coat and no reason at all, which was the first time in nineteen months she had gone anywhere without a reason.

## Four Flights

She went up them rather than down into them, because the tower's floor is the second and the copying floor is the fourth, and going down is two flights and going up is four, and she had learned in the autumn that she would rather pay four flights going up than two flights coming down, because the two flights coming down would arrive at her second floor before she had decided anything.

The stair is a stone stair with a rail on the third side of it that a man put there himself in the spring before she came, when he was sixty and still coughing, and he has never once been thanked for the rail by anybody including himself.

Three counts on the third flight, going up, since the ice. Never back to two. She had said that out loud to nine men in a furnace hall in the middle of the month and had written a number against it on a piece of paper in her own hand in a county office in the summer, and both of those things were true and neither of them made the stair shorter.

At the top of the fourth flight there is a landing with a window in it that does not close, and every person who has ever come up that stair has stood on that landing for about four seconds in the cold before they have gone in, and Feng Lushi has never once told anybody to keep moving.

Feng Lushi was at the long table with his back to the stair door because the draught came down it, and there was a lamp on the table at his elbow that had a glass chimney on it and the chimney had been cleaned that week, which she knew because the last time she had been in this room the chimney had been brown.

He was sixty-two. He had been warm for the first time in twenty-two years since the autumn before last, and he was coughing less, and he was writing more, and there were two hundred or so volumes behind him on the shelves and a third of them on the floor because the shelves had never been long enough for what came in.

Four hundred and eleven volumes in the tower, of which about ninety had a ninth stage in them in his fair hand, and every one of those ninety was in a bound copy and in the building and not in the county, because a tower copy is the building's copy and a county copy is the county's and they are not the same paper and they do not go to the same place.

“Column,” he said.

“That is the column.”

He took it and read the top of it, which was her name in the county's hand, and then he put it down flat on the table and squared it with two fingers, and then he began to copy it, which was what he had done every time for four months and had never once done in a hurry.

## The Column In A Fair Hand

His copy came out the way his copies always came out, which is better than the county's, and anybody who has ever had a thing copied fairly can tell the difference in about four seconds and cannot say what it is.

It read one, two, three.

Then a ruled line with nothing in it.

Then five, six, seven.

Then another ruled line with nothing in it.

Then nine.

He had been copying that column for nine years and he could not read what was in the gaps of it and had not been able to for as long as either of them had been paying attention, and he had not explained that to her in nineteen months and was not going to start.

He wrote the nine the way he wrote everything, with the brush going down and the hair coming off it at the end of the stroke, and when it was finished he put the sheet flat on the table in front of her and turned it round.

“There,” he said.

## What She Wanted To Happen

She had wanted him to ask.

That was the whole of what she had come down the hill for, and it had taken her four miles and about eleven minutes at a counter and the better part of a day's thinking to admit to herself, and she was admitting it standing in a warm room with a fair copy on a table in front of her.

She wanted one man in this world to look at three ruled lines in a column he had copied nine years' worth of, and say *what is in them*.

Anybody could have said it. The county's own clerk said it out loud in a room in front of a queue on the nineteenth of the month, four words, three times, and stopped. Gao said it out loud in a hall so that nine men would hear it from a mouth instead of off a wall. A man over a tanner's door had said the opposite thing at a public counter in the summer and had been right.

She wanted it from the one man who had written down every stage she had ever taken, in a fair hand, at the hour it happened, for twenty-two years, and had never once asked her what any of them meant.

He did not ask.

He had not asked since the autumn before last, at about the third hour on a floor with the lamp turned down and a question in her mouth that she had not asked, and he had gone on not asking, and by the time a person has not asked a question for four months the not-asking has stopped being a courtesy and become a wall, and she had learned to read walls for a living.

## Why

He fed the lamp while she thought about it, and then he said it, and he said it the way he said everything, which is at the speed of a man who has had twenty-two years to decide how much of a thing to say.

“A keeper who asks is a keeper who has begun to want something,” said Feng Lushi. “I have wanted nothing since the autumn and I would like to go on not wanting things until they are done with me, and the only way I know to not want a thing is to not know whether there is one.”

He put the brush down in the rest.

“That is the correct answer,” he said. “It is not a very good one. I have thought about it for four weeks and I have not come up with a better one, and I have gone back to copying.”

## The Arrangement He Put In Writing

Below his floor, on the second, is the reading room, and above his floor, on the fifth, is the roof and the weather. Between those two and the copying floor there is a locked press and the district's quarterly bundle comes out of it on its own schedule and goes up four flights in the arms of a clerk from the district who is younger than him and steadier than him.

That is what the arrangement is. One quarter off his own stair, at his own written request, entered on his own sheet in the ordinary hand, not refused and not thanked for.

What it bought him is the only thing he has ever asked for in twenty-two years, which is that the thing that goes up this stair goes up true.

A district wage quarterly. Oil. A hundredweight of coal. A chair in the county office on reading days, which he has never once sat in and which is standing empty in a low room with eleven windows in it this morning.

And the standing of a Listener, for life, set aside able at his own request, on the understanding that whatever comes up this stair goes up as it is — entered or not entered, held or not held, refused or not refused — and that a keeper who loses a thing on the way up has not lost it.

That is the trade. He made it in the autumn on a sheet of his own paper and he has not gone back into it once, and there is a notice of it on the wall of the landing in his own hand so that a man carrying a bundle up four flights knows what he is carrying and what it is for.

“They do not use the chair,” said Feng Lushi.

“I know.”

“They have never once used the chair in eleven years of the office having it and eight months of me not sitting in it,” he said. “It is the most valuable chair in this county and the only one in the building with nothing in it, and I would like it understood that I put that in writing as well.”

He put the brush down in the rest.

“That is the second piece of paper this quarter that I have wanted somebody to read,” he said, “and you did not ask me about either of them.”

“No,” said Ning Yan. “I did not.”

## The Price Of The Lamp

She stayed.

There was nothing to stay for. The copying was finished and the sheet was in her hand and there was no second column and no second sheet, and the ordinary thing to do was to go up four miles in the cold and be at the floor by the ninth hour, and she stayed for about forty minutes with the sheet in her coat and her right arm held close.

The lamp was warm. The chimney was clean. There was a smell in the room of lamp oil and hot paper that she had smelled four times in nineteen months and had never once been able to get anything else out of.

Somewhere below her, on the stair, a district clerk was carrying a bundle up four flights with a strap over his shoulder and was going to come down those four flights with it in his arms empty, because the man who owned the lamp had asked for that arrangement in writing in the middle of the autumn and had not been refused and had not been thanked for it.

That was the whole of the exchange between the two of them. He had given up a quarter of the stair he had walked for twenty-two years, and in exchange he got a bundle carried up by a man whose arms were steadier than his, and it came down empty, and he had written down in his own hand that he wanted it that way.

She did not thank him for the lamp and he did not expect it, and she did not ask him anything either, and they sat in a warm room for forty minutes with an hour of lamp oil in it that somebody else was paying for.

## The One Sentence

“You will be up here again before the thaw,” said Feng Lushi. “The column gets copied at the quarter and the quarter is in the spring.”

“I know when the quarter is.”

“You will be up here again before the thaw,” he said, “and you are not going to be able to say what came back.”

“Yes,” said Ning Yan.

She went out into the cold at about the seventh hour and up the four miles with a sheet in her coat and an hour of somebody else's lamp oil in her, and she thought about the one sentence she had spent in that room, which had bought her one fact about a lamp and a chimney and a man who had not asked her a question for four months, and had cost her nothing at all, and had been worth less than eleven minutes.

She did not thank him.

She got up the stair on three counts and never got back to two, and she lay down in the second bunk with the licence over her head and a fair copy in her coat that read one, two, three, a ruled line, five, six, seven, a ruled line, nine.

Two blank lines in it. She had spent four miles of road and an hour of a lamp somebody else was paying for, and one sentence of the only voice she had, to find out that she wanted to be asked something.

The only person who might ever have asked her was a keeper who had decided four weeks ago, on purpose, in writing, in his own head, never to want anything again.
