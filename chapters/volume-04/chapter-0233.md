# The Blind Run

She was at the plank before the fourth hour on the fifth, which she has not done in about a month, and he was at it before she was.

He wrote her down the way he writes everybody down. The date, and the mark of the stretch she has worked since the twenty-first of a month two months back, and the five.

Then he shut the book part way and put his hand flat on it, and looked out past the cart turn, and said the name of it in the ordinary voice, the way a man names a neighbour's field.

“The bad flat,” he said. “Past the turn and down and along. Three-quarters of a mile of it. You have not been on it in a month and you have been near it about nine evenings running and I can see the pair of you from this plank.”

“I have not been near it.”

“You have stood on the rise,” he said. “Same as every second man on a line of work, once a season. And I told you on the fourth of the month before last not to go and be clever at it, and I am not going to tell you twice, and that is the last time it comes up.”

He squared the page with the heel of his hand.

“You want it.”

“I want it.”

## What It Pays

He looked at her for about the length of a mouthful, which is a thing he does.

“There is nobody to hire on that ground,” he said. “That is not me being hard on you, that is what it is. It is not on my book because it is not my work, it is not on theirs because they have never heard of it, and if I write a day's hours against it I am writing a man's hours against a stretch nobody sent him to, and my book is true.”

“Yes.”

“It pays two coppers,” he said, “and it goes in your hand, and it does not go in mine.”

He said that last part twice, slowly, the way a man says a thing he wants a person to have properly before the person acts on it, and then he put the pen behind his ear.

“You can work my stretch four hours and go out there the rest of it, and I will pay you two a day for going, and I will not write one hour of it anywhere, and if you go into that low side and it does not come up, I will not be the man who stands a stretch on it, because there is no stretch and there is no board and there is nobody to stand it.”

“Yes,” she said.

Nobody persuaded her. That is the whole of what happened at that plank on the fifth of a month at about the fourth hour. She asked for it out loud at a trestle in daylight and she did not argue for it once, and a man of about forty-five told her three true things about it in about nine sentences and got on with his day.

## Her Name, Third Time

He wrote it in at the fourth hour with the other line, and said it as he said it, in the way the plank heard it.

“Twenty-two.”

That is her name on that ground for the third time. A foreman said it at a counter inside the wire about two months back and she gave it and it is the name the strike went through. A man with a book of four columns and a painted mark said it at this plank about a month back and she gave it and the two books began that hour.

The third time was the worst one, because this was the trestle where she buys her bread with the money and sleeps within a hundred and forty yards of it, and she said it out loud in front of four men at the pot and a man with a boot off his foot, and none of them had anything to do with it.

“Twenty-two,” the man with the boot said. “You have said that here three times.”

“I have.”

“You go out there for nothing,” he said.

“Yes.”

“I will come and look at it,” he said, “on the eleventh, about the fourth hour, and I will not go down into it.”

“I would rather you did not,” she said, and meant it, and it was the only thing she said to anybody all morning that was about herself.

## What Is On It

She found out what was on it at about the fifth hour, on the rise, with the bar across her knees, and he told her in the ordinary voice in one breath because she had been walking toward that rise for a month with a thing she could not ask him about.

“That ground is listed,” he said. “It has been listed since before you came east. The layer lists jobs, not men, and that one is on it because it is work nobody will take and nobody has finished.”

“And when somebody finishes it.”

“Then it settles,” he said, “and it does not settle into a county book and it does not settle into a man with a board at a trestle two hundred yards behind you. It settles into the thing that is reading the whole of this, and I am a piece of that, and I have not got to work very hard to know what is on a stretch you are standing on.”

He said it in about four sentences and he did not advise her, and he did not tell her it was worth doing, and he did not tell her it was a waste.

That was the whole of it and she sat on the rise with a bar across her knees and did the arithmetic, and the arithmetic was one line long.

Nine days of work on it and there is not one hour of it anybody can read except what she writes herself with the flat of a second finger at eleven minutes a line on the back of a dead weight ticket.

And it is the only work within nine days of road that does not have to be written by anybody else.

## The Word

She went back down and picked the bar up.

She did it at about the fifth hour on the fifth of the month in the ordinary voice, standing on the ground she had been standing on every evening for a month, with four men at a pot about eight feet behind her and nobody in the room but him.

The price had been said on the twenty-sixth of the month before, once, in a number, and she did not say it again and he did not say it again.

“One day out of the three I was keeping,” she said. “Say it and hold it until the crossing is over.”

“You know what it is.”

“I know what it is.”

“I am holding it.”

That was the whole of the word and of the holding. It went into the ground the way the stages go into the ground, without a sound anybody in nine counties could have said out loud, and it was under a day and it was put down when the crossing was over and nobody in this country has ever held anything for a person and will not go on holding it after.

She had one mark left on the back of a dead weight ticket after that, and the one mark was four sentences, and she had already decided what the four sentences were for and she did not spend them on this.

She worked four hours on the mark that was painted on the plank and then she went out past the cart turn with the bar on her shoulder.

## The First Hour Of It

It takes forty minutes from the trestle to the place where the road turns down, and about ten minutes of that past the rise on ground that is not a stretch and not anybody's job.

Then the low side.

It is a foot and a bit and it is quiet. She had been inside it on one knee on the first of a month and it had answered her boot nine or ten times and given back the same answer every time, and it did not tell her anything about what was underneath her while her weight was on it, and that is the whole of what is dangerous about it.

She went in about eleven feet from the top of the cut and put the bar in, and the bar went down through about a foot and stopped, and there was nothing under it.

That is the second time. On the first she was on her knees with her hands in it and she did not put a bar in it at all.

She built a hold from nothing, which takes about as long as a man counts out a day's coppers, and put the weight over it, and turned the bar, and got two feet of it up in one movement on ground that is not worked by anybody and has not been worked by anybody, and there was nobody standing anywhere near her to see it.

She wrote it down at the top of the rise at about the seventh hour, on the back of a dead weight ticket, with the flat of a second finger, in one line that took eleven minutes: the fifth, the stretch past the turn, the count of the hours.

The first day of it was four hours and it took a long time to do four hours and she was at four counts of breath inside the second one and did not go in.

She walked back up past the last stake at about the seventh hour with her hands cut across the two places a bar holds a hand and went and bought bread for five coppers out of the two coppers a day she had earned and the five in her hand, and the woman behind the counter put it down on the other side of the salt.

She had said her name out loud for the third time on that ground at a trestle in daylight, and nobody had persuaded her, and she was on it, and there was no book in the world with her name on it past the cart turn.
