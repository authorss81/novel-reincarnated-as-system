# The Sub-Contractor's Mark

She worked five days from the twelfth to the sixteenth and she did not try to put anything into the three dates that were empty.

That is the decision and it took about four minutes to arrive at and it is the first decision in a month she did not have to argue with anybody about.

A book that keeps hours that were not worked on the stretch it names is a book that lies. That is not a discipline and it is not a punishment. It is a wage-book, and the wage in it is somebody else's money, and the hours in it are hers.

## Squaring The Book

He squared it on the sixteenth at about the seventh hour, standing at the plank, and she was there because she is always there at the seventh hour.

He turned the page and ran a thumb down the hours column from the top.

He did not stop. That was the thing she had braced for on the eleventh and it did not come. He ran his thumb down three empty lines and went along the three empty lines and went on to the twelfth, and wrote the figures in beside the twelfth, and the thirteenth, and the fourteenth, and the fifteenth, and the sixteenth, in the ordinary way, and shut it.

“You have not said anything about the tenth,” she said.

“No.”

“You could have put hours in them.”

“No,” he said, and squared the page against the plank with the heel of his hand, which is what a man does to a book he has finished with. “If I put hours in there and a man reads that book in six weeks, he has got a woman on a stretch on the tenth and the stretch was not worked, and then he has got a hole somewhere he cannot see, and a hole he cannot see is worse than one he can.”

“A hole I made.”

“A hole you made,” he said. “It is in my book and my book is true. It is not my business what a hole in it costs a woman nine days of road away.”

He did not look up when he said it and she did not thank him for it, and thanking him would have made it a thing he had done for her, and it was not that.

## The Counterfoil

He tore one off the book on the sixteenth and put it on the plank face down and went to the pot.

That is what the stubs are. A strip of the same paper as the book, torn at the rule, about nine inches by two, with the date on it and the stretch and the hours and the rate and his mark in the corner, and a painted mark beside the mark.

There are four of hers in an oilcloth against her ribs and they are all one contractor's.

That one was a different contractor's.

She turned it over at about the seventh hour and looked at the mark in the corner for about as long as it takes to look at a thing, and then she went and put it in the oilcloth above the Clay Plate with the other four, and it is the fifth, and it is the first one in the world that a person can hold in a hand and read and cannot check.

## What A Mark Is Worth

A mark nobody checks is worth exactly what the man who makes it is worth, and that is not nothing.

That is what she had worked out over the fifth day, sitting on a stone past the trestle at about the fifth hour with five counterfoils in her lap and three of them a month old.

The course's stubs are a contractor's mark and a paint and a date. Every office that has ever read one of them read it in a room and never saw the man and never saw the ground, and a wedge of paper went up a stair out of nine days of them and was read aloud by a clerk she was nine seconds from and did not go to.

And this one is a different paint and a different hand and a different man who is not answerable to the man who struck her name out of a book, and there is no wedge of paper anywhere in the world that will ever come out of it, because a sub-contractor's stub does not go up a stair. It goes in a coat, and it is carried about by a person, and it is kept.

Both of those are true and only one of them is worth anything to her, and it is worth something, because five stubs in an oilcloth with five different days on them and five different marks is a record a person can keep.

Two books cannot make a run and a run was the whole of the instrument. That has not changed. What has changed is that she has five pieces of paper that say she was somewhere on five days, and they are all she has, and none of them can be compared with either book.

She put the five out on her knee in the order they were torn off and read them against each other the way a clerk reads two columns, and the dates came out clean — the twelfth, the thirteenth, the fourteenth, the fifteenth, the sixteenth — and the hours came out at eleven on every one of them, and the stretches all said the same painted mark, and not one thing on any of the five referred to the ninth, the tenth or the eleventh.

She did not write those three days anywhere.

That was the whole of the fifth day and it took about eleven minutes a line to write down on the back of a dead weight ticket and about an hour to sit with afterwards.

## The Woman With The Team

Wei Ran came past the trestle on the fourteenth at about the second hour with one team and a load of stone and did not stop, and she slowed down for about the length of a cart being walked past a trestle.

“You are still on that stretch,” she said.

“Yes.”

“You are on the worst-worked stretch between here and the wire and you have been on it twelve days and I have come past here six times.” She looked at the trestle, at the pot, at the four men. “And you have not asked the man for a different one.”

“No.”

“You could ask him,” Wei Ran said. “He would not give you a better one and he would not say no. He would put you on the same one and he would think about it for a day.”

It was the correct thing and it was said as a fact and she did not look at her while she said it.

“Twenty coppers,” Wei Ran said, “and I take you out past the cart turn and set you down at the yard and I come back on the way, and it is not a favour and it is not a question. It is a rate and a road.”

“I know what is out there.”

“You know what is out there the way a person knows what is on a board.” The horses were moving. “I have taken two loads that way in four years and I went round both times, and I am telling you that because you have got a bar and you have been looking over that rise for a fortnight, and everybody who works a line looks at ground nobody hires on about once a season and about half of them go.”

The cart went on.

She did not go.

## The Seventh Stage In The Body

The seventh came on the first of the three days she spent on ground nobody records and it is in her body now the way the sixth is and the fifth is, and it is three things.

She is heavy. There is a base under her that a body carries instead of a spine and it is colder than the sixth and colder than the fifth and it is the coldest thing about her, and she is cold at a fire and out of it and cold in a hut with three boards in it, and it is not weather and it will not be weather.

She is strong in a way that has stopped being useful. The two feet of it up in one movement, the four turns of a bar without shifting her feet — a man at the trestle watched her do it on the fifteenth and said one word about it and went back to his pot, and the word was not a compliment, it was the word a man uses when he is looking at something he does not have to do.

And her left hand has got eleven holds in it that hold to the end of a stroke, out of about sixty she has tried since the fourth of a month three months back, and she loses two or three in a week and writes the ones that hold on the back of a dead weight ticket with the flat of a second finger at eleven minutes a line.

She is eighteen years old and she is the best body on nine days of road and she is the slowest hand on the one stretch she is allowed to work, and both of those have been true for six weeks and this week is the first one where she has been able to hold both of them at once without something in her closing up.

## What Five Days Is

Five coppers a day and bread at the store and about five coppers in her hand on the twelfth and about five in her hand on the sixteenth.

The tin is gone since an evening in a month five months back and is not empty and is not spent and is not held. Those are the only words for it.

On the last of the five she took the stub out of the oilcloth and looked at the mark in the corner of it again, and then she put the oilcloth back over the Clay Plate, which is against her chest and which she has not taken off in four years, and she lay down on three boards with five stubs and three dead weight tickets and a ration's marks on the back of one of them.

A book on a trestle past the last stake has three empty dates in it, and every one of the three is true, and there is no hour in the world that will ever go into them, and she has asked for nothing and been refused nothing.

She is the best body on nine days and the slowest hand on the one stretch she is allowed, and there are eleven holds in the oilcloth that hold to the end of a stroke and about forty that do not, and she has five stubs with five different marks on them and not one of them can be checked against a book.

Two true books and no run in either.

And the stretch nobody is hired on is four days past the cart turn from where she sleeps.
