# Irregular

The quarterly schedule came down off the Hall board at about the seventh hour, once a quarter, because a crew reads its own schedule and that has been true every winter since before she came to it.

Nine men read it in a hall with no roof.

The hall has had no roof since before the thaw of the year before this one and is never going to get one back, and there is water standing in its low corner from a gutter that goes nowhere, and about six inches of the floor at the bottom of that corner is a different colour from the rest of it all winter.

Nine men read a word on the front of a sheet.

*Irregular.*

It was the fourth time in a year. It was in a clerk's hand, the ordinary hand, in the ordinary place, on the ordinary line, and it did not have anything to do with the amount of work in the quarter or the hours in it or whether the hours had been asked and worked, because the hours had been asked and worked and the nine men had been on the floor for them.

It said that a quarter's schedule did not stand level, and a quarter's schedule that does not stand level cannot be pressed by anybody.

That is what the word does. It is not an accusation and it is not a punishment. It is the county saying that a sheet with a gap in it is not a sheet anybody can put a claim on, and a sheet nobody can put a claim on is a sheet that the men on it cannot press.

And the winter has had no extra work in it since the thirty-first day past the last day of the thaw, because the cut ran out then, and there has been no other work inside four miles of a furnace floor since.

## Gao Doing It Again

Gao did the sum out loud at the near end with the board behind him.

He had done it once, in a hall, on the twenty-second of the month before last, and the reason he had done it then was that he had wanted nine men to have heard a number from a mouth instead of off a wall, and he had said that out loud at the time, in those words, in front of all of them.

He did it again and this time it cost him something.

“One, two, three,” said Gao. “Five, six, seven. Nine.”

He put two fingers on the board.

“Three holes in nine lines and the licensed one in the middle of five weeks of a cold end,” he said. “I did that once and I told you all at the time that I was saying it so that it would not be something read off a wall, and I have said it twice now and the second time is for her, and she is standing at the frame, so I will say what it is while she is in the room.”

Nine men stood in a hall with no roof.

“We have asked for our hours and we have worked our hours and they are on that sheet and they will not be paid out of it,” said Gao. “That is not what the word means and I am not going to stand here and explain a word to nine men.”

“It means the sheet is not a claim. It has not been a claim four times this year and it will not be a claim until a clerk on the square decides it is, and no clerk on the square is going to decide it is while the word is on the front of it.”

He put his hand down.

“That is the sum and I have said it out loud and I am not saying it a third time.”

## What She Looked At

She was standing at the frame, which is a thing Gao holds and which she had come in on her own hour to look at, and she had the sheet off the board and had it flat on the frame with her left hand on it.

Eleven minutes where it used to take four, with the flat of a second finger, and the right arm open about a third of the time, and the hall cold enough that the wet of her breath was coming down onto the back of her hand.

She was looking at the column and she was not reading it. Everyone in that hall thought she was reading it and she was not, and she was doing the thing she had come down the hill to do, which was to find out whether there was anything she could do.

## The Thing She Knows About Signing

Any man of the crew can sign for a shift. That had been true since the spring before this one and it was the reason a crew of nine ran a furnace floor of thirty without anybody ever being off it, and it was the rule she had been standing inside of for two years without ever once needing to think about it.

She thought about it for about as long as it takes to count nine.

She could not sign for nine men. She was not one of the nine. The crew signs for the crew and she was a licensed person on a schedule that was a member's sheet with an annex on it, and a member's sheet and a crew's sheet were two different objects and they were kept in two different places by two different sets of men.

And a woman who entered a shift she did not work on a crew's sheet would not be using a rule.

She had watched a man do it once, in the autumn, and she had gone cold thinking about it, and the man had been a man of the crew and had had a right to it and had done it for a man who had been sick, and even that had taken him four minutes of standing at the frame deciding.

There was nothing available to her here. That was the finding.

## The Finding

She had spent nineteen months building two things.

The first was a signature. Her name went into a stage column in the county book of this county and of three others out of nine, and it was a real hand on real paper and a county read it and a district copied it and a crate carried it up a stair past every office she had ever stood in front of, and there was not a clerk in nine counties who could strike her out of anything.

The second was eleven minutes. It took eleven minutes with the flat of a second finger where it used to take four, and she had said that out loud to nine men in this hall about six weeks ago, and they had heard it from a mouth, and Gao had put a bar down and said something about the frame that she had taken as an offer.

Neither of the two things was worth a single thing to nine men.

Nine men were owed a quarter's wages by a sheet that could not be pressed, and what she had was a hand that writes slowly and a name that four counties could look up.

She stood at the frame and worked that out. She had not known it. She had gone nineteen months carrying the two of them around inside her as a thing she was building, the way a person carries a thing they are saving for, and she had never once put them down next to the thing they were supposed to be for.

They did not fit.

Her signature is in a column that decides what a county thinks a person is. It is worth a great deal in nine counties and it is worth nothing at a frame, on a board, in a hall with no roof, to a crew that is owed a quarter and cannot press a sheet.

The only thing in that room that could pay anybody was the frame, and the frame does not pay, and the thing she owns is a column.

She was eighteen years old. She was licensed to a ceiling of nine and hunted in writing by an office she had never seen. She could walk four miles down a mountain in the deep cold on three counts and back again on three counts, and she had signed a quarterly with her own hand and got a date put on a shelf in a county office and a bar on an application she could not take back.

And she was the only person in that hall who could not pay anybody.

## What She Did About It

She did nothing.

There was nothing to do. That is the whole of what she did about it, and it took eleven minutes, and she went back to the frame afterwards and wrote the Hall's own sheet out in full for the quarter with the flat of a second finger in the ordinary hand, in a hall with no roof, in front of nobody who cared.

It took eleven minutes where it had taken four the year before.

Gao held the board and did not ask her a single question, and he did not offer again, and he had not asked her anything since and did not ask her anything then.

She put the pen down and squared the sheet and went down the hill, and there were nine men in a hall behind her being owed a quarter's work by a piece of paper they could not press, and there was nothing whatever on that floor that she was able to do about it, and she had spent six weeks believing that what she had built was for them.

She had not told them what she had built. She had told nine men at the frame about the heel of her left hand, which was a true thing about her, and there had been no point at which she had ever told them what she thought it was worth.

She went up the four miles and lay down in the second bunk with four marks on a dead card against her ribs and a sheet in a hall with no roof that could not be pressed by anybody in the world, including the woman whose signature was on it.
