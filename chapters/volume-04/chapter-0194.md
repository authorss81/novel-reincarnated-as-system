# The Fourth Bridge

She walked the first of the two days.

The Verge road out of the county is level for about four miles and then it is not. It banks up along a cut with the water down below it and a high bank on the other side, and the cut is cut stone, and the stone is laid in courses that a person can put a hand on and count, and she put a hand on them going east because her right arm was open and it wanted something to do.

She got eleven miles out of the first day.

She had been able to do that for most of a year. What was new was that eleven miles now had a shape at the end of it that it had not had before, which was nine days, and at the end of the second day the shape was eight, and she was not going to reach the far end of it by walking at eleven miles a day and she was not going to reach it at all if she did it at six and could not stop.

At about the fifth mile she caught herself doing a thing she had not done in four months, which was to put a weight on one side of a thought and see whether the other side held, and she stopped, because it was the shape of a person who has to pay somebody for the answer, and she had four coppers and a tin and a road.

She had nine days in her head and she was two days from the fourth bridge and there was a place on the road where a cart stopped, and everybody who ran that road knew where it was, because it was the kind of thing that everybody knows.

She got to the fourth bridge on the second day and stood on the wrong side of it for a while before she understood that the thing on the road was for sale.

A contractor's cart, loaded with stone going the other way, pulled in under the arch to water the near ox, and then came out again on the other side and stopped, which is not a thing a cart does unless it is going to stop.

The driver was somewhere in his middle thirties. He had a board across his knees with a load written on it and a list under the board, and when she asked him what he took he told her, and then he told her the rest of it, and she stood in the road dust and listened to all of it without interrupting once.

He was not being kind. He had a load and a schedule, and a schedule is a thing a man with a load cannot be late for, and every single thing he said was about loads and days and the places where a cart turns.

“Twenty-two coppers,” he said, “and it goes as far as the turn of the Verge where the ground gives up being flat. The stone turns back there because stone will not go where I am going.”

“And the other one?”

“The other one is fourteen, and it goes out one day, and it does not come back for you.” He looked at her boots, and then at her hands, and then at nothing. “I have turned round with a woman on that one before. You get off at the turn and you walk, and it is a different thing entirely, and I have told her so, and she takes it anyway.”

“Twenty-two.”

“Twenty-two, and I want it before we move.”

She asked him what a man got paid at the end of the road, and he gave her the day rate in coin, plainly, in the ordinary voice, because a man who will not tell you a rate is a man who wants something for telling you.

“Six,” he said. “A day. Six coppers, and a place at the fire if there is a place at the fire, and there is usually a place at the fire.”

Then he shut his mouth and went to look at his near ox, and that was the end of the trading.

## The Second Day's Ride

She got on at the bridge before the light and the cart went on past the level and onto the first of the banks with the stone load grinding behind her, and by mid-morning the driver had stopped talking altogether, which she took as a kindness and later understood as simply a man alone with a load.

She rode two days' worth of road in the first day's worth of sitting still, and being still was the expensive part.

Sitting still on a stone load with a hard edge in it is harder work than the walk had been.

Her right arm was open before the first hour was out and she had it across her chest in the second hour and she kept it there. Her left hand had no heel on it, and the tail-board had a lip along the edge, and by the middle of the second day she had found out that the way to ride two days on a tail-board with that hand was to hang it off the edge and let the lip take the weight instead of her palm.

She ate twice. She bought both of them.

That is what the tin was for and that was the whole of what it was for, and by the evening of the second day there was enough in it to buy a third meal, and she looked at the third meal for a while and then bought it and ate it too, because she was going onto a bank of high ground the next morning and a woman who starts a climb hungry gets nothing out of the climb.

## What She Did With It

She bought the second cart.

Not the first. The first was fourteen coppers for one day out and no return, which is not a seat, it is a leg, and a leg bought at fourteen would have put her at the turn of the Verge with no coin and no way to pay for the climb out of it.

The second was twenty-two and it went two days and turned at the same place, and she got her hand on the tail-board before he had finished saying that, which made him look at her once and then not look at her again.

She paid it in coin, out loud, on the bridge.

She had not done that in a long time. Somewhere in the middle of it she found out that the thing she disliked about paying a man on a bridge for a lift was not the money and had never been the money; it was that he was not going to remember her and she was going to remember him, and that was an imbalance, and it stayed an imbalance after the coin was gone.

The driver counted it into a fold of his coat without comment, because a transaction of that size does not require comment.

She had about thirty coppers and she had had about thirty coppers for a fortnight while nine men went cold, and thirty coppers had bought a seat at a bridge.

The flat Qin had put into her hand at the stone was eaten by the second evening, sitting on a tail-board with a stone load behind her and her back to it.

## The Arithmetic

She did it on the tail-board of that cart with two fingers and a splinter, in the dust, hunched, with the load shifting behind her and the driver's shoulder in front of her, and she worked it out twice because the first time she got it wrong.

Thirty coppers is five days of the pay at the end of that road.

The cart had cost her about four days of the pay at the end of that road, and had moved her two days closer to it.

**So the passage costs more per day than the work pays.**

She sat there and let that be a fact for a while.

She had spent four volumes working out that the reason she was under a ceiling of nine was that nobody inside her own county would certify an hour worked past its boundary, and she had put thirty coppers on a tail-board to go and find out whether the far end of the road was worth what it cost to reach it, and the far end of the road paid six coppers a day, and the reaching of it had cost her four.

The work out there would pay for the trip. She did not doubt that for a moment. A season's days at six coppers would come to more than thirty several times over, and she was going to have to work through a season whatever else happened, and there was no part of this that was a bad bet on the money.

What the money did not do was pay for the part before the work starts. That part had to be bought, with coin you had before you had earned anything, and she had bought it, and it was in the driver's coat.

And the road back was the same road and cost the same, and there was no arrangement she could see by which a woman who had put thirty coppers into going east also had thirty coppers put by for going west.

She wrote the reach on the back of her hand in the shorthand a carrier uses, so that she would not have to be carrying nine or eight in her head for nine days.

## What the Carrier Knew

She spent one of the day's ration on the tail-board, with the load going over the stones behind her and the sun off the water on the left.

She asked him what was at the end of the road.

He gave her an answer about a cart.

“The road goes on past the turn and up onto high ground,” he said. “There are four places on it where a cart turns. The last one turns because there is nothing past it worth an axle, and that is not a thing somebody decided. That is just where the ground stops being worth a wheel.”

He shifted his weight and looked at the near ox again.

“Everything that runs that road is either stone going out or paper coming back,” he said. “Stone is worth running for. Paper is not worth running for. Paper weighs what a leaf weighs, and I am not taking a loaded cart forty miles to bring back a leaf.”

Then he said the last of it, and he said it in the ordinary voice a man uses when he is describing a road he has run four times a season since he was nineteen.

“They will not take you past that last turn for money,” he said. “You can put a woman down on high ground at the turn and she will walk the rest, and she will be right to, and that is what everybody does.”

She did not ask him a second question.

There was no second one that would have bought anything, and she had asked about the far end once, and the answer she had got was a cart turning four times and a leaf weighing what a leaf weighs, and that was the whole of what a man on a bridge knew about nine days of road.

She had seven of the nine left in front of her and no coin left to buy another one with, and she had about four coppers and a tin.

Three on the bridge, four on the tail-board, three on the flat after, and she let it go back to three and kept it there, and she did not spend another one of the day's ration that night.
