# The Second Book

The course was closed and she was the strongest body on nine days and every part of that was worth nothing.

That is the first thing to understand about what happened on the twentieth of that month and it took her about three days to be able to say it without wanting to break something.

A course pays by the day and it pays out of a contract, and a contract is held by a man with a crew, and there is no version of a foreman's contract that carries a woman he has struck out in his own hand in the second hour of an afternoon in the open.

She is not too strong for the work. There is no such thing as too strong. There is a book, and the book does not have her in it, and a woman who is not in the book is a woman standing in the open next to a piece of work that eleven men do not need her for.

Past the last stake.

Past the last mark on the board, past the last thing anybody on that course attends, past the wire, there is about three-quarters of a mile of ground that belongs to nobody on that camp's books and to a man who has a book of his own.

He is a sub-contractor. He works the ground between the course and the far end of the reach, he is not answerable to the foreman, he has a crew of four and sometimes six, and he is not on any board at that camp.

She found him on the twentieth at about the sixth hour, at a trestle of his own about two hundred yards past the last stake, with a book open on it and a fire going under a pot at one end of it.

Four columns. Date, stretch, hours, price.

And a painted mark beside every stretch, the way a course paints its marks, and the marks are not the same marks and they are the same paint.

He was about forty-five and he was eating standing up and he did not put the book down when she came to it.

## What He Said

“Twenty-two,” he said.

She did not understand it for a moment, and then she did. It is the number he will have her by, and a man calls a hand he has just taken on by the number that goes in his book.

She had said her name. It is the name on every document she owns and she had said it twice on this ground by then, at two different trestles, and she said it again in the ordinary voice because it was the thing that made the hours go in.

“Twenty-two is thin for a hand that has been on the ring,” he said. “Five, and a place at the fire. That is what a man out here pays who is not the course.”

That was the whole of the difference and he did not explain it, and it is not explained anywhere in the world either.

Six coppers is the rate at the course. Five is the rate off it. The difference is one copper a day and there is not one thing in nine days of road that will account for it, and it is not a mystery and it is not going to become one, and a woman who stands at a trestle and asks why gets told that the course carries a contract and a stretch count and a board and nine years of a name on it and that he carries four men and a book.

## He Did Not Ask

He did not ask her why the course closed.

That is the thing she went back over most about that trestle.

He was not there. Nobody had told him. He has four men and six men and a book of his own and a stretch count that comes out of a contract that is not the course's contract, and a man who runs work out past the last stake has never in his life asked a hired hand where she came from or why she came, or what is in her head, or what her name is on a document.

She asked him, because she is the one person out here who asks things, and she asked him straight out at about the sixth hour with the bar over her shoulder: does anybody out here know why the course closed.

He said no.

And then he said: “Do not stand out there and work a stretch that is not on my book, because if it is not on my book and I have not put you on it, there is nobody who has to pay you and there is nobody who has to keep you.”

He said it the way a man says a thing about a tool.

## The Stretch Nobody Is Hired On

He named it himself, about a minute later, with his mouth full, without any particular reason for it.

“There is one out there past the cart turn,” he said. “The bad flat. It is the worst ground on nine days and it is on nobody's book and it is nobody's job, and I have got a man who will go in it if I tell him and I do not tell him, because nobody hires on it and nobody carries it and anybody who stands in it is standing in it for nothing.”

He looked at her about for the length of a mouthful.

“Do not go and be clever at it,” he said. “I have seen four people be clever at it this season.”

That was the whole of what he said about it.

She did not go on it. She did not ask him about it, then or afterwards, and she did not ask him why nobody hires on it and she did not ask him who would be responsible if somebody fell in it, and she has not asked him any of those things at any point in the two weeks that followed.

She heard it, and she put it where she puts things she is not going to use yet, and she went back to the trestle and said that she would take what he was offering.

## She Took It

She said it in daylight, at the trestle, with her name said out loud for the second time on that ground.

“Twenty-one for the twenty-first of the month.”

He wrote it in. He wrote the date and the mark of a stretch and the word of the hours in his own hand, in a book that is not the course's book, with four columns and a painted mark, and he did not ask her to confirm anything because there was nothing to confirm.

Five coppers a day and a place at the fire. And bread bought at a store in the mornings, because she is not in the fire, and that arrangement has been the arrangement for two months.

Two books in two hands.

His and the course's. Hers in both, on the same ground, within about two hundred yards of each other, in two hands that do not know each other, and neither of them answerable to the other, and no clerk on nine days of road has ever heard of either.

And the hours split.

That is what she worked out on the first morning, sitting on a cart wheel about a hundred yards off, at about the fourth hour, with a bar across her knees.

From the twenty-first of this month there are hours in the course's book and hours in his, on different days, and neither book has a run in it. A man reading either one of them gets a fortnight with a hole in the middle of it and a woman on the other side of the hole.

She knew that at about the seventh hour on the first morning and she did not do the arithmetic out loud.

The whole of the second relicensing — three legs, a clearance with no county stamp, three marks in a room at the west end, a countersignature in an office's hand read aloud out of a book she was not shown — was built out of one thing.

A continuous run of her hours, in one book, in order, with dates.

That is the instrument. That is the only instrument there has ever been for her, and she took it apart with her own mouth at a trestle in the open in the time it takes to turn a bar over, and nobody had to be persuaded and nobody argued with her.

## What It Costs

She went over it that night and it came out the same as it had come out that morning.

The hours to the seventeenth of this month stand in the course's book in a foreman's hand, in order, with dates, and a wedge of paper in a building at Stony Ford went up a stair out of them, and none of that is touched by anything she did today.

Everything after the twenty-first of this month will be in a book held by a man who is not answerable to the course, on ground that is not the course's ground, at a rate that is one copper a day less.

And the best thing about the whole of it, which is the part she turned over about nine times that night and is still turning over, is that she is being paid five coppers a day by a man who does not know what she is, for the right work, by a woman who knows exactly what she is.

She wrote that out on the back of a dead weight ticket in her own hand with the flat of a second finger and then she looked at it and did not write it again.

## The Other Two Things She Was Told

He had a fire and there were four men at it and one of them was mending a boot, and the sub-contractor talked when a man with a pot talks, which is about half as much as a man with a board talks and in the same voice.

The far end of the reach is ordinary country.

He said: “There is a woman out past the far end. Her name is Wei Ran and she has got one team and she carries to the end and back and she is twenty. She is the only person on this reach who is out on the far side of everything every week and comes back on her own account.”

He said that about the way a man says the name of a carter or the owner of a kiln. It was a fact about a place.

And then: “A carter will take a person out past the last stake for money. Not on a schedule. You go when he goes and you pay him what he asks and he will take you over the rise and set you down in the middle of a field and come back for you in the evening if he is coming back that way, and he usually is.”

He said: “It is not nothing. It is ordinary country and there are people in it and none of them are waiting for you.”

The fire that is not lit out of her share is the same fire it was at the end of a month that is now four months back, and it is five coppers a day instead of six, and the bread she buys at the store is the same bread.

She is colder than she was in the middle of that month and one copper poorer every day of the week and there is no part of that which is a wound and all of it which is a bill.

She went over the wire at the fourth hour on the morning of the twenty-first and worked a stretch on the far side of a piece of ground that a man with a book had put her on, at a rate that a sub-contractor pays, and the ground went over the top of a boot in one place and not in four, and she worked eleven hours at it.

In the oilcloth above the Clay Plate there are four stubs of paper with a contractor's mark on them that is not the mark on the new book, and about ten coppers, and a licence with a ceiling on the front of it that she has never touched.

That is what she has on the second of a month at about the seventh hour.
