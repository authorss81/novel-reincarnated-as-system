# Unpaid Ground

She went out on the fourth of a month at the fourth hour and he wrote her down before she had the bar off the wall.

That is the whole of the arrangement and it has no other shape to it. A man runs work out past the last stake and the day goes in his book at the start of the day, because a day has to be entered before the day can be worked, and she is twenty-two on that book the way she is written on the course's board, which is to say as a number a man's thumb can find.

The book is open on the plank on the end nearest the fire and it is open from the fourth hour until about the seventh, and it is squared at the end of a run, and he does not close it in between.

## The Ninth Morning

She worked nine days.

That is the fact of the first week and it is not an achievement and nobody on that ground would have called it one. She was at the trestle at the fourth hour on the fourth, the fifth, the sixth, the seventh, the eighth, the ninth, the tenth, the eleventh and the twelfth of that month, and she put her bar on the wall at the fourth hour and she took it off it at about the seventh, and the hours went in in his hand with a date on them and the mark of a stretch painted on the corner in the same paint the course uses and not the same hand.

The stretch was not hers.

That is the part that stayed with her all nine days and it is the part that made the week strange. The stretch she was on was a stretch somebody else had worked before her and would work again after her, and it had a mark on the plank and a figure against the mark and a name against the figure, and her hours went in under all three of those things as a fourth line that belonged to the book and not to the ground.

She was the slowest hand on it.

That is not a figure of speech and it is not humility. She put two feet of it up in one movement on the ring and eleven men on that course could not do it, and she had done four hundred yards of the worst of it in a month, and she was on a worked stretch at the fifth hour every day for nine days and she could not keep pace with a man of about thirty with a bad shoulder.

## What A Grip Costs

The order is gone out of her left hand.

She said it out loud once, on the eighteenth of a month two months back, at a dressed stone in a county nine days west, in front of nine men, and it was cheaper than being asked. Twenty-six days of that month had gone into building what is left of it, and what is left of it is a bar and a boot and a body, and every grip she makes now is made from nothing.

Nothing is the wrong word for what it costs. A hold at the middle of a bar and a hold at the end of it is two holds, and between them the bar is only worth what the weight of it says.

So she builds them one at a time.

She keeps the count on the back of a dead weight ticket in her own hand with the flat of a second finger, which takes about eleven minutes for the line, and the first four days she wrote four holds and lost two of them before the seventh hour and did not write the lost ones down.

By the ninth morning she had eleven that held to the end of a stroke and she had lost about forty, and none of the forty were in the ticket because there was nothing to be gained by a column of things that did not hold.

The hand is not the only cost and it is not the largest one. What the hand does is make her slow, and being slow on a worked stretch in the fifth hour is a thing eleven men and a girl of nineteen can see from two hundred yards.

## Two Books

She did the arithmetic on the second morning and it came out the same as it had come out on the first.

There are hours in the course's book in a foreman's hand from the fourth of the month before last to the seventeenth of this one, in order, with dates on them, and a wedge of paper in a building at Stony Ford went up a stair out of them, and none of that is touched by anything happening on this ground.

And there are hours in this man's book from the twenty-first of the month before, in order, with dates on them, and they go into the same reach and the same season and the same nine days of road.

And a man reading either one of them gets a stretch with a hole in it, and a woman on the other side of the hole.

That is the whole instrument. There is no other instrument in the world and there has not been one since the twentieth of a month last year, when she paid for a second relicensing with it out loud, at a counter, in daylight, in front of a man who asked her nothing.

She had taken it apart herself on purpose, eleven days before, at this trestle, in under the length of a bar being turned over.

She did not do the arithmetic out loud.

## The Man Mending A Boot

There is one man on that trestle who talks, and he is not the sub-contractor.

He is about nineteen or twenty and he has a boot off his foot and a needle and about an inch of thread, and he works at it at the near end of the plank with his knee out, and he has been doing it for four days and it is coming apart again every evening.

“You are the one that came off the thirty-nine,” he said, on the sixth.

“I am.”

“Before it was closed.”

“It was.”

He pulled the thread and thought about it.

“There is a difference in the ground at the far end of it,” he said. “Nearer where the bar stands. It is thin on the top and it is thick underneath and when you go in past the boot it is like putting a bar into a wall that has been built.”

“Yes.”

“Not like this.” He turned the boot over. “This is like putting a bar into a bucket of something wet and it goes down and it comes up and you are pulling on the whole of it.”

“That is the difference.”

“Then I will not be working that end again.”

He said it the way a man says a thing about a barrow, and went back to the boot, and did not ask her anything about where she had come from or what was on that course, and there was not one moment in nine days when she wanted to say anything to him about either.

At the seventh hour on the ninth day the boot came apart again, and he looked at it, and said, “It wants doing twice,” and put it away.

## What Five Is

She had ten coppers and she earned five a day and she spent five a day.

That is all it says and it needs no explaining anywhere. There is bread at a store at the turn and the bread is five coppers and she has no share in the cook-fire under the pot at the end of the plank, because a man who pays five a day to a hand he does not carry feeds her about a foot away from his own pot at the seventh hour and that is the whole of what either of them gets out of it.

She was colder than she had been at any point in the winter that is two months behind her, and she is not hungry, and has not been hungry since the first of the Foundation stages, and cold at a fire and out of it, and there is no part of that which is a price she chose to pay.

It is weather. It is a base underneath her that runs cold the way a stone runs cold, and she has put it there herself and it is working exactly as a thing works when it is working.

On the eighth evening she stood at the store counter with five coppers on it and about five coppers in her hand and worked out that she had been doing arithmetic at a bread counter for about two minutes and a half, which is longer than it takes to buy bread.

## The Ration

She spent one of it on the tenth, at about the fifth hour, sitting on a stone past the trestle with the bar across her knees.

It bought her nothing.

She did not find out what it bought. She let it go the way a person lets go a thing they did not need and are not going to look at, because he has been quieter for about three months than he was and there is about as much of him as there was at the start of the season and a little less, and she is not going to keep him up out of a habit that four years old.

The count on the back of the ticket is four marks and it has been four marks for some days and she is the only person in the world who is keeping it.

## Past The Cart Turn

On the twelfth, at about the seventh hour, when he was squaring the book, he named it again.

He does that. He has a stretch count and a book and a fire and he names what is past his ground the way a man names a neighbour's field, without any particular reason for it, and the first time was eleven days ago and he did not warn her twice and he did not warn her once this time either.

“There is the bad flat past the cart turn,” he said. “Same as it was. You have not got to be told about it twice.”

“Yes.”

“You have not been near it.”

“No.”

He looked at her for about the length of a mouthful, which is a thing he does, and then he said, “Do not go and be clever at it,” and squared the book and put the pen behind his ear.

Past the ground she stands on every morning, past the cart turn, past the last thing on that trestle's plank, there is three-quarters of a mile of the worst ground on nine days, and it is on nobody's book and it is nobody's job, and everybody who has ever named it has named it in the ordinary way and then told her not to be clever at it.

She had not asked him a single thing about it in nine days and she did not ask him one now, and she stood at the trestle about four minutes later with her bar on her shoulder looking at the rise beyond the ground she works and did not walk over it.

Nine days' work in two books in two hands, and not one run in either of them, and ten coppers that turn over without building, and eleven holds that hold to the end of a stroke out of about fifty she had tried.

And past the cart turn, where the drift is thick on the low side because that is where the work was worst, the ground nobody hires on.

She has seen it from two hundred yards every evening for nine days and she has not put a bar in it and she has not asked what it is worth.

She wanted to. That is the honest part of it, and she wrote it out on the back of a dead weight ticket in her own hand with the flat of a second finger, took eleven minutes over the line, and did not write it again.
