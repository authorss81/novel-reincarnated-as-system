# Hands In Two Books

The first of a month is the day a man's book is worth something and the fourth is the day it is finished.

She knew that in the ordinary way, having watched it four times since she came out here, and on the first she stood at the near end of the plank at about the seventh hour with her bread in her hand and watched him do it.

He turned the page back to the first of the month. He ran his thumb down the date column the whole way without stopping, which is a thing a man does to check that nothing has been torn out, and then he ran it down the hours column, and then he squared the page against the plank with the heel of his hand and put the pen behind his ear.

Three empty lines went past his thumb in the middle of it.

He did not stop on them and he had not stopped on them at the end of a run in the middle of the month before, and a hole you cannot see is worse than one you can.

## On The Plank

She took the oilcloth out on the second, in the hut, and laid it out on the plank in daylight because a thing that is going to be checked is checked in the daylight.

Five counterfoils. Four with one contractor's mark and one with a different mark in a different paint from a man who is not answerable to the man who struck her name out of a book, and the five dates on them ran clean from the twelfth of the month before to the sixteenth of this one with nothing skipped anywhere.

Three dead weight tickets, two of them full, and on the back of the third one, in her own hand with the flat of a second finger, the count of what she had and how much of it there was.

She checked four things against four things and she took about eleven minutes a line with the flat of a second finger, which is a line she has never once been able to do in four minutes and is not going to be able to do in nine days' road.

Date against date. Stretch against stretch. Hours against hours. Price against price.

Then she did it the other way, which took longer, and which is the only way that counts.

## What A Clean Split Comes To

Every hour she has worked since the seventeenth of a month two months back is in one book or the other. Not one is in both. Not one is in neither.

That is the best split a person can have and it is worth nothing.

She wrote that out on the back of the third ticket, in her own hand, and took eleven minutes over it, and it came to these words: *nothing is in two books. nothing is in none.*

Then she wrote the second line underneath it, which took another eleven minutes, and it came to: *a run is one hand in order with dates.*

Then she stopped, because there was nothing else to write and she had known there would not be, and because a thing written twice in the same hand on the same ticket in the same hour is a thing a person is arguing with herself about.

The whole of the instrument her relicensing was built out of is a continuous run, and a continuous run is one book in one hand with dates on it, and she had put the run in two places on purpose at this trestle eleven days after she had paid for it, at a counter, in daylight, in front of a man who asked her nothing.

She did not do the arithmetic out loud. She did it on the plank with the flat of a second finger and it came out the way it had come out every time since the seventeenth of that month.

## What She Did Not Ask

There were three things she could have asked for and she asked for none of them, and it took her about four minutes to work out that she was not going to.

She could have asked the sub-contractor to copy the hours out of the course's book into the back of his own, so that there was one hand holding the whole of it. He would have done it if she had asked him in the ordinary way, because a man who runs stone out past the last stake has had men ask him for worse things, and because the course is not his business and he does not know why the course closed and has never once asked.

She could have asked the course. It is nine hundred yards inside the wire and closed against her and she has not been inside it since the seventeenth of a month two months back, and asking would have meant standing in front of eleven idle men and a foreman who has already said in the plainest words he has got that he would do the whole of it again, and explaining what she wanted a copy of, and there is not one version of that conversation where a man takes his thumb off a page.

And she could have gone back to the county. There is no date west on any board past the last stake for the rest of the season, and the strip with her name on it went out on the sixteenth in the inside of a coat about sixty years old, and the county does not know where she is and has not asked.

She said the first one out loud, once, on the second, at the plank, in the wind, to nobody who had been asked for anything.

“Copy the hours out of the course's book into the back of yours,” she said, “and there is one hand holding the whole of it.”

It was the right thing to ask. He came and got a pot about nine seconds later and said what he says.

“Twenty-two,” he said. “Fifth of the month. Same as the rest.”

“I did not ask you for a day.”

“You did not ask me for a day,” he said, and put the pot on.

She did not ask him again, on the second or the third or the fourth, and a decent man does not offer a thing twice, and the door shut with both of them standing at the plank.

On the third he told her one thing about the course, unasked, which is the only thing he has ever said about the course in a month.

“The man over the wire keeps a copy of everything he sends,” he said. “He does not keep a copy of what he keeps. If you ever wanted a thing out of that book you would have to be a man who sent something.”

That was the whole of it. She had not asked him for it and he did not say it again, and it shut the third of the three doors at the same plank where she had shut the other two herself.

All three were open on the second of the month at about the seventh hour and all three were shut before she got off the plank, and no man in the world refused her anything.

## The Calm

The strange part was the calm.

She had been cold for about six weeks — cold at the fire and out of it, cold at four counts in a body with a base underneath it that runs cold the way a stone runs cold — and on the third and the fourth she worked four hours a day on somebody else's stretch with the wind off the rise and she was not cold in the way she had been cold.

That is the whole of what it was and it is worth more than the four hours.

She was not calm because anything had got better. The attendance had gone west with a foot and a half of blank paper in it and it was never coming back. The three empty dates were in a book at a trestle and were going to stay empty. The order was gone out of her left hand and eleven holds out of about sixty, and every part of her that had gone wrong since the thaw had gone wrong in that hand.

She was calm because she had stopped.

For four years she had been carrying the idea that if she could only get the paper right the rest of it would follow, and she has stood in six rooms in six counties with a schedule and a licence and a wedge of paper and a request, and every one of them was the right paper and not one of them was the answer.

The run was the instrument. Two books cannot make one.

And there is one place within nine days of road where a book is not read by a clerk from a county and not kept by a man who is answerable to a foreman, and where an hour is written down by the hand that worked it and kept by the people whose ground it is, and where nobody has ever once been refused anything because they asked well.

She had been inside that place on one knee.

## The Fourth

She put the ticket back in the oilcloth above the Clay Plate with the ration's marks on the other side of it, and put the Clay Plate back against her chest where it has been for four years, and rolled the oilcloth and put it inside her coat against the five counterfoils.

She worked the fourth at four hours and went to the bread counter at the seventh and gave five coppers for bread and had five in her hand on the fourth and had five in her hand on the first.

And on the fourth evening she sat on three boards past the last stake with the bar against the wall and did the thing she had been not doing for about a month.

She did not ask anybody for anything.

She had a quarter of a mile of ground past the cart turn and it was on nobody's book and no hour of it would ever go into a wage-book in this country, and she was going out onto it at the fourth hour of the fifth, in daylight, with her name said out loud, and the one thing she had wanted since the seventeenth of a month two months back — that the hours would go into a book in one hand with dates on them — was not going to happen on that ground or on any other ground within nine days of road.

What was going to happen is that somebody out past the end of the working reach would see her standing on it.

That was the whole of it, and she went to sleep on it, and it was the first four days in six weeks that she did not lie there working out what she could have said differently.
