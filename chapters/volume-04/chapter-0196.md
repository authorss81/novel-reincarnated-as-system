# The Field Agent

The high ground came down into somebody's country about two days out and the change was so gradual that she did not find it.

There was no gate. There was no stone and no post and no clerk and no notice, and the county's boundary went by in the middle of an afternoon between two fields, the way a boundary goes by when nobody has put a mark on it because nobody needs to.

She noticed it about a mile after the fact, standing on a rise, and worked out what she had noticed, and then deliberately did not think about it any further.

The difference was not a line on the ground. The difference was everything else.

**Lamps.**

The first hamlet she came to on high ground had four lit windows at the second hour of the last of the light and there was smoke out of one of them and a woman hanging cloth on a line, and somewhere behind the hamlet a dog was going, and it was a small ordinary sound and it went on for a while after she passed.

A county that is inside a bound does that at this hour, but she had not seen it from the outside in four years. Every window she had looked at since the eighth stage of her life had been a window with a light in it that somebody had put there because a rule said to put it there.

These were just lit. That was all. Somebody had put wood in a thing to keep it going, and it was going because it was wanted to be going, and nothing about it was a rule.

There were fields. Not strips; fields, ploughed, with a ditch at the edge of them and a gate into each, and the ditches all ran the same way, and somebody had spent four hundred years making the water agree with itself.

There was a child on a step who watched her go past without any interest whatever, and a dog that did not even get up.

## What A Country Does To A Road

The other thing about it was the road.

She had spent nine days on it and she could have told anybody who asked that the road was the same road the whole way, and she would have been wrong, and she noticed it on the second evening because a wheel-rut had a different weed in it.

Inside the boundary the Verge road was a county road. It was maintained, and what that meant was that it was cleared at intervals by men who were paid out of something, and the verges had been cut back because a rule said they would be.

On high ground it was not maintained by anybody. It was simply used. It had ruts in it in the places where ruts happen when four hundred years of carts go the same way, and between the ruts there was grass, and in the grass there were weeds that only grow where wheels do not go, and one of those weeds came up through the middle of the rut and had got about nine inches tall.

Somebody had dug a ditch at the edge of the second field and then had put a stone in the ditch every twenty yards with lime mortar, which no county does and no rule requires and which was there because a woman whose name nobody in that country knew had wanted the ditch to hold in a wet year.

She walked about a mile of somebody's care and understood that she had arrived.

## Nine Days Without A Word

Not one person on the high ground said a word to her, and it took her until the middle of the second evening to work out how strange that was.

Nine days. Every mile of it. A woman walking alone on a road with a bag, going somewhere, nine days out, in a country where nobody knew her face.

In her own county that is not a thing that happens twice. At the fourth mile somebody has said something to somebody. By the second day there is a version of her at a trough and a version of her at a frame and a version of her in a lane, and by the fourth day there is a version of her at a counter with the wrong thing said about her in its margin.

Here, nothing. Because here there was no clerk and no frame and no trough and no counter, and nobody had ever been asked to write a licensed one down, and there was nothing on that ground that would ever put her on a page.

She was standing on a rise outside a hamlet at the second hour of a still evening and she found that she had walked nine days into a country where nobody was waiting for her and had not once been frightened of it, and that this was the first thing since the winter that had not cost her anything to get.

Then she thought about why, once, in one sentence, and left it there.

The things that had happened inside the boundary had gone out of it, all of them, every one, in nine years, to every county at once, and everybody inside had heard all of them, and nobody out here had ever heard one of them, because out here nothing is announced.

She did not turn that into a theory. She stood on a rise in the dark and let it be the shape of a place and went down to sleep.

## The Man In The Plain Coat

He was ahead of her on the road when she came round a bend at the end of that same evening, and he was walking the way she was walking, and he had been walking it a long time, because there was no cart on that road and no ox and no light except what a man carries, and he was about a mile ahead when she saw him and about the same distance ahead when she passed the place where the road bent and he was not in it.

He came the other way at about the fourth hour of the next morning.

Plain coat, good boots with the mud off them, a notebook under one arm, and a face she had seen twice a month at a counter on a square in a town nine days behind her.

He did not stop because of her. He stopped because there was a bend and he was on his own side of it, and then he stopped because it was her, and he came up the bank out of the road, and he stood about four feet off with his notebook under his arm.

He had been walking east ahead of a road he was under no instruction to walk, and they both knew it, and neither of them said it.

“Bai Zhu,” she said, because the name was in a Warden's book and in a county book and she had not said it out loud in four months and there was no reason to be coy about it in a lane.

He took it the way a man takes a fact he has already written down twice.

“I have not asked you a question,” he said, “and I am not going to start, and I want you to have that in the same breath as the rest of it so that you do not have to spend a morning on it.”

That was the whole of his opening. It was the same sentence in the same order that he had said in a hall with no roof and on a step in a lane and in a market, and she had heard it four or five times now and it had never once been anything other than what it was.

## One True Thing

She spent one of the day's ration there, standing in a lane on high ground with a dog barking a long way off, and it bought the truest thing he had said to her in four volumes.

She asked him what he was doing on this road.

“I am following a referral,” he said. “It went up a stair in your county and it is in a room I cannot go into, and the woman it is about is nine days east on a road, and I have walked out here on my own money with my own boots because the thing I am sent to do cannot be done standing in a room.”

He said all of that in the ordinary voice and did not complain about it.

Then he said the thing she had not asked for.

“When you get there,” he said, “the wages are low and the lodging is worse and nobody will ask you anything.”

He looked at the notebook and did not open it.

“That is not a courtesy. That is what a place is like when the pay is by the day and the paper is thin. You will be able to do more there than you have been able to do anywhere, and the reason is that nobody there is going to want anything off you that is not your hours, and you have not been in a place like that since you were a child.”

He shut the notebook against his side.

“I have no business telling you that. I have never had any business in your case and I do not have it now, and I am telling you because you are going to walk into it in a day and a half and a woman ought to know what a place is before she stands in it.”

She did not thank him.

She had it in her mouth and she did not say it, because thanking a man for a thing he did in the course of his own work is a way of turning his work into a kindness to her, and it was not a kindness to her, it was a field man standing in front of a woman he could not yet touch being decent about the shape of the next four months, and she was not going to hand him the other thing.

What she said was: “You will be here on the course when I am.”

And he said: “I will be on the course when you are,” in the same voice, and neither of them said what that meant or whether it was true, and then he walked on up the road and she let him get a hundred yards ahead of her because she was not going to walk behind a man she had spent a season out of reach of.

She spent the afternoon on the high ground above the hamlet and went down the far side of it at the last of the light, and the far side had a valley in it with a line of trees along the bottom and a road that followed them, and she could see, about two hours on, where it came out on flat ground, and where there were shapes along it that were not fields.

## The Morning After

She saw him twice more before the course, and both times he was doing exactly what he had said he was doing.

The first was at a low stone wall where he had sat down and had his coat off and a tin cup beside him, and he did not greet her, and he did not stand up, and he wrote three lines in the notebook while she went past, and she did not slow down.

The second was on the track a day later where two men were burning an old hedge and the wind had brought the smoke across, and he was standing upwind of it with his notebook shut, watching two strangers at work, and when she came up he turned his head about a quarter of the way and went back to watching them.

He was not waiting for her and he was not keeping her and he was not collecting her.

He had a case with a woman's name and her stages and her hours in it, and he could not do one single thing with any of that on this road, and he had walked out here anyway on his own boots, and he was buying nothing and he was not pretending to be friendly about it.

At about the middle of that second day she worked out why he was taking the trouble, and the answer was not in her own head, it was in his coat, in the fact that a man who had been wrong about one thing in four volumes would rather be standing where the woman was than be somewhere else with a report.

He was there because he did not want to be able to say afterwards that he had not come.

That was a professional thing and it was the first thing anybody had done for her in a year that was not about her, and she had been without one for about eleven months and did not find out that she had been without it until that evening on a track with smoke coming across it.
