# The Course

She came into the camp at about the fourth hour of the afternoon on the ninth day and nobody met her.

There was a fire, and a kettle on a bar over it, and eleven men and a foreman of about fifty and a girl of nineteen, and a man mending the heel of a boot with a piece of leather and a length of wood, and a trestle with a book lying closed on it, and about forty feet of wire strung between two stakes at the back.

All of that was going on when she came in and all of it went on.

The man with the boot looked up once, the way you look up at anything that moves, and then went back to the boot. The girl at the fire moved a pot a half-inch so that the handle was not over the flame, and that was for the pot and not for her.

She stood at the edge of the camp with her bag in her hand for the better part of a day.

## Not A Test

The first thing she wanted, on arriving, was to be asked what she wanted.

She had a whole string ready. She had had it ready since about the sixth day, on the flat after the fourth bridge, where there had been a hamlet and a woman hanging cloth and a child who had not looked up.

The string was the thing you say to a man with a book when you want to be put on it, and it was a good string, and it was not one word of it long, because she had learned in four volumes that a long speech in a room is a thing a person makes when they are frightened of the room.

She did not get to use it. The question never got made.

She worked out over the rest of that first evening and most of the second day that this was not a test and it was not hostility and it was not a courtesy.

A place where people are paid by the day does not care who turns up in the day. That is the whole mechanism. The day comes, and the men who are on the book are on it, and the man with the book writes the day against the men who are on it, and a person standing at the edge of it with a bag in her hand is not a fact that has entered the arithmetic.

She had spent four volumes being a fact that entered the arithmetic of rooms. She had never once in her life stood in a place where nothing she was doing had entered anything.

By the second evening she had about five coppers and no bed and no way to be useful to anybody, and she had said nothing to anybody, and she had been hungry once that day and had not had anything to buy with.

## What Is Called The Course

She asked about the wire, which was the only question she asked in two days, and a man with a broken thumbnail told her, without stopping what he was doing, that the wire was for the ground inside it and that a person not on the book went round it.

She asked him what the work was called.

He said: “The course.”

And then he said, because she had a notebook out and he thought she was writing something down for somebody, “That is what we call the job. There is no other name for it in this camp and if you ask in Lowwater they will tell you different, and what they tell you is a word and not a job.”

It was a work site inside the camp. There was cutting in it and stakes and a man working a bar with a long handle, and she did not get inside the wire and did not see the shape of it properly and was not going to, because not being on the book was a rule and she was going to respect the rule until the book had her on it.

## Two Days At The Edge

The second day she watched the whole of the work being done from outside the wire and counted the shape of it without being able to name what she was looking at.

That was a thing she could do and it was worth nothing and she had not had occasion to use it in a long time.

Ten of the eleven men came out through the wire and went to a place at the far end of the camp where there were two things that were not camp, and they worked those two things in turn for about an hour, and then they came back through the wire and went to the ground inside it, and the one who did not go in was the girl, who sat on a stone at the wire and did a thing to a length of something with her hands.

She could have lifted any one of the eleven out of the ground with the arm that worked. She had carried a hundred and seventy quarry loads in thirty-four days at a rate four of them could not have held at a walk, and she was standing outside a wire with a bag in her hand watching a man of about thirty work a bar with a long handle for four hours.

It is not strength. That is the thing nobody tells a person who has got strength, and she had had strength for about four months and had had it turned into exactly one thing in all of that time, which was carrying.

Nobody on that course needed carrying done. The job was the other thing entirely.

## What The Camp Did In The Evenings

In the evenings the men sat on the ground in a ring on the side of the fire away from the wind and did nothing at all.

That was the thing that nearly finished her. In four years she had never once been in a room where a set of men sat in a ring on the ground and did nothing, and it turned out that the difficulty was not the work and was not the wire and was not about five coppers and no bed.

It was that nobody was working.

She had been in rooms. She had been in a furnace hall with a roof off, and a tower with a stair, and a square with a queue, and a counting lane, and a house with a wall in it. In every one of them, at every hour, every single person was doing something, and what they were doing was about her, and she could read the doing.

Eleven men sat on the ground for two hours a night and mended things and ate and argued about nothing.

The arguing was the worst of it. It was not about her and it was not about money and it was not about the ground inside the wire. It was about a man called something she did not catch, and whether he had done a thing on a stretch that had a number, and the answer given across the fire at some length, and then the subject dropped.

She sat with her back against the outside of a cart wheel about six feet from the nearest of them and listened to about nine hundred words of the inside of a place where nothing was wanted from her, and it was the first evening in four years that she was not being spoken to, and she was so tired of being spoken to that the tiredness of it came out of her shoulders for the first time in about a year.

Nobody came over and said anything to her. Nobody brought her anything. Two of them looked at her once, in daylight, and looked away.

She did not thank anybody for any of it, because there had been nothing to thank, and that was correct.

The foreman was a broad man with a foreman's authority sitting on him as heavily as a coat, and he walked the wire twice in the two days, and both times he looked at her and both times he did not stop.

## What She Felt

It was humiliation.

It was specific and it was not about the book and it was not about the wire. It was the knowledge, standing at the edge of a place where eleven men were paid by the day to do the hardest work she had seen in four years, that she had nothing whatever that any of them wanted, and that the reason was not her fault and was not their fault either, and that the place was correct about it.

And underneath the humiliation, about a hand's depth under it, the thing she would not admit to herself that afternoon, which was that she would have been exactly the same woman in a county office.

She would have been the only one in that room who could not pay anybody else, standing at a counter with an open counter and a clerk's pen and a form, and every single person above her and around her would have wanted a thing off her, and she would have given them one of them, because she always had.

This was the first place she had stood in where nobody wanted one thing from her.

She did not enjoy finding that out. She stood at a stone outside a wire for two days and let it happen and did not turn it into anything.

The trestle opened on the second morning. The book came off it and a man came and put eleven days into a column and squared the page and went away, and the whole of it took about four minutes, and not one word of it was said in her direction.

She got up on the third morning before the light with about five coppers and a bake that was nearly gone, and went and stood behind the fire, and waited to be asked.
