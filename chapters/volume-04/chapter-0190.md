# A Bed of Ash

She went down the hill at about the sixth hour on the morning after the attendance with about thirty coppers in the tin and she did not buy the stone.

That was the whole of it. It took about ninety seconds at a board behind a well in daylight with the man who sells it standing on the other side of it, and she did not bargain and she did not say why.

“Nine,” said Da Shan.

“No today.”

He looked at her for about as long as it takes a man to put a chalk in his hand and not use it.

Then he put the chalk down.

## The Arithmetic Of A Board

He came round the side of the board and stood with his back against the wall, which is a thing a man does at a board behind a well when he has decided he is going to stay a while.

“Nine years I have had this wall,” he said. “I have never once had a buyer stand at it and say not today.”

“I have had buyers not come. I have had buyers come and count the money and go. I have had a buyer in the summer of the year before last tell me the price was wrong and then buy at my price two days later and tell a market he had beaten me.”

“Not today is a different thing and I am going to say what I think about it and then I am going to go back to my wall.”

He put his thumb on the chalk line along the top.

“Four loads a week is nine coppers a load and four a week is thirty-six a week,” said Da Shan. “I have had four a week off you since the middle of the month and that is the only thing on this board that has moved in a winter. The eight frames are nineteen years. The two that came off and came back are mine and they cost me a fortnight and I have not told anybody what a fortnight costs.”

He took his thumb off the wall.

“You stopping is not a hardship to me. I want that understood before anything else. I have a wall that has not moved in four years and I have made my arrangements about it and I will make the same arrangements about this. I have no widow and no debts and I have eleven other customers and nine of them are dead or too old to lift and I will be selling to two of them by the thaw.”

“Then why are you standing at your wall talking to me.”

“Because in nineteen years a person does not stop at a wall. It is the one thing I know how to do and you have been the only person in four years who came to it, and you are not going to be able to make that back up to me by standing on the other side of the street all winter.” He put the chalk in his hand. “That is the true thing about it and it is nothing to do with you.”

## The Thirty Coppers

She had counted it on the day she read the notice and had been spending it at the rate of four loads a week ever since, and on this morning there were about thirty left.

Thirty coppers is about six nights of stone at four loads a week.

That is not a small thing in a room with ten people in it in the third month of the cold. It is six nights of the fire not going down twice, and the board in the window has been in that window since before she came and has a gap under it you can put two fingers through and feel the outside on, and there is nothing else about that room that is warm.

She stood at the board behind a well with thirty coppers in a tin under her coat and worked out, out loud, in the ordinary voice, to a man who had not asked, the thing she had been not saying.

“The last of it would have carried the fire to the end of this month,” she said. “Six more nights of it and then eight nights of ash, and the road opens on the eighth of the next month, and I would be at nothing on the morning it opens.”

“Yes,” said Da Shan. “And you are going to keep it.”

“I am going to keep it.”

“For nine days of road.”

“For nine days of road,” said Ning Yan, “or for whatever the road is, which is probably longer than nine days because I have not asked anybody how long it is.”

He did not say anything for a moment, and neither did the man in her head, and she had spent four marks on a day and two of them had gone on nothing at all, and she had not minded, and she noticed that she had not minded, and she put that on the list of things she was not going to look at again this month.

“Nine days is a carrier’s figure for the pass with the sledge loaded and the going good,” he said. “You are not going with a sledge.”

“No.”

“Then you should ask a carrier,” said Da Shan, “and you should ask him before you spend the thirty and not after, because the answer does not change what is in your hand but it changes what a man in my trade will tell you about the ground.”

She had four coppers of it left that were not in the tin.

She put them on the board.

“No,” she said.

## No Accident

Nothing happened to anybody.

Put down first, plainly, because the cold in these four nights is the sort of thing that gets turned into a body, and no body is what happened here.

No man was hurt. No man was ill. Nothing was broken and nothing was lost that had a name on it.

The fire in the crew quarters went down twice between the fourth hour and the light on the first night, and twice on the second, and twice on the third and twice on the fourth. She fed it at the fourth hour and at about two hours before the light with what was in the bucket, and the bucket was the same bucket it has always been.

Nine men slept in twelve bunks for four nights in the third month of the cold in a room that goes down twice a night anyway.

Somebody coughed on the second night. Somebody has coughed since the autumn.

## The Thing She Had Done To Nine Men

The thing she had done to nine men was this.

There is a fire in that room on those four nights that would have been there, and there was not going to be one, because a woman stood at a board behind a well at about the sixth hour that morning with thirty coppers in a tin and did not buy four loads a week.

And every one of the nine knows what she is.

They know she is a licensed member of this county with a ceiling of nine and seven conditions on the back of it. They know a certificate went through that building in the middle of the winter and that a man certified in a Warden's hand that the thing in her head is real and unauthorised and carried by a person in good standing, and they know it went up a stair past every office she has ever stood in front of.

They know she is hunted in writing, and they know she told nine men out loud about the heel of her hand in a hall with no roof, and they know there is a hole where the fourth stage should be and another where the eighth should be and that a county wrote both of them down in four words three times and stopped.

They know she is eighteen.

And not one of them has ever asked her for anything, and not one of them has ever asked her for a single thing in two winters, and she has been carrying nine men around inside her head as a thing she was building something for.

And in the morning she went down a hill and made nine men cold for four nights on purpose, at a board, in daylight, over about thirty coppers, for a road.

There is nowhere to put that.

## The One Who Noticed

Qin noticed.

He noticed on the second morning, at about the seventh hour, when nine men came in off the shift and the room was the temperature it had been for two nights instead of the temperature it had been for two winters, and he looked at the bucket and then at the fire and then at her.

She was standing at the frame with the slate.

He did not ask her.

He did not ask her the way a man asks a question. He did not say anything at all, and he went and got a second bucket from the corner and filled it himself from the butt outside and put it by the stove and came back and sat down on the end of the second bunk, which is his bunk, and looked at his boots.

That is all he did.

She stood at the frame and held a bar and did not look at him, and the whole of the rest of that morning was the sound of a man not saying a thing he had decided not to say, and it went on for about four hours.

It was worse than if he had asked.

That is the part she has not been able to put down since. Nine men read a nine and three holes in a column and the word on the front of their own schedule and a word said out loud about a hand, and none of them said anything, and she had priced that silence at about the cheapest thing anybody had ever done for her and she had been right about that.

A man who notices a fire has gone out and fills a bucket himself and says nothing is a different silence entirely, and she had no idea what it was worth, and she had been the one who made it.

## The Eleventh Day Off

On the fourth of those nights, at about the second hour in the morning, Da Shan's boy came down the lane with a lamp and a message from a board behind a well, which was that the county had had a carrier in at the first hour and the carrier had said what everybody who works a pass says in the third month of the cold.

The thaw is eleven days off.

Eleven days off on the fourth of those nights, which is the eighth of the next month.

The notice on the shelf by the door of the county office says the season's road east out of this county reopens on the eighth of the ninth month, and a carrier with a lamp has said that the thaw comes on about that day, and the two of those are the same day and have always been the same day and a county puts a date on a road because a county puts dates on things.

Eleven days.

She stood in a cold room with a fire that had gone down twice and had not been fed since the fourth hour because there was nothing left in the bucket to feed it with, and she worked out that she had four nights in front of her of it, and then ten more after those, and the road.

She had about thirty coppers in a tin.

She did not buy the stone.
