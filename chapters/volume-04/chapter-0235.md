# What Merit Settles

Fourteen days.

That is the whole of it and it is not a figure anybody would have believed in a month.

A woman of eighteen, at the eighth stage of a realm she has no paper for, with the order gone out of her left hand and four counts of breath on the flat and in a grade and in a foot and a bit of quiet ground, took three-quarters of a mile past the cart turn to the end, alone, by hand, with a bar, in fourteen days.

The sub-contractor did not know she had started. He did not stand a stretch on it, and there was nothing to stand.

Fourteen days is eight more than it should take and it is because of the breath.

## The Rules Of That Ground

She worked it by hand and by day and she worked it in the ordinary way, which is that you go in at about the fifth hour, because the low side is soft on a morning after a night of wind and it holds a weight wrong until the sun has been on it for about an hour, and you are out by the seventh because there is nothing out there and a body at four counts does not know how far it has come.

You build a hold before the bar lands. She built one about forty times an hour for fourteen days and she lost about eleven a day and kept the rest and wrote the ones that held on the back of a dead weight ticket with the flat of a second finger at about eleven minutes a line, once in the evening, in a hut with three boards in it.

A bar into the low side goes down about a foot and stops, and a boot does not tell you which way it has moved while your weight was on it, and there is no ring anywhere in it, and that is the whole of what makes it dangerous.

The ground answers every turn of a boot the same answer.

## The Fifteenth

On the fifteenth she went in.

It was about the sixth hour and she had been on the low side for about two hours and she put the bar in at a spot she had put it in nine or ten times that fortnight, and it went down a foot and a bit instead of a foot, and she had her weight over it.

She was in to about the hip before she had decided to be and she was in it for about as long as it takes to count eleven and the bottom of it came out from under her and put her on her back in six inches of something that was not mud and was not water.

She lay there.

There was nobody within a day and a half's walk of her, in a country where two men have to be found by walking, and the drift on the low side is a foot and a bit all the way and it is quiet.

She got up in about the time it takes a person to get up and she got up the hard way, on her hands first, and she got her bar, and she did not go into that spot again for three days, and she worked the two days either side of it a hundred yards short of where she had been.

There is no version of this where she was rescued and there was no version of this where a man stood a stretch. There is a woman in a foot and a bit of quiet ground, on her back, with nothing coming back through the bottom of it.

She did not fall on the bar and her arm was not open and nothing broke in her that she has not already had back once.

That is what it is on that ground, and it is the only shape it takes: it is work, and the ground does not care who is standing on it.

## The Last Mark

The twenty-first.

She came onto the last quarter at about the fifth hour and worked it to the end, which is a rise of about four feet and the last hundred yards of the whole thing, and at about the sixth hour she stood at the far end of it with a bar in her right hand and about six inches of lime in a cloth at her belt that she had had since a morning in a month that is six months back.

She laid the mark.

A course paints its stretches. A sub-contractor paints his. There is no paint out past the cart turn, so she mixed the lime with a little of the water that stands in the cut and laid it on with the flat of the bar end in a mark about the width of a hand, and she put a second one at the foot of the rise, the way a run is finished, and she stood back about nine feet and looked at it.

Then she wrote it on the back of the dead weight ticket with the flat of a second finger, one line, eleven minutes: *the twenty-first, the end of it, marked.*

That is the whole of what she did on the far end of the working reach in fourteen days and it is what any man on any line of work would have done on the last day of a run, and it took about four minutes, and it is the reason the rest of it happened.

## What Settles

It happened about a quarter of an hour after the mark, while she was standing nine feet off it with the bar in her hand.

She felt it go up through the ground the way a stage goes up, except that a stage goes out and this did not go anywhere she could tell.

Then he came back.

That is the only way she has ever been able to put it. For about three months he has been a thing she spends one mark of at a time and gets four sentences for, and she had gone into the fourteenth day with nothing left of him that she could spend on purpose, and at about the sixth hour on the twenty-first he was there, all of him, in her head, in about the way a person is there.

He said, in the ordinary voice, in about three sentences:

“Your job is on the list and it is finished and it has settled. That is what settles here. It is not a man. It never was a man.”

And then he said the part of it that is the whole of the batch.

“I am taking a share of it and spending it now, out of your foundation, and it does not come back. The base under you is one stage short of finished and I am taking it out of a base that is one stage short of finished, because that is the only place it is.”

“Out of the base.”

“Out of the base,” he said. “It is how I have always paid. You have known that since the first night and you have never once asked me for the invoice.”

He did not say how much. He did not say a figure of anything, and she did not ask him for one, and she has never once been able to get one out of him and has stopped asking.

## What The Tax Takes

Her right hand went at about the sixth hour on the twenty-first, at the far end of the bad flat, nine feet off a mark she had laid in lime about a quarter of an hour before.

It did not hurt the way a thing hurts when it is cut. It went the way the order went out of her left hand and the way the heel went, which is that it was there at one moment and it was not there at the next and everything else in the hand was the same.

The index finger of the right hand.

She looked at it on the lime mark and it was not there and the other three were and the thumb was and the back of the hand was and the aperture was open a third of the way along the first joint of the second finger in the ordinary way.

It is the finger that made the turn.

That is the whole of what it was, and she had a word for it by the fourteenth day.

The index is the finger that turns a bar over at the end of a stroke, the one that gets under the end of it and rolls it down and takes the weight coming back. It is the finger a hand puts a licence nail in with, and there is one nailed straight above a bunk nine days west.

She did not put it in. A voice she could not touch put it in, on her hand, on a night four months back, and the finger that did it is this one.

It is gone.

It is gone from that hour to the end of this and nothing in the world offers it back and nothing in the world has ever said it would. There is no stage after this that puts a finger on a hand. She is at the eighth of nine and there is nothing above nine anywhere.

Four fingers' worth of grip left on that hand, and eleven holds in the other one that hold to the end of a stroke out of about seventy she has tried in four months, and a left hand with the heel of it gone and the order gone out of it and a writing hand that takes eleven minutes a line with the flat of a second finger.

She did not put her hand over her face on a piece of ground nobody tends at the far end of the working reach.

She picked the bar up off the ground with the other three fingers and the thumb and got two feet of it up in one movement, which she can still do, and she put it down on its side, and she stood there on tended ground that she had tended.

## She Told Them Out Loud

She came back past the cart turn at about the seventh hour on the twenty-first and she put the bar against the hut and went to the trestle and there were four men at the pot and the man with the boot and the man with the book.

She said it in the ordinary voice, standing at the near end of the plank, the way a woman says a thing that has happened to her and not the way a woman says a thing that is going to happen to somebody.

“I have lost the index finger of my right hand,” she said. “It went at about the sixth hour out on the far end. It is not coming back and nobody is going to be offered it, so you can stop working out whether I am going to lose the other one, because I know and I am telling you before you ask.”

There were four men at the pot and one of them had a spoon in his hand.

There was a silence at the pot about as long as it takes to count eleven.

The sub-contractor wrote her down on the twenty-second at the fourth hour and did not look up, and said, “Twenty-two. Five. Same as the rest.”

That was the whole of what he said about it and it was the whole of what she needed, and she did not thank him for it, because thanking him would have made it a thing he had done for her and it was not that.

The man with the boot said: “I said on the seventh that I would come and look at it on the eleventh and I did not come.”

“You said you would not go down into it.”

“I did not come at all.”

“No,” she said. “You did not.”

He went back to the boot and it wanted doing twice and he did it twice.

## What It Bought

She went to bed on the twenty-first with four fingers on one hand and eleven holds in the other and a base under her that is one stage short of finished and about as cold as a stone, and with a voice in her head that had been a ration and a count for about three months and was a whole thing again.

She did not put it in the column of marks on the back of the ticket. She has been keeping that count since the tenth of a month that is two months back and it is hers and nobody in the world knows she keeps it, and she did not write on the twenty-first that there was more of him than there had been, because it was not a thing that came out of the four marks and she wanted it written down somewhere by somebody and there was going to be somebody for it later.

What she wrote, on the back of the third dead weight ticket, in her own hand with the flat of a second finger, in one line that took eleven minutes, was this:

*the finger is gone and it went for that.*

That is the whole of what the finger cost and she is not going to be asked for it twice, and there is not one thing in the world that gives it back.
