# Closing The Ground

It was made at a table, in a room at the west end of the camp, on the fourth of a month, by the people whose course it is, and she was not in the room and was about a mile off.

What she knew about it afterwards was in this order and she never had it in any other order.

A rule, nine years old. A foreman who told a man with a notebook what a rule was, at a wire, on the eleventh of the last month, because a rule is a rule and it is not a secret.

A piece of paper, read out of a notebook, in an office's hand, in the ordinary voice, at a table.

And then, on the eighth of the month, at about the sixth hour, in the open, at a trestle, eight or nine words, and everybody carrying on.

## The Rule

A course may close its own working ground against a person it does not carry.

That is the whole of it and it took her a week to understand that there was nothing underneath it.

It is not an expulsion. Nobody is turned off anything and nobody is put off a cart. It is a thing a line of work does about a person standing on it who is not on it, and it has been in use nine years and it has been used twice and both times it was right.

She got the shape of it in about ten minutes at the wire on the fourth of the month, after a fortnight of not asking anybody anything.

The rule is: the ground a course works is the ground the course is carried on. If a person is not carried by the course, then the course cannot put that person on its ground, because a line of work that kept its working ground open to anybody who was not on it would not be a line of work, it would be a field.

So the ground closes. From the morning of it. And it closes on the person, and it closes on the stretch she was standing on at the time, and everything that happens after that happens because of an arithmetic that has nothing to do with her.

That is what it is, and she had known it was coming since the eleventh of the last month at a wire, standing forty feet off, and there had not been one hour of the twenty-two days since in which she could have done one thing about it.

Two things, and they are on the same paper, and both of them are true.

The first: the thing that is in her head is unauthorised.

The second: it is carried by a licensee in good standing.

That is the certificate a field man of a Warden's office certified in a low room with twenty people in it, about two years back, in a county four hundred miles west, and it went into a book and then it went up a stair, and the referral that carries it went past every office she had ever stood in front of.

She had relicensed above it three times. The second licence was made on a schedule read aloud out of a contractor's book by a clerk in a building at Stony Ford whom she was nine seconds from and did not go to, and the third leg of it is in a hand that is not a person's.

Both are on the same paper.

Both are true at the same time and neither one cancels the other.

There is not one argument in the world a woman standing at a trestle can make against a piece of paper that says a thing is unauthorised and carried by a licence. The second half of that paper is the only half with a name on it, and the name on it is hers, and she put it there herself, at a trestle, in the second week of a month that is four months back.

The room at the west end of the camp is where the wedges of paper live, and it has a table in it and a stove in it, and on the fourth of a month there were four men in it: the foreman, the tender, the man with the board, and the man in the plain coat with the notebook.

She knows that because she was told nothing and worked it out, and because she went and asked, later, on the eighth, standing at a trestle, in about the time it takes to turn a bar over, and because a foreman does not lie to a woman about the day his ground closed.

The man in the plain coat read it out.

Not out of a file. Out of his own notebook, which he has carried under one arm for about a year, and he read it as he reads everything, in the ordinary voice, at a table, to three men who were entitled to hear it, because a foreman asked him what the paper was and he answered him truthfully.

That is the whole of what he did. A foreman asked a question. A man with a notebook answered it. Nothing was bought and nothing was threatened and nothing was touched and no question was asked of anybody in that room, including her, who was not in it.

## The Eighth Of The Month, Sixth Hour

She was at the trestle.

The day's readings were half done and her right arm was open and she had been out on the low side of the thirty-nine since the fourth hour, and she had come in at the sixth hour the way she comes in at the sixth hour, and there were about forty people inside about a hundred yards of her.

She heard it before she understood it, because a thing that is said at a trestle in a working camp is said in the ordinary voice and at the ordinary volume and with no ceremony on it at all.

She had her hand on the plank.

She had her hand flat on the board with the flat of a second finger about a foot to the left of the cell she was writing, and it was on the wood, and it had been on the wood all morning.

The foreman said: “The ground is closed to you from this morning. It is closed from the fourth.”

That was about nine words and there were two of them in the middle of it.

Nobody stopped talking. Nobody looked round. Two men went on discussing a man who had left, and a third said something about the thirty-one and it was about the same volume as it had been four minutes earlier.

Somebody behind her said *aye* and somebody else said something about the cart and a dog went along the track at the far end of the camp, and the whole of the sixth hour of that morning went on being the sixth hour of a morning.

The tender said, “Aye,” and went back to his board.

And that was the eighth of the month.

## What She Did

She finished the line she was on.

That is not a thing to be proud of and she was not proud of it and there was nothing else available. She was standing at a plank with eleven minutes left in a day's readings, and she wrote the date in the left cell and she wrote the figures in the cells in the order they came to her, and she did not go and stand in front of the man in the plain coat and she did not go to the foreman.

There is no version of anything she could have said.

She worked that out at the trestle, standing still, in about as long as it takes to get a bar over a foot of ground, and she was right, and it is the whole of what happened.

She had four years of paper behind her. Every argument she had ever won, she had won with a form.

The thing on that piece of paper was correct. She could not even argue that it was not, because the second half of it was her own name in a county's hand and a seal she had never touched, written by an office that countersigns in a hand which is not a man's.

And a foreman of about fifty with a daughter on that line was not being careful and was not being cruel.

He was doing the only thing he is built to do, and he was doing it correctly, and he had been right about everything since a trestle in the second week of a month that is four months back.

She had no counter.

Nothing at all, on that morning.

That is the first half of it and she was standing at the trestle with the arithmetic going and the arithmetic was immediate and it was not about her.

The course is contracted to tend a certain number of stretches in a run of nine days. The contract pays by the day out of a stretch count, and a stretch count is what a course is paid for.

The ground is closed against a person it does not carry. So the stretch she was standing on is not on the run any more. Not because it is bad ground — because no one on that course may work it, and a stretch that nobody works is not a stretch.

Which means the run is shorter, and the contract is smaller, and there is a hole in the board where a stretch used to be, and about nine men who were going to be put on that stretch in the next four days have nothing to be put on.

And a girl of nineteen who was going to be put on it on the ninth has nothing to be put on, and she is somebody's daughter.

## The Board With A Hole In It

She went past it at about the seventh hour and it was still up.

She had not asked to see it. She was going past it to get a bar and she was outside the wire by then, and a board under an eave does not stop a person walking, so she was about four feet off it and she looked.

The margin is the working reach and it is ruled in painted marks down the side, and a mark that is no longer on the run does not come off. It stays where it is and the line stays on it, because the stretch is still a place and the work was done there, and what is not there any more is the work.

So there is a mark on the margin with a rule under it and nothing in the cells.

That is all a hole in a board is. It is a mark that has stopped getting a figure in it.

It was the third one down from the join at the end where the low side is, and she had written in it herself on about eleven days of the last month, one at a time, with the flat of a second finger.

She looked at it for about as long as it takes to breathe out and went and got her bar.

## The Girl Was Idle

The girl of nineteen came over the wire at about the fourth hour the next morning with a bar, and she worked the twenty-eight, which is not the stretch she had worked all season, and she worked it well, and at about the eighth hour she came back past the trestle and put her bar down in the rack.

The contract did not carry her on that day and she did not know that and she did not ask.

A rule does not make an exception for the woman who was good at it, and it does not make an exception for a girl of nineteen either, and she was somebody's daughter, and the foreman did not say one word to her about any of it, and she did not know there was anything to say.

## What She Said Out Loud

Nothing.

Not one word in her own defence, at the trestle, in the six-hour, in front of about forty people.

Not a speech and not a sentence and not a question and not the beginning of one.

The whole of it is that she is not the kind of person a ground closes on, and that is the entire reason it worked and the entire reason it took about nine words.

There was nothing to be taken out of her hands, because there was nothing in them.

She went and got her bar at about the seventh hour and went out past the wire, because the ground inside it was not hers any more and there was not one person on that course who would have said a word if she had walked out of it.

## The Thing She Spent

She spent one of that day's ration on the way out past the wire, standing in the open about two hundred yards off, and it bought nothing at all.

She asked him, in four sentences, whether there was any part of it that was about her work.

He said no. He said it in the present tense and in about as few words as the question took, and it was true, and it was the whole of what he had for her.

She did not tell him she had spent it.

## The Order Of The Next Four Days

The stretch went back in three days. She watched it do it and she knew the four places where it comes over the top of a boot and she had known them since the second week.

The board has a hole in it. The board has a hole in it and the hole is not going to be filled, because a hole in the board is the shape of the contract, and the contract is smaller now and will be smaller at the end of the run than it was at the beginning.

Nine men are idle and a girl of nineteen is idle, on a working season, in a country where nobody is paid for anything they are not doing.

And she is outside the wire.

She was outside the wire with a licence, a claim, four stubs of paper in an oilcloth, and the only book in the world that can say she was ever here, and she cannot get to any of them.
