# The Stone and the Fire

The quarry face had been shut for a month and there was nothing behind it, and the only thing she could buy with money in this county was the thing on a board behind a well.

She went down the hill at about the sixth hour with an empty hand and came back at the eighth with a sledge-load of stone in it, because four loads a week at nine coppers is thirty-six a week and that is what a week of stone costs when the only stone there is costs nine.

Da Shan had the board behind the well with eight frames on it, two of which had been off in the middle of the month and were back because four men of the Qinghe furnace had carried them up a hill on their own time, and the boards had chalk on them in the ordinary way, one line for every load, and the date at the top.

He had never asked her a question. He had never asked anybody a question in nineteen years of a wall, on the grounds that a man who sells stone all day does not get paid to know what it is for.

“Nine,” he said. “I have told you it is nine and I will tell you again at the end of the month.”

“You have told me it is nine.”

“It is nine because it was five when you started coming and I have watched what happens to the price of a thing when a woman with one arm comes to a wall behind a well every week and buys four loads,” said Da Shan. “You are the only person in nineteen years who has ever moved my price and you did it by being regular.”

He put the coin into the box under the board without counting it, because he had counted it while she was counting it, which is the whole of what a man who has been doing this for nineteen years is.

## The Figure

She did it that night on the mat in the second bunk with the ticket end of a dead weight card under her thumb, and she did it three ways and it came out the same way three times.

Thirty-six a week.

About a hundred and forty coppers in the tin.

Thirty-six a week, and four weeks of it is a hundred and forty-four, and she was a few coppers under four weeks of stone, and a few coppers under four weeks is a shade under four weeks, which is the last week of this month.

And the season's road opens on the eighth of the next one.

She put her finger on the two dates and left them there.

Seven days between the last of the stone and the day the road opens.

Seven days in the first week of the thaw, with a wind out of the north-east off the high ground coming down the valley at night, in a room with a board in the window that had a gap under it you could put two fingers through and feel the outside on, and ten people in it.

That was the figure. It was the first time in nineteen months that the thing running out had a date on it that she could write down, and the date was seven days before a door.

## What She Did Not Do

She did not tell nine men.

She made it in about nine seconds, the way she had made every decision since the middle of the winter, and the reason for it was that she had worked out what a man does with a number like that, and there were nine of them, and every one of them would carry it up four miles to the frame and say it out loud in a hall so that other men had heard it from a mouth.

They had done that once already with a different number, in the middle of the month, and Gao had stood at the near end and said it out loud in front of nine men so that it would not be something read off a wall.

If she gave them this one they would make a thing of it, and a thing they made of it would be a thing they tried to fix, and the only fix nine men have for a shortage of stone at four loads a week is to carry more of it up a hill on their own time, and she was not going to be the reason a crew of nine men started carrying stone in the deep cold at hours that were not on anybody's schedule.

The hours on that schedule already read *irregular* for the fourth time this year and the word was stamped on the front of it in a clerk's hand, and an irregular schedule is not a thing a man can be paid extra against.

So she kept it.

## The Second Morning

She went down again on the second morning because going once in a week had become the habit and the habit was the only thing she had that cost nothing.

The board behind the well had one line on it from the day before and eight frames on the wall, and the two that had come off were still on, which she had expected, because the only thing that had ever come off that wall in nineteen years was a thing that stopped.

“You are going to come on the day after tomorrow as well,” said Da Shan.

“Yes.”

“You have not said that before.”

“You have not asked.”

“No,” said Da Shan. “I have not asked and I am not going to, and I will tell you the true thing about this board while I am standing at it, because you have been buying four a week since the middle of the month and there is nobody else in this town who has bought four a week since the middle of the month.”

He put his hand flat on the wall under the frames.

“These eight frames are the whole of what I have done in a winter,” he said. “I have sold two of them and got them back. I have sold nineteen coppers' worth of kindling and a quantity of lime to the lime-house and about four boards of shutter to a man who does not pay until the thaw. This board is my winter and you are four loads a week of it.”

He chalked the day up along the top.

“Four loads a week is the only thing on this wall that has moved all winter,” said Da Shan. “That is not a favour to you and it is not a reason. It is a fact about the wall, and I am telling you because in nineteen years a person who buys four a week is the only kind of person who has ever come back, and I would rather you knew that I noticed than that you thought I had not.”

## The Fire

The fire in the crew quarters went down twice between the fourth hour and the light on both of those days and had to be fed both times, and that is not a thing that happens because of a shortage of stone.

It is a room with ten people breathing in it and a stove that is thirty years old and has never had a good day, and it has gone down twice between the fourth hour and the light every night since before she came to it, and it will go down twice between the fourth hour and the light on the last night of the winter for the same reason.

She fed it at the fourth hour and at about two hours before the light, and she put on a half load at the second of those and kept the rest of the load by the bucket for the morning, which is what a person does with a thing that has to last.

That is the whole of the arithmetic of a cold room in a crew's quarters, and none of it is clever.

The quarter's hours went up the stair on the twentieth with the word on the front of them, and the crew's own work went on being asked and worked and entered on a sheet that is not a claim, and the nine men who were on that sheet came in off the floor at about the ninth hour and the room filled up the way it fills up.

A man at the window said something about the board in the window and a man at the fire said something about the wind, and that was the whole of what nine men had to say about the fire going down twice a night in the first month of the deep cold.

## The Third Thing She Did Not Tell Them

On the second evening she let herself do the arithmetic she had been refusing since the first night, which is what a hundred and forty coppers is for if you stop buying stone and spend the whole of it on the room.

It buys about four weeks of a fire that goes down twice between the fourth hour and the light and has to be fed twice.

That is the whole of what it is.

It does not buy a board for the window, because a board for the window is four coppers and she could have had that in the autumn. It does not buy a second stove, and there is nowhere in this county to put a second stove. It does not buy the nine men's hours and it does not buy a quarter of a winter.

And it does not buy a bed, which is the thing she wanted it to buy. A bed is a place, and she had not got one, and the only room in this town she had ever been able to put her coat down in was a wash house with eleven women on the floor in the third month of the cold.

The girl at the trough is paid four coppers a week out of it and four more out of a district fund for the cold months, and has already had a quarter paid forward to the thaw.

She lay in the second bunk in the cold with the fire a bed of ash and the licence nailed straight over her head with its head side to the room.

Under the floor there were nine men, and one of them was twenty-two and walked four miles down a mountain to stand eight feet off in a queue so that a woman with a ruined hand could take eleven minutes at a counter without a clerk shortening them, and not one of them knew that the fire was going to stop, and she had decided that on the second day of the month and not on the day it stopped.

Seven days between the last of the stone and the day the road opens, and she was carrying the number by herself, and she had decided to carry it, and she did not tell them.

And she did not put any part of it on a dead weight card, because a card with a rule on it is for a thing she is doing about a man in her head, and the stone was never going to be a thing she did about a man in her head. She had seven days and thirty coppers and nine men and she was going to sit on all three of them by herself until one of them gave.

It was going to be the tin. The tin was always the one that gave.
