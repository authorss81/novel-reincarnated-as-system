# Struck Out

The book is written at the start of a day and squared at the end of a run.

That is not a rule anybody made. It is how a foreman's book is kept, because a day has to be entered before the day can be worked — you cannot have a man out on the thirty-nine and not have the thirty-nine in the book, or the wage is a wage for something nobody can say happened.

And a run gets squared. That is the last thing in the month a foreman does with a book, and he does it standing at a trestle in the open, in the ordinary way, because a book that is squared is a book other men can be told to read.

## The Seventeenth

He did it at about the second hour.

She was at the trestle, because she was at the trestle at the fourth hour every morning and had been for about four months, and because she had nowhere else in that camp to be. She had been outside the wire for nine days. There is a place about two hundred yards off the ground where a person can sit down out of the wind, and she had been sitting down there at the fourth hour and at the seventh hour for nine days, and about nine men were standing about inside the wire not looking in that direction.

The foreman came out with the book and squared it on the plank and turned round the page.

He had been running a thumb down a column since the first of the month. She could see him do it. It stopped at a line and went along the line and stopped and went along.

Then he took a pen off his belt and drew one line through his own handwriting.

Not through her name.

Through her name.

That is what a foreman does. A name that is on a run and not on the ground is a name on a book that has not been worked, and it has to come out, and there is one way of taking a line out of a column and it is a stroke of a pen and it is not a form and there is nothing to fill in.

He did it standing up, in the open, at about the second hour of the seventeenth, with about six men within thirty feet, and he did not do it at night and he did not do it in a room.

His daughter was standing about ten feet off with her coat over her arm, and she had been standing there since before he came out with the book.

“Da. Is that hers.”

“It is the line closed on her.”

“That is her hours.”

“It is her rate from the eighth. Her hours are in the book and they were in the book the morning the ground shut, and that is the last morning they can be in it, so they stay in it. A man at a desk four hundred miles west reads this book in order with dates on it and a hole in it is a hole in it.”

He said it once and flat and about the work, in the ordinary voice, with a thumb still on the line he had drawn.

The girl stood there for a bit and then said, “Oh,” and went and got her bar out of the hut.

Ning Yan was close enough to touch the plank the whole of it and was in none of it, and nobody looked at her while any of it happened.

## What Stands And What Stops

The hours stand.

Everything from the fourth of the month before last up to the eighth of this month is in that book in his own hand, in order, with dates on it — every day she worked, the stretch, the figures, the painted mark of the corner.

Every one of those days was a real day at a real stretch at a real rate, and they were read out of that book into a schedule in a building at Stony Ford by a clerk whom she was nine seconds from and did not go to, and that schedule is in a room past her county's boundary with a countersignature on it in a hand that is not a person's.

Those hours do not go anywhere.

What stops is the rate. From the eighth of this month there is no line at all, and there is not going to be one, and the book does not close and is not shut on her account. It is squared and it goes on being a book other people write in, tomorrow and the day after and for the rest of that season, and on the first of the next month a man will open it at the first of a page and start again.

## What She Did Not Say

She did not ask him to do it later.

There was nothing to ask, and she worked that out standing about four feet off a plank at about the second hour of the afternoon with her arms at her sides, and it took the length of a bar being put in a rack.

A book that keeps a name on it that has not been worked is a book that lies. That is not a punishment and it is not a discipline. It is a wage-book that has stopped being true, and the wage in it is somebody else's money and the hours in it are hers and the office past the boundary reads it in order with dates.

If he left her name on it, then the first of the next month would go up the stair with a woman on the reach who had not been on it for nine days, and the whole of what she bought at a counter in a county four hundred miles west — an hour in order, with a date on it, in a book that no county inside the bound holds — would have a hole in it as big as nine days.

So he drew the line, and she would have drawn it herself, and there was no version of anything she could have said at that trestle.

## The Man In The Plain Coat

He was there.

He had been on that course for about a month and a half and he had never once been at a trestle, and on the seventeenth of the month he stood about eight feet off with his notebook shut against his side and he watched a man strike a woman's name out of a wage-book.

He wrote it down.

He wrote it down afterward, when the foreman had gone, which means he stood there and let it happen first and then wrote it, which is the thing he has done every time.

He did not look at her and he did not say one word to her about it and he did not come near her for the rest of the day, and no paper that came out of his notebook that day carried anything on it but an office.

She did not thank him for any of that. She did not thank him on the eleventh of the last month either, and she is not going to, and it is not going to be a thing she thinks about in a week.

## The Fifth Stage

It came in the same week.

She had the interval running from the twenty-fifth of the month before, twenty-two days, and it came on the nineteenth of this month, and she took it at about the fourth hour in a bunk with three boards on it in a hut with nine men asleep in it, in the ordinary way.

[Ning Yan of the county of Lowwater, servant-born, under a ceiling written in her own hand. Foundation, stage five, is held.]

And here is the thing about the two of them, and she had about two days to sit with it, and two days was enough.

They were not connected.

Her name came out of a contractor's book on the seventeenth because a rule that is nine years old and has been used twice and was right twice has been applied to it, and the foreman squared his book.

The stage came on the nineteenth because a base gets heavier and the intervals lengthen and twenty-two days is what twenty-two days is.

She had two days in which to be certain that one had nothing to do with the other, and being certain of it was worse than not knowing.

Because if the stage had cost her the name, then she could have put the two together and carried both of them as one thing. She could have said: that is what it took. That is the price. And a price is a thing a person can hold.

They were two things that happened in the same week, and neither of them was about the other, and she was eighteen years old and standing at a trestle at the west end of a nine-day line with about ten coppers and a bunk and a fire that is not lit out of her share.

It is not a wound.

It is a line through a name in an ordinary book, drawn by a man doing his job in the second hour of the afternoon in the open.

And it is the first time in four years that a piece of paper has been about her that was written by a person who does not know her.

Everything else she had ever had — a burial line, a surety transfer, a countersignature, a relicensing with a ceiling in her own hand, a sheet with three marks on it, a schedule read aloud out of a book she was not shown — every one of that was done by somebody who knew exactly what she was and what they were doing about it.

This was a foreman squaring a wage-book on the seventeenth of a month.

He does not know what she is. He was told that the thing in her head was unauthorised and he believed it because it was on a paper and because it is correct, and he did not care in the way a person cares about a thing they are doing to a person.

He cared in the way a person cares about a line of work and about eleven men and a daughter and a contract.

Not defeat.

That was the first thing and it surprised her and she let it be surprising for about a day.

What she felt on the seventeenth, standing at a trestle, watching a line go through a name in a hand that was not hers, was a clean cold thing with no lump in it anywhere, the way a thing feels when a boot comes out of a hole and the boot is yours.

And the reason for it took her until about the fourth hour of the eighteenth to get hold of, and it is the whole of what she has got.

As long as her name was in that book, it was the only book in the world with her name in it.

She had spent four years making a set of hours into a record, and then nine days walking to find a book that would hold it, and the whole of what she came out here for was one column in one man's hand on a trestle.

Now there is a second possibility.

She looked at the second possibility for about four minutes at the fourth hour of the eighteenth, and the second possibility is that there could be another book, held by another man, in which her hours go in on their own and are not answerable to the first.

She did not do the arithmetic out loud. She did not do it out loud because she had been in rooms where she did arithmetic out loud for four years and every one of them turned out to be a room with a man in it.

## Where That Left Her

She is the most expensive worker on that ground.

She had done about four hundred yards of the worst of it since the second week of a month now four months back, at a rate that not one of eleven men could have held at a walk, and she was the only person on nine days who could have stood in the ring at the fourth hour and got two feet of it up in one movement, and she has been outside the wire for nine days with a bar she is not allowed to put in the ground.

The ground is shut.

She is eighteen years old, standing at a trestle, in the first working season on a line that nine days of road away has never heard anything announced in, and she is the only person in the world who knows what the drift is loudest where.

And there is going to be a second book, or there is going to be nothing.
