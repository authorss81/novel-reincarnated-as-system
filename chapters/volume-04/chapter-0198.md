# The Hour It Starts

The foreman came to the trestle himself, which she learned afterwards was not unusual, and he had the book under his arm and he put it down on the plank and did not open it.

He was about fifty. He had been the foreman long enough that nobody on that course gave him the thing that goes with the title, and he had a broad face and a bad left thumb and he looked at her the way you look at a column you are going to have to make a decision about.

“You want a day's pay,” he said.

“I want to be on the book.”

“That is not the same and you know it is not.” He put his thumb down on the cover of the book. “On the book is a name with a county behind it. If I write you in here under nothing, then there is a day's wage against a line that no office on this side can send up anywhere, and my book stops being a book and starts being a notebook.”

She had heard that structure four times in four years from four men who were each doing it in their own way, and it had never once been said to her as a favour.

## The One Thing She Had Never Been Able To Produce

“Say the name,” he said.

He had a board and a pencil and he did not have the book open, and he was waiting.

She had been asked for her name about four hundred times in four years. She had given it in a hall to a copyist who wrote it in the wrong order. She had given it on a form to a woman taking a name off it. She had written it out on a column in a hall with no roof in eleven minutes with the flat of a second finger.

She had given it to a man on a step and to a man in a lane and to a man in a market and to a girl at a frame, and in every single one of those cases it was the beginning of a thing about her that somebody would write down.

This was the first time it was the whole of what she was there for.

“Say it properly,” the foreman said, “because a county is not a thing I can guess at. I have had men here whose counties never heard of them and their hours have gone up a stair for years and never come down, and that is fine by me, because it is not my money. But if I write a county on you, that county can read this book. Every hour. In order. With the dates on.”

He said it exactly the way a man reads a line off a board.

“I am telling you what you are about to buy,” he said, “because nobody ever tells anybody that and everybody finds out about it four years later in a room you are not standing in.”

## Four Seconds

She said it in four seconds, and it was the same name she had never once been able to put in a place that wanted it.

He wrote it down. He did not repeat it. He did not look up to check the spelling and she did not spell it for him, because he had it right the first time, and that was the first thing that had ever happened to her that way.

Then he put the book open.

The arithmetic took about two seconds and she did it behind her teeth with her eyes on the page.

Before she said it she was nothing at all. There was a woman standing at a trestle at Stony Ford with a bag, and no office on this side of anything had ever heard of her, and the only paper about her in the world was written in a county nine days west and could not get any further than a stair.

The moment the name was down there was a woman with a county behind her in a contractor's book, and an office past that county could take the book, or a copy of it, or a sheet out of it, and read every hour she worked in order with dates on it, forever, and act on all of it.

It was the same thing a registrar had written on a bar in the ordinary hand. It was exactly that. And a referral had gone up a stair past every office she had ever stood in front of, and it carried her name and her stages in order and her hours, and it could only be executed past a boundary no county paper crossed.

She had about two seconds in which the whole shape of what she was doing was visible to her at once, standing at a trestle with the book open in front of her, in daylight.

She said nothing about any of it.

There was no version of that morning in which she did not say the name. The alternative was nine days of a body at Condensing nine walking back up a road she could not pay for, and a woman with no clearance does not get paid, and the thing she had come here for was a book that keeps hours, and a name was the only key to that a person had ever had.

She said the name first and thought about it afterwards, and thought about it in about two seconds, and it stayed said.

## The Half Second Afterwards

That was the half second.

She had got the name said and the book open and the work given to her and the slip in her hand, and about half a second afterwards, alone, in the middle of a morning with the fire going and eight men already through the wire, she understood that she had just made herself findable by the one office in the world that could act on an eight hundred jin standing claim against her.

Not by the office that had refused to answer a request on a shelf. Not by the office that had stamped a bar on a petition. By the office that holds the schedules, which is the office that finds people.

She worked out the whole of it while a man twelve feet away drove a bar into the ground.

The referral went up the stair carrying her name, her stages in order and her hours. It could only be executed past a boundary. She was going past that boundary. She had been past it for two days without knowing it, and the man behind her had walked the same road for two days, and neither of them had said anything about it on it.

And now the hours are in order with dates on them in a book that that same side can read.

So the two halves of the trap, the counter and the execution, which she had known about since a bar was written in a slower hand in an ordinary room, were the same object, and she had put her own hours into it with her own mouth.

She did not regret it.

That is the part she turned over twice on the walk back from the trestle and it came out the same both times. She would do it again in the morning. She would do it again this afternoon, in a bunk with three boards and a mat on it, in a cold morning with her arm open, on the tenth day and the eleventh.

And that was the frightening part, and it was not the finding, and the finding was that she would do it again and it was no use at all to her knowing why.

## Cleared

The foreman did not make a ceremony of the hiring.

He put a mark in a column for the day and wrote the stretch and wrote the day rate, six coppers, and then he wrote out a slip in his own hand on a piece of board-pressed paper with his own name at the top of it and hers under it, and gave it to her.

It was not a county paper.

It carried no county stamp and no county number and no registry and no clerk's signature and nothing on it that anybody anywhere would be able to take to an office and get anything out of. It was a contractor's word, given out at a trestle to a woman he had decided to hire, and it was worth nothing at all to a relicensing and it was worth exactly one thing to the woman it was written to.

She took it.

A woman with no clearance does not get paid, and a woman who walks off with a piece of paper in her hand that she could not produce anywhere is a woman who will be asked about it at every counter for the rest of her life, and she took it and folded it once and put it in the oilcloth.

Because it went in a different book from the county one, that is the whole argument she had not made yet, and she did not make it then. She had the slip.

## The Girl

The girl at nineteen came over the wire at about the second hour and worked the inside of it for six hours while Ning Yan did the outside of it, and neither of them said one word.

The work was not lifting.

She watched, in the first hour, and worked out what the tool did and then worked out how to do the thing the tool did with two hands and a foot, and after that the girl came over the wire and worked the same eight yards in eight minutes and went to the next stretch, and she did that eleven times.

Ning Yan took forty on the same eight yards the first time.

She did not say anything about that either. There was nothing to say. The girl had her father's hands and eleven years on it and she was doing in eight minutes what a body at Condensing nine took forty to do with everything it had, and nobody on that course was going to be impressed by the second number and nobody was going to be disappointed by it.

## The Coin

He paid her at the trestle at the end of the day, in coin, out of a fold, and counted it onto the plank.

Six coppers.

He put the book down open beside the coin without being asked to and without making a thing of it, and turned round to the page where the days went in, and she stood there and read her own line.

It was in a hand that was not hers.

It had the date on it, and the stretch, and the hours, and the rate, and six coppers written against it, and it was the fourth entry down in a column of about forty, and it had been there since before she had got to the trestle, which meant it had been written at the start of the day and not at the end, which meant somebody on that course had decided at the beginning of a day that this woman was going to be on it.

She put the six coppers into the tin with what was left of the old tin, and that was the first money she had earned in this book.

And that evening, in a bunk with three boards and a mat on it, the tin came out lighter than it went in, because the place at the fire was a third of the day's pay and two flat cakes were another third and the rest of the day was water, and she understood inside about an hour of having the six in her hand that there was not going to be a sum in this tin by the end of a season.

She had been living hand to mouth in a cold county for two weeks and she had not noticed it because a warm room with nine men in it does not require coin.

Out here it did. Out here the fire, the board and the bread came out of the same six coppers, and a woman who expected to put something by was going to be disappointed at the rate of one day.
