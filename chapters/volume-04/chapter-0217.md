# The Left Hand

The heel of her left hand went before the quarry at Lowwater, three years and a bit ago, and it is still gone, and it was gone for a year and a half of this before anybody out here ever saw her and it is not what this is about.

What went on the twenty-fifth of the month was the order.

## What An Order Is In A Hand

Two years. That is what she put into it, on and off, in every place she ever worked and at every hour she was ever paid for.

The heel is the part of a hand that knows what the ground is doing. Take it away and a hand can still grip, and can still take a load, and can still pull, and can still close.

It cannot sequence.

The order is not strength and it is not knowledge. It is the sequence, in a hand, that knows where the fingers are going before the fingers go: the bar comes over and the wrist is already turned, and the turn is not a decision, and the four fingers are already half-closed on a thing that has not arrived yet, and the thumb has already gone to the place on the shaft where it always goes, and none of that has happened because anybody thought about it.

A hand that cannot feel a boot learns to feel a boot by knowing where it was last time.

That is the whole of what she rebuilt, on a mat in a cold room and in a bunk with three boards on it and on the tail-board of a stone cart and in about nine hundred other places over two years, and she did it the way a person learns anything that has no teacher, which is by doing the thing wrong nine hundred times until the wrong stops happening.

It worked.

On the low side of the forty, at about the seventh hour on the fourteenth of the month, she put a bar into two feet of the worst of it, got her weight over the pull, and turned the bar over at the top of it in one movement without anything in her having to be told to.

That was two months ago. That is what she had on the twenty-fourth.

## The Twenty-Fifth

The interval is twenty-two days this time and it came round on the twenty-fifth of a month and she took it at about the fourth hour on a bunk with three boards on it.

She ordered it in the ordinary way, the way she had ordered everything since a cold quarter in a county office four years back.

## What It Took

She still holds.

That is the first thing to say and it is the thing she checked first, at about the fifth hour, sitting on the edge of the bunk with both hands in front of her.

She could hold a bar. She could carry a bucket of drift. She could pull a thing toward her with both hands and take two hundredweight of it off a heap. She could turn a bar over at the top of a pull, eventually, if she stood still first and thought about the turn first and let her hand find the place on the shaft where it goes.

It is not slower in the way a hurt hand is slower. It is slower the way a strange road is slower.

The sequence is gone.

Every grip now has to be built from the beginning, one at a time, by her, in about the length of a breath, before the bar lands. And a bar in a ring has to be turned over in a single movement and there is nobody within nine days of road to learn that from and there is not time to watch a woman learn a hand and no room in a working day for a woman who is learning one.

It is not slow in the arm.

That is the part nobody who has not had one believes. The arm does what it is told and the arm is the strongest thing on that ground and it has never once been the thing that failed.

It is in the half-second before.

Before the pull, a hand that has an order in it is already holding the bar in the place it will be when the bar comes up, and the four fingers have gone to the shaft and the thumb has gone to the mark and the wrist has begun the turn, and all of that is over before a person has decided anything at all.

Without it, there is a moment — it is not long, it is not even a full beat of a foot — where the hand is waiting to be told, and then the telling comes down from about four inches up, and then the hand does it.

Four inches. That is the distance the order has to travel.

And the difficulty is not the delay. A bar will wait for a hand.

The difficulty is that in the ring the turn has to be at the top of the pull, because the ground is wet to two feet and if the bar comes up flat the wet under it has nothing to hold to and it comes off in the foot. So the turn cannot be built. It has to be there.

She built it four hundred and some times on the twenty-fifth. She built it on the six days after that, and on the six days after that, and she had built it every time since, and it gets about a tenth quicker every ten days and it is not going to be the same again and it is not going to be as fast as a hand that was never broken.

## The Hand She Went Out On

She went out to the ring at the fourth hour on the same morning, with the hand she had got, which is the only way there is.

That is what a tax looks like. Not a wound. Not a punishment. A thing that was the most expensive part of her and was now a thing she had to do from the beginning every single time, and the interval that paid for it was twenty-two days of her hours in a book written at the start of a day by a man who never checks it against anything.

She was out on the low side of the forty at about the fifth hour and she turned a bar over at the top of a pull at about the sixth hour and it took her about twice as long as it had taken her on the twenty-fourth.

She got it over.

She got every bar over for eleven hours.

At about the ninth hour a man she had never spoken to put his own bar down flat and asked her, in the ordinary voice a man asks another man a thing at a ring, why she was stopping on every pull.

“Why are you stopping on every pull.”

“My hand is slow.”

“Mine is slow too,” he said, and went back into it, and asked her nothing else.

She got on with the work.

She turned four hundred bars over that day, one at a time, from the beginning of every one, and the morning went on the way mornings do.

## What She Wrote About It

On the back of a dead weight ticket, in her own hand, with the flat of a second finger, in about eleven minutes for the four lines, because a county hour takes eleven minutes and an ordinary one takes the same.

Left hand, hold: yes.

Left hand, carry: yes.

Left hand, pull: yes.

Left hand, turn at the top of a pull: built each time.

And then, because it was the thing she had actually come out here to find out and had not wanted to write down:

Left hand, write: unchanged.

The hand that writes is the hand that has the heel gone and it has never had the order either, and every hour she has ever entered anywhere in nine counties has been written with the flat of a second finger in about eleven minutes where it used to take four.

That has not changed and nothing on the twenty-fifth of that month changed it, and she wrote it on the ticket in case there was ever a morning when she needed to know which of the two hands was the one that had gone.

## The Girl Of Nineteen

The girl of nineteen came past at about the eighth hour on the twenty-fifth, did her eight yards in eight minutes, and did not stop at the end of it.

She had stopped once, on the third of a month, and asked a question about it. She did not ask it again, and she did not look over, and the whole of the difference between the two of them was in the turn at the top of the pull.

She was nineteen.

She had never lost a heel and had never had to rebuild anything and did not have to stand at a plank at the fourth hour for three mornings with a dead weight ticket in her coat, and she does the same work in a third of the time.

She was not better than Ning Yan at anything except this. She was not stupid. She was not wrong about any part of it, and she had been right about the pace since the second week of a month that is now three months back, and there was nothing to say to her and she was not going to say it.

Grief, and underneath the grief, which she had not expected and did not go looking for: envy.

Not of the girl, who has done nothing to her and never will and whose father's hands she can see from the trestle. Envy of the fact of it. Of a body that was never broken and therefore never had to be rebuilt, and therefore has never had to find out what it is like to lose the thing you spent two years putting back.

She was the strongest body on that course.

She had lost the ability to do the ordinaryest thing on it.

That is what she has on the twenty-fifth of the month, and the foreman did not soften it and it did not cost him a day's pay, and it changed nothing at all about the wage-book, and the mark of a cross went against her name in the column for the thirty-ninth day of the season the way it goes against every name on that page.

There is a thing about the hand that is worth saying once and only once, because it is the whole of it.

Two years of rebuilding a working hand from nothing, and she had done it twice in her life.

This was the one that finished.

There is nobody within nine days of road who has anything to do with any of it. Nobody in nine counties has ever asked her for it. It is not on the licence and it is not on the back of the licence and it is not in any of the five documents with her hand on them, and the one office that could ever want it does not know that she exists.

She had been the most expensive hand on nine days of ground for two years and there was nobody to hand it to.

She went over the wire at the fourth hour in the morning with the hand she had and worked the low side of the forty until the light went, and she turned a bar over about four hundred times and built every one of them from nothing.
