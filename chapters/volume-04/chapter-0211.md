# What Six Coppers Buys

There is a store at the back of the camp between the cook-fire and the cart track, and it is a plank on two barrels and a roof that is a wagon board, and the man behind it sells oil and salt and lamp and nothing else that anybody out here has a use for.

She had been past it about fifty times and had not gone in, and she went in on the twelfth of the month at about the second hour in the afternoon with the last of what she had been keeping in the lining of her coat.

He was about sixty and he had a thumb like a shovel and he listened to the whole of it, which took her about a minute and a half, and then he went behind the barrels.

He weighed them out in front of her. That is the thing she had not expected and the only thing she wrote down afterward in her own head.

Four pounds of rendered oil, out of a tin, into a stoppered jar, and he put the jar on the scale against a flat stone until the arm came level, and told her what it weighed.

A half-peck of quicklime out of a barrel, and he put the scoop on the scale twice because the first one was heaped and he did not leave a heaped scoop on a scale.

Two hanks of hempen line off a hook, and he weighed those too, and said that nobody out here buys line by weight but that he weighed it because she had asked.

A length of good ox-hide, about two feet of it, cut off a length that was a good deal older than she was.

A round of beeswax the size of a fist, wrapped in a leaf.

Five things, weighed and measured, one at a time, in the ordinary way, by a man who was not going to be paid for his afternoon and who did not appear to find any of it interesting.

## The Sum

It was thirty coppers.

She had thirty coppers in her hand and she had had them for five days, because for five days she had not put her share into the fire on the plank at the west end and had gone to the store for her own bread instead, which is what you do when you are not putting into a fire.

Six coppers a day and a place at the fire. One number, not two.

The fire, the bunk and the bread all come out of the same six. That is how it has been since the first working morning and it is why there has never been anything spare in it, and she had understood that arithmetic on her second day there and had gone on not looking at it.

The base under her was bought out of the part of the six that had been keeping her warm.

She put the thirty coppers on the plank.

He counted them twice, which was fair, because they had been in the lining of a coat for five days and she had not counted them in that time either.

Then he put them in a box, and then he said the whole of what he said to her in the whole of the afternoon, which was: “I will want the oil inside a week or it goes thin, and I have had one jar go thin out here and it went to the man who lit the stove in the hut and there is not another one of him.”

He did not ask her what the oil was for.

He did not tell her that she was the first person to have asked him for any of it. He did not make a speech, and he did not charge her for the minute and a half of listening, and he did not say that thirty coppers was a lot for a woman at a course to come in with on a working afternoon.

She did not bargain. She did not ask whether there was another way to do it that cost less.

That was the whole of it and it took about nine seconds, standing at a plank.

## The Five Days Before The Plank

It took five days to get, and this is the part that is easy to write down and hard to have done.

She had six coppers a day and every one of them went out before the light was off the hill. So the thirty had to come out of five days in which she spent nothing at all, and the way to spend nothing at all at a camp in a working season is to stop buying the fire.

She worked out on the seventh that the place at the fire was the largest single thing in the six that she could put a hand on without it being a thing about the bread, and she put her hand on it on the eighth, and on the eighth at the second hour she stood at the west end of the plank where the working men put in what they put in, and she did not put in.

There is no list. There has never been a list. The fire is fed out of whatever goes on the plank and the only man who would have known that she had stopped is a man who has nine years of not counting other people's wood.

From the eighth she slept in a bunk with three boards and a mat on it and a second blanket she had not asked for, in a hut with eleven other men in it, in a working season, five days.

On the ninth she noticed that nobody had said anything about it.

On the eleventh she understood that they were not going to, and that this was not cruelty and was not a kindness either; it is that a camp is a place where a person is a pair of hands and a rate, and a hand that has stopped putting into a fire has not become a different thing, it has simply become a hand that does not put into a fire.

## The Interval

The interval runs on days and it does not care what the days were spent on.

Twenty days since the first Foundation, and every one of the twenty was an hour in a book written at the start of a day by a foreman who has never once checked it against anything, and the hours were going in under the name she had said out loud at a trestle in the second week of the month before last, in order, with dates on them, in a book that goes up a stair.

She had not asked whether twenty days was the number and she had not asked whether she could have them now. She had a date and the date was the twelfth for buying and the thirteenth for the rest of it, and she had worked out on about the eighth that the arithmetic of a store at a camp was the only arithmetic in this that she could do at all.

## The Thirteenth

She did it in the ordinary way.

She had been doing it in the ordinary way since a cold quarter in a county office four years ago, and there is no wording to it, and she put it into the air at about the fourth hour on the thirteenth with a bar in her hands on the low side of the ring where the ground is worst, the way a person says a thing out loud in an ordinary place where nobody needs anything.

[Ning Yan of the county of Lowwater, servant-born, under a ceiling written in her own hand. Foundation, stage two, is held.]

The jar of oil was in her coat and the lime was in a cloth at the west end of the bunk and the hide and the line and the wax were in the oilcloth above the Clay Plate with four stubs of paper and three dead weight tickets.

## Where It Went

About two hundred yards, into a field.

Exactly as far as the first one went, because that is how far it goes.

Eleven men, a girl of nineteen, a foreman and a tender are inside the working reach. It went two hundred yards from where she was standing and there is not one person in that whole country within nine days of road who has ever heard a stage said out loud in their life.

Nine counties is what is heard. Nine counties has heard every stage she ever took, in order, in the words of whoever read it out, at the moment it happened.

Nine counties is nine days west and it heard nothing.

Four counties is what is written, and there are four county books with a stage column in them, and the column in the county of Lowwater reads one, two, three, five, six, seven, nine, and there is a space above the row she was standing in that day where a line ought to go and nothing goes into it, because the county that would write it does not know, and there is nobody standing in nine days of road who could go and tell it.

Four is also nine days west.

## Five Days Of Nothing Else

From the thirteenth the fire was not lit out of her share.

That was the whole of it and there was no conversation about it. The fire is fed out of what the working men put on the plank at the west end and there is no entry against any name on it and no one had ever kept an account of hers, and she stopped putting in, and so she stopped being one of the people the fire was lit for.

She had bread. She bought it at the store in the mornings and it was not as good and it lasted about half a day longer than the camp's.

She had a bunk.

She had five days of nothing else, and then she had a job again and the job was the same job at the same rate and the fire was lit for everybody else.

She was not cold for the first night. She was cold for about eleven days, counting the five before the plank and the five after the stage, and about the second of those eleven it stopped being cold and became a fact about the shape of a night.

That is what it is like. Being cold does not feel like a decision after the first night. It feels like weather.

## The Thing She Noticed And Did Not Have Time For

She was not hungry.

It had been true since the first Foundation and it was true on the thirteenth of this month with a jar of oil against her ribs and five days of nothing else in front of her, and she noticed it the way she had been noticing it for four weeks — at the fourth hour, at the fire, with eleven men eating around a thing that was not lit out of her share.

She had put three pounds of bread a week aside in her head since the thaw and she had not been hungry once in thirty days, and there is no part of a working season that puts weight back on a woman.

She noticed it.

There was a second thing underneath it and she had a hold of it for about as long as it takes to get a bar turned over, and then the bar was turned and the ring needed doing, and the four of the day came round again, and she did not have time for it then and she did not go back to it afterwards.

That is the whole of what the second Foundation cost that has a name in it. It is five days of the six coppers that are not spent on the six coppers any more, and it is the part of the six that was warm.

A woman who pays for the thing with the thing that was keeping her alive is not poorer on the thirteenth than she was on the twelfth by any measure she can put a number to.

She was colder on the fifteenth, and there was no fire, and she went back over the wire at the fourth hour in the morning and the boot went where the boot goes.
