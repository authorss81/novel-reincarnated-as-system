# Where It Leaks

The stretch the foreman put her on was a ring, which is what they called the low side of anything, and it was the worst ground on the whole nine days and everybody in the camp knew it before she got to it.

She found out about it at the wire, on the third morning, because she asked one question and got an answer that was a warning dressed as a fact.

“That is the ring on the low side of number thirty-one,” the man with the broken thumbnail said. “You will want the long bar and not the short one, and you will want your left boot off about four times, and do not stand with both feet in it, because it is not the depth that is the problem, it is that it takes the boot.”

There were eleven men on that course and ten of them had been on it before her, and every one of them had a way of walking it.

She watched four of them go over the wire in the morning, and watched each of them put a foot down in a place a person who had not done it would not have chosen.

One went down the left side where it looked like rock and there was no rock. One crossed the low place in two moves and did not stop in between. The girl of nineteen went through it in a rhythm, four strides and a stop, four strides and a stop, like somebody carrying a yoke.

She went over the wire behind them with a bar and none of them said anything to her and none of them slowed down.

## What The Ground Was

It came up over the top of a boot.

That is the whole of it and it is worth saying exactly, because there is no other kind of ground in this book and it is not mud and it is not water and it is not cold in any way that can be got at with a fire.

It is about a foot of it in the low place and it is grey and it is wet all the way down under the grey, and when you stand in it your weight goes through the grey into the wet and the wet takes hold of the boot, and if you put your weight forward the boot stays where it is and the leg keeps going.

Nobody falls in it. That is the thing nobody tells you. It is not deep enough to fall in and it is not soft enough to sink in and it will not hold a person up at all; it holds a boot exactly as much as it holds a stone, which is to say not at all.

So the whole of working the ring is putting your weight over the pull, which takes about forty minutes to learn and about a year to stop thinking about.

She was at Condensing nine.

She was the strongest body in that camp by a long way. She had carried a hundred and seventy quarry loads in thirty-four days at a rate four of those men could not have held at a walk, and she went into the ring at the fourth hour of a morning with a long bar and eleven hours of a day in front of her, and she took forty minutes to do a length that the girl of nineteen had done in eight.

## It Came Back

She cleared it.

That is the part that has to be written down, because it is the part that undoes everybody. She got a bar under about two feet of the worst of it and moved it, and the ground under it was not the ground the men had been describing; it was sound, and it was black, and it took a boot perfectly.

She stood on it and put her whole weight into a bar and the ground held.

She worked eleven hours and she cleared about forty yards of it. The ring was four hundred yards long.

On the ninth day of last month they had cleared it and it had been back by the twelfth. She had heard that from a man holding a board on the morning of the twenty-seventh and she had done the arithmetic on it standing up, and the arithmetic was right, and it was not the whole answer.

The whole answer is that the ring is the bottom of a slope of work about a mile long, and the whole slope was not done in the season before, and the season before that, and the season before that.

The drift does not come up out of the ring. The ring is where it collects.

Everything the man with the board had told her was true and she stood in the middle of nine hours of it with a bar in her hands and watched the proof arrive in the only form proof ever arrives in, which is somebody else's work not having been done.

## Four Hundred Yards

The rest of the day is worth about four sentences because there is nothing in it that is not the same thing happening again eleven times.

The bar goes under. The bar comes up. The grey goes onto the bank behind her in a heap that is not even wet, because the top of it dries in an hour in this wind and looks exactly like the ground it came from, and by the end of the day there was a long grey heap along four hundred yards of that slope that looked, from twenty feet away, like a bank that had always been there.

That is why nobody from outside has ever caught anybody.

It looks like the ground.

She worked eleven hours at it. She cleared forty yards of the four hundred and she was faster than any man on that course by a distance that would have been worth something anywhere else in the world, and the ring was three hundred and sixty yards of exactly the same thing in front of her.

She ate at the fire at the fourth hour and went back over the wire.

## The Girl Came Over At The Fourth Hour

She did the same eight yards in eight minutes.

She had done it that way the first morning and she did it that way this morning, and she did not look at Ning Yan and she did not say anything, and she came over the wire at the fourth hour, worked her stretch, and went back, and that was the whole of the day she had with the strongest body on that course.

Neither of them said anything about it then, and neither of them has said anything about it since.

What Ning Yan thought about, standing in a ring at the fifth hour with a bar and her arm open, was that she was the most expensive body that had ever been put on that ground and the least useful thing on it, and that this had never happened to her before in any shape, and that the reason was extremely simple and had been available to her to notice at any point in four years.

Strength has never once been the shortage.

Every single problem she had ever had had a shortage in it and the shortage was never muscle. The shortages were a name, a hand on a book, a county that would certify a thing, a room she could stand in, an office with a stair.

She had the strength of four men and the camp did not have a job that needed four men's strength, and she had spent nine days walking to find out.

## What She Did Not Say

She did not say one word about the drift to the foreman or to the tender.

Not because she was managing him.

Because there was nothing to say that would not have been a version of what the man with the broken thumbnail had already said in front of him, and the two of them had been saying it for years and had never once been asked to account for it by anybody from outside.

She was the first person from outside who had stood inside a wire and seen it. She was also the last person in the world who was going to walk in there and start an argument about a two-season-old schedule with eleven men standing in it.

She did not offer to fix a schedule.

That was the other one and it was worse, because she had spent four years looking at schedules and she had an eye for one, and she stood in the ring at the seventh hour of a working day and looked at the last season's work going up the slope above her and could see inside about eleven minutes of what had not been done and when.

And she was not going to say it.

Because what would come out of that conversation is that a licensed one in the county of Lowwater had walked in and seen something eleven men paid by the day had not been able to say in nine years, and the answer would be: *no, ma'am* — which is how a place says a thing about itself, and it is not untrue, and it would be over.

She got the ring done by the light and she came out over the wire with her arm open and her left boot full and her breath at four, and the foreman paid her six coppers and wrote the ring in the column against her name with the mark of a cross against it, and the tender wrote the day's hours against his own board and did not look up.

That camp asked her nothing that day, and had been asking her nothing for nine days, and would go on asking her nothing.

She got no information out of anybody and asked for none. At the end of a day spent standing in four hundred yards of the worst ground on nine days by a woman who could have carried a sledge-load in either hand, there was not one single thing on that camp she could have done that a man of thirty could not have done at two hours' notice.
