# Run Lists

The wash house had eleven women on the floor and a girl at the trough who was paid four coppers a week to copy carriers' run lists, and since the road had shut there had not been a run list in this town for a hundred days.

That is the whole of what was wrong with the wash house.

It was not wrong because of the cold and it was not wrong because of the district fund and it was not wrong because of the eighth estate, which had answered four lines about a morning in four and had left the hours as the hours and put four coppers a week beside them for the cold months, and which pays for a splint and a crock and a girl and does not ask questions about either.

It was wrong because the road had shut on the seventeenth day past the last day of the thaw and a hundred days is a season of copying and a season of copying is four coppers a week.

She came in at about the seventh hour on the sixth morning with her hands in her coat, which is the way she had come into that room for about forty visits in a year, and stood in the girl's light.

“You are in my light.”

“You said that four days ago.”

“I say it every day you are in it,” said the girl, “and you come in it anyway, and one of those two facts is going to change and it is not going to be the light.”

## The Copies

The girl's name on her sheet was a name and the room called her something else and Ning Yan had been told both and had never once used either, because the girl had asked her in the autumn, in a lane, for one specific thing and the specific thing was not her name.

In front of her on the bench there were eleven sheets, and nine of them were blank, and the two that were not were blank in a different way.

The girl turned one round.

“Eleven,” she said. “That is a month of one day and a month of another, which is what the eighth estate’s standing order says a wash house copy is, two a week, and I have filled in the day and the sheet number and the name of the carrier on every one of them, because that is the sheet, and the rest of it is runs, and there are no runs.”

“There are none coming.”

“There are none coming because there is nothing to bring them,” said the girl. “A run list is not a thing anybody invents. It is what four men write down when they go out on a road and come back on it. There is no road and there are no men and there is nothing to write.”

She squared the sheets against the edge of the bench with one hand, in three motions, the way she did everything.

“I am paid four hours a week to copy two sheets,” she said, “and it takes me about nine minutes, and for a hundred days I have been paid four hours a week to copy two sheets that have a date and a sheet number and a carrier’s name on them and no road on them at all, and I have done them anyway, because the eighth estate pays for the sheet and not for the road.”

## The Notice On The Square

“I read the notice.”

“You read the notice a fortnight ago and you came down here the next day and you asked me if the carriers had said anything, and I said no. Then you came back, four days later, and you did not ask me. You just stood in my light.”

“You did not say anything the first time either.”

“I said no,” said the girl. “You asked me a question and I said no and you said thank you and left, which is a whole transaction and takes about nine seconds, and I have been standing here for two days waiting for the other half of it.”

Ning Yan put her hand on the bench.

“How much is an hour of your copying that is not on your sheet.”

The girl looked at her.

“That is a different question from the one you asked on the fifth,” she said.

“It is.”

“It costs four coppers.”

“It costs four coppers,” said Ning Yan, “and I am going to pay you four coppers out of a tin under a board in the corner of this room, in coin, now, for an hour of work that is not on your sheet and that the eighth estate will not know about and will not pay for.”

The girl put both forearms on the bench.

“Say that again with the part about the eighth estate in it slower,” she said, “because that is the part I care about.”

“The eighth estate will not know about it and will not pay for it.”

“Then it is mine and I will do it,” said the girl, “and you are not going to thank me, and I have told three washerwomen in the autumn that if you ever thank me I am going to be difficult about it in front of witnesses.”

She held out her hand and took the four coppers and put them in the pocket of her apron with the other money, which was a place a person keeps money they intend to spend on themselves.

## The Hour

An hour of copying is not an hour of copying when there are no run lists to copy.

It is an hour of a girl at a trough in a wash house going through a season of what carriers had written down for themselves, out loud, at a bench, for four coppers.

“There,” said the girl. “That one is the eleventh day past the last day of the thaw. He wrote the day and the sheet and the loads and the time he came back and the weather, and he wrote *pass open* at the bottom in a different pen, and that is the county notice, because the county notice is printed and the pen is not.”

“Go back.”

“I do not have to go back,” said the girl. “I have forty-one of them. They were in the crate when the crate came down in the autumn and I copied all of them then, when there was a reason, and I have sat on them for a hundred days since.”

“Why.”

“Because copying forty-one run lists takes four hours, and four hours is what I am paid, and I would not hand a week’s unused work to a wash house that does not read them.”

She put the third one down.

“And that one says *pass not clear, went down and back*,” she said, “and that is four days after the notice the county has on its shelf. And that one says *pass not clear* with nothing else on it, and that one is a week after the notice.”

The room was very cold and the fire in the wash house is a stove that a person is allowed to stand next to and not allowed to keep, and Ning Yan had her hands inside her coat and did not take them out.

“How many of the forty-one.”

“Seven that say the pass is not clear after the county says it is open,” said the girl. “Five of them are with nothing on them at all, and the five are the ones I would not put a name on.”

“That is not what I asked.”

“It is exactly what you asked,” said the girl, “because you asked whether the carriers believe the county, and they do not, and they have not for a hundred days, and it is not because they are liars. It is because a carrier cannot make a pass open by writing that it is open. He goes and finds out and then he writes it down, and the writing down is the only part of his job anybody in a county ever sees.”

## What It Buys

Four coppers and forty-one run lists and about an hour of a woman's time in a cold room.

It bought her the side-by-side of a notice on a public shelf in her own county against what seven men wrote at the bottom of forty-one sheets with a different pen, and the two did not agree, and the difference was about a week.

The notice said the road opens on the eighth of the ninth month.

The carriers said the pass does not clear until nearer a week after the notice, and five of them had said it by saying nothing at all.

She stood at a bench in a wash house and looked at that for about as long as it takes to count nine.

The county was not wrong about notices. A county posts a date because a county is required to post a date, and it posts it off a return from the district, and the return comes back off the district's own arithmetic, and it does not come back off a man standing on a pass in the dark with a lamp.

The carriers were the ones who went and looked.

## What It Costs

It also cost the thing she had not meant to pay, which is that she had come into that room with a question and had gone out of it having bought the answer, and the answer was not the one she had wanted.

She had wanted a road.

What she had bought was a disagreement between two sets of people who were both telling the truth, about a week wide, standing between her and a date she had already put her whole plan behind.

Four coppers had come out of the tin that morning and the projection had not moved for it, and four coppers was a shade under a day of stone, and it was now in an apron pocket with a girl who had arranged in advance not to be thanked and had been paid.

The stone ran out at the end of the month. The notice said the eighth of the next.

Seven days. And now seven days on the other side of the notice as well, if the carriers are right, which they have been every time anybody has thought to write it down.

She did not thank the girl. The girl had not asked to be thanked and would have made it awkward, and had arranged the whole of her working life over two years on the understanding that a woman with one arm and a god in her head does not come into a wash house and say thank you.

“You are standing in my light again,” said the girl.

“Yes.”

“You have been standing in my light for six days,” said the girl, “and you have not once told me what you are going to do when the road opens, and I am not asking, and I want it on the record that I am not asking.”

She put the sheets back in the pile and squared them against the bench in three motions.

“It will be the eighth of the next month according to a wall,” she said, “and it will be a week after that according to the only people who have to be there, and one of those two is a piece of paper and one of them is a man with a lamp, and I have found that the piece of paper has been wrong about the pass four times this winter and the man with a lamp has not been wrong once.”

She picked up the broom.

“You are in my light until the trough turn,” said the girl. “Then the district fund pays me and you may stand in it for nothing.”
