# Foundation One

The last of the ten was the twenty-third and the schedule went up on the same morning, and she was not in the room.

She had gone in the week before, once, on the strength of having asked once and been told no, and she stood in the doorway for about four minutes and then left, because there was nothing in the room that a woman standing in the doorway could do and staying in it would have made her the sort of person who stays in rooms.

So the number was read out of a wedge of paper on the morning of the twenty-third by a clerk whose job it is to turn a contractor's book into a schedule, and she was about four miles away on the ring, at the third hour, with a bar.

She never knew the number.

## What Came Back

It came back up the road on the ninth.

The foreman brought it to the trestle himself, which she understood by then was not unusual, and he put a sheet on the plank with a seal on it and a line of writing above the seal, and squared it, and went away without saying anything about it.

She stood there and looked at it.

A licence. Same paper. Same front, same fold, same crease worn at the defect line, the same seal, the same hand — or rather, not the same hand, because the last one was a registrar's and this one is not a person's.

The front of it read: **Foundation, stage one.**

And under it, in the space where a ceiling is written, in the same unhurried set of strokes that had put a line of office-name above the seal: a ceiling that had never existed on the front of any document in any office in the world.

There is nothing above nine. There had been nothing above nine on the front of the document anywhere in the world for four months, and a registrar had said so out loud in a room, and it was true, and it had stopped being true on the morning of the twenty-third by a hand that is not a man's.

The back of it was the back of it. Seven conditions on it, unrepealed, unre-listed, untouched — and she did not read it and did not need to, because she had read it once in a county office nine days and four hundred miles behind her and it had not changed and nothing in this world was ever going to change the last line of it.

Eight hundred jin. Seventh and last thing on the paper.

She held it between two fingers and a thumb and looked at it for about four minutes, standing at a trestle in the open on a working morning, and the whole crew were about two hundred yards off and not one of them came over.

## The Line

She ordered it.

There was no wording to it and there had not been a wording to it since the eighth stage in a cold quarter, and she did it the way she had always done it: she put it in the air the way a person says a thing out loud in an ordinary room where nobody needs anything.

[Ning Yan of the county of Lowwater, servant-born, under a ceiling written in her own hand. Foundation, stage one, is held.]

That was the whole of what went out of her.

Nine counties is what is heard, and nine counties had been hearing everything she had ever done since the eighth stage, in order and in the words of whoever read it out, and none of them heard this.

Four counties is what is written, and four county books carry a stage column, and four of those books did not have the first Foundation on it, and would not have it this month or next month, because the county that would write it was nine days west and had not been told and could not be told by anybody standing here.

It is not that something went wrong.

That was the thing she turned over most afterwards, on every one of the four days she had left on the course.

It was not a failure and it was not a malfunction and it was not the layer being broken. Announcements were heard inside the bound and went no further than the bound, and she was nine days outside it, and there was a room and she was not in it, and the whole of the arrangement was about where the room was.

Nobody out here has ever heard one. Not the foreman, not the tender, not the girl of nineteen, not one of the crew.

In the whole of the country she was standing in, in every life in it, not one person had ever heard a stage said out loud.

She had walked nine days out of a place where everything that has ever happened to her was said out loud to nine counties in the ordinary way, and she had come to a place where the largest thing that has ever happened to her can be said perfectly clearly and there is nobody within nine days who would know what it was.

It went about two hundred yards, into a field.

## The Cook-Fire

At the second hour they were at the fire — the crew, the girl, the foreman, all of them — and the argument was going, and she was at it.

She had the licence in her coat.

She stood behind a cart wheel about six feet off with the sheet in her hand and she had about a dozen sentences and every one of them was a lie by the standards of a place where people are paid by the day, and the truest of the twelve was that she was licensed above the ninth, and that was not a thing anybody there had a use for.

She said: “Do not tell me about the ring tomorrow.”

She said it in the ordinary voice, because that is what is said on a course. One of the men said something about the ring being finished on the high side, and the argument went on, and the whole of what she had said went past about nine people like water off a bar.

They were not people who asked. That was what nine days of it had taught her, and it was the only thing anybody out here had ever given her that she had not had to pay for.

That was it. That is the whole of the announcement as it was received.

Eleven men did not look up.

She put the licence in the oilcloth against her ribs, above a Clay Plate that had been there since the sixth day of a winter before any of this, and beside three pieces of dead weight ticket, and sat with her back to the cart wheel and let a fire that a dozen people were arguing beside warm the front of her.

## The Ten Days Before It

The ten days from the fourteenth of the month onward were the easiest ten days she worked in her life, and that is a thing she would not have believed if she had been told.

The column had her name in it and the column was counting, and a thing that is counting does not need anything else from her.

She got up and went over the wire. The foreman wrote the day in at the start of it, which she had worked out about a week ago and had said nothing about. The ring got cleared. She worked the ring and the bar and the high side, and the days went down in a hand that was not hers with a mark against every one.

Six coppers a day and a place at the fire and two flat cakes.

She was full and she was cold and she was the strongest body on that course and she was being paid six coppers to do the least useful thing on nine days, and she was, for about ten days, the least tired person in a country.

That is what an ordinary working life is and she had never had one and did not know that she was having one.

She thought about it on the eighth day, standing at a stake with a bar in her hands at the fourth hour, and what she felt about it was not gratitude and it was not happiness.

It was that a stage had been made out of ten ordinary days of a job that paid six coppers, and that the job had not changed at all, and that if there had been no ceiling anywhere on the front of any document she would have been at that stake on the eighteenth of the month doing the same thing for the same six coppers and would not have been one inch warmer.

## What It Cost

The cost is not the bracket.

It is four weeks of a body that has never been full and cold at the same time, and it starts that night and does not stop for four weeks, and there is no part of it that a county hour or a wage or a bed will take out of it.

Condensing made her full. Foundation puts a base under the full.

She had said all of that in her own body's words on three dead weight tickets and it was worth less than the number of weeks it had cost her, and what none of the three tickets had in it was the arithmetic of it, which was this.

Every hour that bought it was read out of a book she was not shown.

Not one of them. Ten days of them, read aloud in a room on the seventh of the month, into a wedge of paper, by a man who was under no obligation to let her stand in the middle of it and who did not, and the number went up, and the ceiling came down.

If he had been wrong by an hour, there would have been no way in the world to know.

Not by the foreman, who wrote what he wrote in his own book and never saw the wedge. Not by the eleven men. Not by the tender with the board. Not by her, who was four miles off on the ring at the third hour.

And nobody would ever know. It would be in a schedule in an office on the far side of everything and it would be correct as far as anybody could ever establish, because the only person who could check it is a woman who cannot read the number and was not in the room.

She had put her hand on every document of her life since she was eleven years old.

This one she had never touched.

## The Two Weeks Nobody Knows About

The following two weeks she did the ordinaryest thing there is to do, which is work.

She cleared the ring and moved a bar and was paid six coppers and put them in a tin that came out lighter, and she stood on ground she could not fix beside men who could, and she said nothing to any of them about anything.

There was no county in nine counties that knew she was at the first Foundation.

There was no county in nine counties that knew she was on a line nine days east of Lowwater at all. There was a sheet going west with MEMBER ABSENT in one column and nine days of good hours in the other, and there was a schedule that had gone up a stair with a counter above hers and above that not hers, and neither of those papers had her stage on it, because a stage went on a licence and a licence was in a coat.

So there are two books in the world that could be asked about her and neither of them would answer a question about today.

## What She Felt

She did not find it funny.

She wanted that written down because she had thought about it, because everybody who had ever come to her with a thing like this had wanted it to be a joke and it was not one.

She found it correct.

Everything that has ever happened to her inside that boundary was announced, in nine counties, in order, at the moment it happened.

She had hated it and hidden from it and used it and been hunted by it for four years.

And now the first Foundation of her life — the first one, the thing she paid for in a refusal and a claim and eight hundred jin and a year of not forgiving a man — is said out loud in a country with a light in it and a dog going and a child on a step.

And being correct is worse than being wrong, because being wrong would have been something somebody could fix.
