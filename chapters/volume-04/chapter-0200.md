# Three Marks On A Sheet

They were in a room at the west end of the camp with a fire in it and about a hundred sheets on a plank table, and they were signing other men's hours.

She knew two of the three before she was inside the door, and the third she had written on a sheet in a corridor at the west end of a furnace quarter a long way from here.

Lu Kang, Deng Chao and Pei An. Three men whose whole trade is going into four or five offices in a season and putting their names on other people's records.

There was a hundred sheets on that table and hers was going to be one of them.

That was all. That is not an event. A man who signs other men's hours for a living does it on a day like any other day, and there were a hundred sheets, and the bundle went into a satchel when the light went, and it went west on the first carrier.

She had come nine days east with a sentence ready for that room.

## What She Had Come To Say

It was a good sentence. It was about what a mark was worth and what a mark was for and that she was not a household and had no guarantor and had not come out here to sign anything on somebody else's behalf, and it was seven words long and it had been walked up and down nine days of road.

She never used it.

## The Room

It was a stone room with a fire in it and one plank table and two benches and a satchel on the wall, and there were about a hundred sheets on the table and a second fire outside doing nothing at all.

It was warm.

That was the first thing, and it was the thing she was not braced for. She had stood at a trestle in the open all day in the wind and she came into a room in the dark at the end of it that was warm, with a fire in it, and men in it, and three men writing, and nobody stood up.

For about four seconds she stood just inside the door and let it be warm.

She had been cold for nine days. She had had a bake and a tin and a flat wall with no roof on the third night, and a fire she could not reach in a county a fortnight ago. She had not been in a warm room with other people in it for the whole of that time and she had been telling herself it did not matter.

It mattered. Four seconds was enough to find out.

That was the only warm thing in nine days that had not cost her, and that was the whole of what the room gave her.

They asked her nothing. Not one question in that room, and she found out afterwards that this was not a courtesy: there was nothing on a sheet like hers for them to ask.

“Sit there,” Pei An said, and put the top sheet in front of her, and turned round.

She gave them the sheet. There is a form of words for that and it took her about four seconds and none of them looked up.

## Three Marks

Deng Chao read it and did not read it twice, which she noticed, because she had watched a hall clerk read her name four hundred times and read it twice every time.

He wrote his mark.

He wrote his mark on a sheet that said a woman was licensed in a county nine days west, and he did not ask her a single thing about the county, or about the licence, or about the hours the sheet said she had put in, and he did not look at the hours.

Lu Kang did not read the sheet at all. He put his mark on it and signed his own name beside it.

Pei An did read the sheet. He read it all the way down including her line, and he turned it round and pushed it across, and said the only sentence in that room that was in any way about what she was doing.

“You should know what it is worth,” he said.

“How much is it worth.”

“It is worth what the other ninety-nine are worth,” he said, “and the other ninety-nine are worth what the course's week is worth, and the course's week goes up a stair on the carrier and comes back down on the next one, and I have never seen a mark of mine do one thing more than another.”

He put the nib down.

“You have not bought a name. You have bought a week.”

## Nobody Was Thanked

There was no charge and she paid none, because there was not one to make.

She did not thank them.

She stood at a plank table at the west end of the camp and put a sheet back into a bundle of about a hundred other sheets, and she did not thank three men.

It was not coldness and it was not pride. It is that a thing done in the ordinary course of a man's trade is not a gift, and there is a place at the back of a person who has been handed one gift and not another on the same day where the two of them get kept apart.

One of them — Lu Kang, who had not read the sheet — put his hand flat on the bundle on his way past.

“Same day next week,” he said. “We will be here.”

They were not there for her.

There was a bundle on the table and there were three men whose trade it was to mark sheets, and they were marking sheets, and it was not any day in particular and it was not about any of them.

That was the whole of it, and she found it later the hardest of anything about the nine days: she had walked all of them east to pay three men for something that a man's ordinary week was worth.

## What They Were Actually Like

She had time while the bundle went into the satchel and she used it, because there was nothing else to do with it.

Deng Chao had a cough and was not going to admit to it and put his sleeve over his mouth twice in ten minutes. Lu Kang wrote every mark in exactly the same shape, which is not easy and is not common, and had been doing it long enough that the nib had a flat on it.

Pei An was the one who read and the one who had told her what it was worth, and when she looked at the sheet again after the satchel was closed she could see why: he read everything, including the parts that had nothing to do with what he was signing, and he did it at the speed of a man checking a pair of boots.

Three men, three trades, one instrument, a hundred sheets, an ordinary evening.

None of them had done her a kindness and none of them had refused her anything, and she understood in that room that these are the two faces of the same thing and that she had spent four years meeting only the second one.

She took the sheet back with three marks on it and went out into the dark.

The cold outside went into her within about forty steps and she found, in the middle of that walk of forty steps, that she was disappointed, and worked out immediately that she had no right to be, because nothing whatever had been offered to her and she had not asked.

She had wanted the room to cost her something.

## Afterwards

She wrote it on the back of a dead weight ticket, in her own hand, with a pencil stub, by the fire outside the camp, with the heel of her left hand on the corner of it.

Not the one from the quarry. She had more than one. A weight ticket is the thing a place that weighs things gives you when you do not need the weight.

She wrote the three marks in a corner, and she wrote a line, and then she put the ticket inside the oilcloth against her ribs, above the plate, beside the column of marks and the working name.

The sheet itself she did not have. She had handed it to them and it had gone into a satchel and the satchel was on a wall and the satchel was going west on a carrier in the morning, and the three marks on it were three marks on a hundred other sheets in a bundle, and she had nothing in her hand except a piece of board-pressed paper that a foreman had written her name on at a trestle for no reason at all.

That is worth understanding on its own. There are exactly two documents in her life now that any office on either side of anything could get hold of, and both of them are in somebody else's satchel on a wall.

The line said: **A sheet with three marks on it is worth a week and not a name.**

She looked at it for a while after she had written it, because it was the first true thing about the road that she had been able to put down in one line, and because it was nine days old and it had taken a hundred sheets and three men and a room with a fire in it to arrive at.

She had wanted a sponsor, and she had prepared to pay for one, and she had budgeted a purse that no longer existed, and she had written the sentence on the flat by the fourth bridge on the second day in the dust behind a stone load with the driver looking at his near ox.

She had a week.

Somewhere west of here, up a stair that she could not climb, was the office that could put a countersignature on a thing, and the thing needed a schedule of hours worked at the ceiling asked for, and the schedule was being kept, in order, with dates on it, on a plank table nine days east by a man who was owed nothing by anybody.

She did not know what the three marks would be worth to that office yet.

She had a feeling it was going to be exactly what a man who signs other people's hours had told her it was worth, and she went to bed on it.
