# What a Question Costs

The ration went wrong in the third week of the month and she let it.

The rule on the back of a dead weight card is a stroke through a row when it is spent. She had kept it for about a week.

On the second of those seven days she came in off the floor at the ninth hour having used three. On the sixth she came in having used two. On the seventh she came in having used one and sat on the mat in the cold and looked at the card.

The card had four marks in every row of the first few days and then it had three, and then two, and then one, and she had not decided to make it do that.

That is what he had said in the yard. He had said she was going to be extremely bad at this, and she had been bad at it in exactly the way he had said, which is that she did not waste him on things she wanted to know, she wasted him on things she did not need to know, and the thing he meant by a day running out was a day she had already spent at a cold end on a question about a cold end.

She had four rows of one mark on it and she was not going to finish the seventh with a stroke through it, because there had not been four things worth spending and she was not going to pretend there had been to make the card come out even.

## The Question

The road is the only thing left in her calendar.

Not the quarry and not the wall behind a well and not the four loads a week she has stopped buying. The road. What it does past the fourth bridge, and how much of it there is, and what is on the other side of a bend in it.

She has nine counties of carriers' word and one county notice and a girl with forty-one run lists in a pile in a wash house, and she has been inside the bound her entire life, and everything she knows about what is east of the fourth bridge is a road name.

He can tell her in a breath.

That is the whole of what it is. She does not need eight days for it and she does not need four and she does not need one. She needs a fraction of what she spent on a name, and a name was the last of the eight.

It would buy her a map. Not a drawn one — a thing put into words, from the inside, in the voice of a man who has never once been wrong about a shape.

## The Moment It Would Be Easiest

She was on the mat at about the ninth hour on the sixth of those seven nights and the fire had gone down twice and had not been fed since the fourth hour and nine men were asleep in twelve bunks and there were three marks gone from the day.

“Ledger,” said Ning Yan. “Now. Before you price it.”

He did not say the price.

She said it first, and that was the whole of what happened, and she had not planned it and would not have been able to explain afterwards why she had done it in that order.

“No,” said Ning Yan.

There was a silence on a mat in a cold room with nine men asleep in it that lasted about as long as it takes to count nine.

“No,” she said again. “Not at the sixth of seven nights in a cold room with thirty coppers under a board and a road I have never walked. Not tonight, and not in the next four days, and if you put the price in the air now I will not hear it.”

He did not put it in the air.

“That was expensive,” said Yuan.

“I know what it was.”

“You do not, because you have never once worked out what you are refusing, and I have watched you refuse four things on a mat in the last week and you have not worked out one of them,” he said. “That is the first of those five I have ever agreed with you about and I would like it recorded that it was not agreement.”

“It is recorded. Do not improve on it.”

“I am not going to,” he said. “I am going to be quiet now, and you are going to spend two days of me on paper, and paper is cheaper and slower and it will get you to the fourth bridge in the same season.”

She did not explain why.

That was the whole discipline and it was the only one that had ever worked between them: she was not going to tell him the reasons, because the reasons were that she was afraid of what a map would do to a licence with nothing above nine on the front of it anywhere in the world, and he had never once been able to hear a reason without treating it as a cost to be entered.

## What A Carrier Knows

She got it out of paper in two days.

Forty-one run lists in a wash house, which she had already paid four coppers for once and could look at for nothing after that because the girl copies them and shows them to whoever stands in her light.

And two carriers at a board behind a well, on the second afternoon, for nothing, because Da Shan has a wall and two of his customers are alive and lift and are in his yard most afternoons and will talk about a road to a woman with a sledge if she stands still long enough.

Here is what a carrier knows about a road.

He knows the loads and he knows the days. He knows where a cart has to turn and where a sledge has to be taken off and carried, and he knows which side of a cutting holds snow in a bad month, and he knows the name of every inn between here and wherever he has been, and he knows which of them will not take a woman alone and which of them will.

He knows a place past the fourth bridge where the road stops being the same width.

That is all. That is the whole of what came out of both of them in an afternoon and it is worth more than anything a man in her head could have said about it in a breath, because it has been checked by people who have to get a sledge over it.

And none of it was about anybody.

Not one sentence of it was about a man or a name or a household or a line in a county book. It was about loads, and days, and a place where a cart turns, and a woman alone, and snow on the north side of a cutting.

She stood in a lane in Lowwater at about the fourth hour with forty-one sheets in her head and a carrier's afternoon in her head, and she worked out that the road she has to walk is a road with an ordinary shape and ordinary difficulties and no surprises in it at all, and that a voice in her head could have told her the same in about nine seconds.

## The Other Thing The Lists Show

And then, on the second afternoon, with the forty-one, she found the thing she had not gone looking for.

Thirteen of the forty-one are runs out of this county that do not come back.

Not runs that were cut short and not runs where a carrier turned round. Runs that go out, and go a long way, and end, and come back from somewhere further on, with the loads written at the far end in a different hand.

She had read those thirteen twice in a hundred days at a bench in a wash house for four coppers an hour and had taken them for thirteen men who happened to be sent further than the others.

They are not thirteen men who happen to be sent further.

Every carrier who goes past a certain point on that road goes on doing the same work and every one of them comes back from somewhere past it. The lists stop having a far end. The date is written at the top and the loads are written at the bottom and the middle of the sheet, for the whole of the run, is empty, and a sheet with an empty middle is a sheet about a piece of road that is not on it.

She sat on the bench in the wash house and read it four times.

There was nothing on that page about what was past it. There was not a word, and not a name, and not a shape, and not a single thing in forty-one sheets written by thirteen men who were good enough at their job to keep a run list.

What the lists said was that the carriers do not run past that point, and the reason they do not is not that the road is bad.

It is that there is nothing on the far side of it for them to run to.

That is a fact about carriers. It is not a fact about what is over there and it is not an answer to anything, and it took her four readings to be sure that a list which stops is not a description of what it stopped at, and she was sure of it, and she wrote nothing down, and she went up the hill.

## The Third Flight

On the seventh of the seven nights she went down the hill at about the eighth hour because the quarters were the coldest room she had been in for a week and she had a week of her own cold in her.

She got to the tower and went up the first flight and up the second and up the third, on three counts, and stood on the landing with the window in it that does not close.

Two flights and a landing from the warmest room in this county.

Above her, on the fourth floor, a man of sixty-two was sitting at a long table with a lamp on it with a cleaned chimney on it, writing down a thing that had happened somewhere out of nine counties and would go up four flights in a district clerk's arms in the spring.

She stood on that landing for about four seconds.

She was not going to go up. There was nothing to go up for and no version of going up that she could have explained, and a woman who has been rationing four sentences a day on a dead weight card does not walk into a warm room to be warm.

She went back down the third flight on three counts and out into the cold and up four miles to a room with nine men asleep in it and a fire that was a bed of ash and had been a bed of ash since the fourth hour.

## What It Turned Out To Cost

The thing she found out in seven days in a cold room she had made on purpose was this.

A sentence is cheap.

That is the whole of it and it is the opposite of what she had been pricing. A sentence is cheap and has been cheap the entire time, and she had spent eight days on a name because the name was worth eight days and not because eight days was a lot.

The expensive thing is a question about the far side.

Everything she wanted to know cost a day and a half, and she said no to it on a mat, and the way she found out what the road was going to be — in two days, in paper, in a wash house and at a board behind a well — cost her four coppers she had already spent once and about four hours of a woman's time that she had been given and four miles of road twice.

The discipline was not do not waste him. She had had that right for a week and it was the wrong rule and it is why the card had four marks in every row of it.

The rule is: do not buy a thing with him that a carrier will say out loud in a yard.

She wrote that on the back of the dead weight card under the name, in her own hand, at about the ninth hour on the seventh of seven nights, in a room where the fire was a bed of ash and nine men were asleep and not one of them knew that a man of twenty-two had filled a bucket himself two days earlier and had said nothing.
