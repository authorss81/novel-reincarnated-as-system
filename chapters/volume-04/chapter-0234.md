# The Hour Without A Line

Twenty-eight days. She had been counting them off the back of a dead weight ticket since the ninth of the month before in about the time it takes to write a line with the flat of a second finger, and on the seventh of this month at about the fourth hour she stood in a hut with three boards in it with the bar against the wall and said the word.

She said it the way she has said every one for two years, which is that she says it and does not hold it and lets it go.

This time it did not go.

She did not notice for about two seconds, because it has never once not gone, and then she stood in the dark with her hand flat on the Clay Plate under the oilcloth and worked out what had happened and it came out in about a breath.

She had said the word to him and he had not let it out of the room.

She did not tell him to.

## Not Telling Him

That is the part she has gone over the most since, and she went over it on the seventh, on the eighth and again on the ninth, and it came out the same every time, and what it came out is that there was no moment in it where she decided.

She has ordered him not to decide anything about her without telling her first, and she did not decide this either. She made a word and did not put it into the air, and she has done that twice before in four years and both times she had gone in and had it out again, and this time the base under her came up the last step while she was standing in the dark not saying anything.

She thought about saying it out loud to him after the fact — *let it go* — for about as long as it takes to count out a day's coppers, and she did not, and what stopped her was not pride and was not the account.

What stopped her was that a stage is worth something to the layer and it is worth something to the nine counties and it is worth nothing at all to the far end of the working reach, and there is one announcement in four years that a board two hundred yards behind her has ever taken, and she had been carrying that date against her ribs since a month that is four months back.

She wanted it on that board. She wanted it there more than she had ever wanted nine counties to hear it.

That was the reason. She wrote it out on the back of the ticket in four minutes and eleven minutes and did not write it a second time.

## What Went In

An hour worked.

That is what there is. On the seventh of a month, from about the fourth hour to about the seventh, on a stretch past the cart turn that is not on anybody's book, in the hand of a woman with the flat of a second finger on a dead weight ticket in an oilcloth against her ribs.

It is the ninth hole in a column and the holes have been there for four years and they are the ordinary shape of a person who has done something out of order, and this one is not out of order. This one is exactly on time and the fourth stage column in four county books is going to read one, two, three, five, six, seven, eight, nine, and there is no book in nine days of road that has heard the eighth.

She worked the day.

She went out at about the fourth hour and she was on the low side past the turn by the fifth and she put the bar in about eleven feet from the cut and got two feet up in one movement and put it down on its side, and the ground answered her boot ten times that day and told her nothing about any of them.

The base underneath her went another step heavier and colder in the course of the morning and she was at four counts of breath before the second hour and she stayed at four counts for the rest of it and did not go in.

Eight hours of it past the turn and four hours on the mark that is painted on the plank, which is the eleventh hour of a day, and the eight past the turn are not in anybody's book and the four on the plank are in a man's at the fourth hour and the day is one day.

That is what she had bought with a day out of three at a trestle on the fifth, and it is not more hours than she was getting, and it is a different nine days of them.

## The Width Of It

At the far end of the working reach, past the last stretch anybody attends and past a yard with stone in it, there is a plank on a post.

There is a narrow column at the end of that plank with no heading on it, and a man has been putting strips up against the back of it off a cart for nine years, and nothing within nine days of road has ever filled it except him.

On the seventh of that month he put one up.

He put it up the way he puts all of them up. He ruled a line, he tore a strip off the same cloth square against a rule, and he wrote the date at the left the way he has written every date for nine years, and the rest of the line he did not fill, and he pushed it into the gap between the plank and the post where the weather does not get at it.

He did not know what the width of the gap meant. Nobody could, standing there.

And it was the width of a stage, because that is how wide a gap is when you are copying onto a board you do not own and you are copying what arrives and not deciding anything.

## Too Late

She found out about it on the twenty-second, when she went and stood in front of it, and by then it had been up fifteen days and it was going to stay up.

That is the mistake and it is the only one she made in the batch, and she understood it in about four minutes standing at a post in the ordinary voice of a woman talking to herself, and she wrote it out flat and plain at the back of a dead weight ticket and read it back to herself twice.

*You have put a hole on a board you cannot read.*

That is all of it. There is not one page of paper in nine days of road she can get to that would show her that column, and she has known since the seventeenth of a month four months back that she cannot read a word inside it, and she told nobody she had told the difference between a line and a reading.

She cannot take it back.

She could have let it go in the ordinary way on the seventh at about the fourth hour in a hut with three boards in it, and it would have been heard by nine counties inside about a second and by a board on this course inside about a second after that, and by nothing at all past the last stake, and there would be no gap on the post.

She wanted one line out there. She got one line out there, and the line is a hole, and it is dated, and a man who has never heard an announcement in his life has put a keeper's gap up in his own hand for a thing he cannot see.

The three empty dates in a wage-book at a trestle are honest and they can be pointed at.

This one cannot.

## What She Is Carrying

At the seventh hour she came back past the cart turn and put the bar against the hut and stood at the plank while the man with the boot worked at a thing that wanted doing twice.

“Twenty-two,” he said. “You have been out there since the fifth.”

“Yes.”

“You are not saying your hours to me,” he said, “and I would not know what to do with them if you were.”

“No.”

He pulled the thread through the boot and bit it off and did not look up.

“You have got a hole in that man’s book,” he said, “and he has squared it twice now and he is not going to fill it, and you are out there making another one in a thing I cannot see.”

She looked at him.

“I do not know where the hours go,” he said. “That is a true thing and I have thought about it for five days and I am saying it because you have said your name out loud twice at this plank in front of me and I would like to have said it out loud once.”

Then he went back to the boot.

## At Eight Stages

She was Foundation eight when the light went, and the base under her was heavier and colder and slower than it had been at the seventh, and she was the strongest body on nine days of road and the slowest hand on any stretch anybody had hired her onto, and both of those had been true for two months and this was the first day she had held both of them without something in her closing up.

She lay down on three boards with five counterfoils and three dead weight tickets and one mark left on the back of one of them, and she did not spend the mark, and she put her hand flat on the Clay Plate through the oilcloth.

Twenty-two days to the ninth. Twenty-one by the morning.

One stage short of finished, and she had just made a hole on a board she cannot read that a man on the far side is going to keep for nine years, and nothing she does between now and then is going to put a line in it.
