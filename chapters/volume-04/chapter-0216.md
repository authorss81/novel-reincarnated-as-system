# The Last Column

The board is two planks set on end props and it is not one board.

She had been writing on it for three days in the last month of the month before and had looked at about sixty feet of it, and the last month of this month she had nothing to do with it and had gone round to the other end of it twice and had not read it.

The far half of the board is a separate plank, a narrower one, let into the near one at about the sixtieth foot, and the join is not flush — it stands about half an inch proud, because one plank came from a cart and one plank was cut on the ground, and somebody let it in about nine years ago and did not bother after that.

## The Last Column On It

At the far end of the far plank there is a column.

It is narrower than the columns on the near plank. It is ruled on its own, separately, and there is no heading over it in anything she could read, and it does not carry the painted marks, and no cell in it has ever been filled by anybody on that course.

She had looked at it three times in three weeks, in between a job and a trestle, and on the seventeenth of the month she gave it an hour of the fourth because it was the seventeenth and there was a cart due.

## What Fills It

A strip.

It is about four inches wide and it is the same cloth the near plank is made of — a coarse oiled stuff, the stuff a contractor makes a wage-book cover out of — and it comes into the camp off a cart.

The cart comes off the far end of the working reach, past the last mark any of them attends, once every so often, driven by a man who is not on any roster at that camp and whose cart is not identified on that course's board and does not appear in any book at that trestle.

The strip is cut to the width of the column and it is pushed into the gap between the plank and the prop and it goes up against the back of the board where the weather does not get at it.

It has been going into that column for nine years.

That is Ma Sheng's whole account of it, given standing at the trestle in the ordinary voice on the seventeenth, in about as long as it takes to count out a day's coppers.

“It comes off the cart and it goes up and it is not for us,” he said. “It has been going up since before I came, and the one before me used to put his up too, and I have never asked what it is and neither did he, and it is not ours and we do not fill it.”

“Who does.”

“That is not on my board.”

## What She Could Read

She could read the frame of it and not one thing inside it, and the difference between those two things is the difference between a hand and a language.

Dates at the left. That is the first thing and she had that in about a minute: they are dates and they are in the same order the dates come in and they are spaced the way dates are spaced, which is about the same as any other.

Then the spacing of what is on the right of them, and it was not the spacing of readings.

She knew what a reading looks like written down because she has been writing them on the near plank for three days with the flat of a second finger and a line takes her eleven minutes where the tender takes four. A reading is yards and it is written the way a man says it, and the figures in that column were not written the way a man says a thing about ground.

And the marks down the right-hand side of it were not painted marks. There was one in most of the lines and it was a figure, and it was not a measurement of anything she could put a name to.

Nine years of lines.

She stood at the far end of a nine-day line at about the fifth hour and looked at nine years of a thing that somebody past the last stake has been copying onto a board, and she could not read one word of what any of it said.

## The One Line That Was Different

There is a line near the bottom of it with a date on it that is the thirteenth of this month.

She looked at that for a long time and she did not put her finger on it and she did not get closer to it.

It is in a different form from the nine years of lines above it and below it. It is shorter than the lines around it. The marks on it are not the same marks. There is a figure in it that is not the figure that the other lines have in the same place.

She is the only person within nine days of road who can tell the difference between that line and a reading.

That is a thing about arithmetic and not about paper. A reading is a number of yards worked at a place on a day by a man, and every line for nine years is that, and she had been reading that kind of number for four years in nine counties and could not be shown one of them by mistake.

And this one is the thirteenth of this month, which is a day she knows the whole of, because she was on the low side of the thirty-nine at about the fourth hour of that day with a bar in her hands and a jar of oil in her coat and she put a thing into the air and about two hundred yards of a country heard it that has never heard one.

She told nobody that she had told the difference.

She did not tell the foreman. She did not tell the tender. She did not say the words *there is a date in there that is mine* to any person on that course, and there is nobody within nine days of road to say them to.

She did not say out loud what the line was.

## What Nine Years Of It Looks Like From The Wrong End

She came at it sideways over three days, the way you come at a thing you are not going to be allowed to read.

On the nineteenth she counted the lines and stopped counting at about eleven hundred and did not finish, because a woman standing at a board counting lines for an hour is a woman doing something at a board.

On the twenty-first she worked out that the strip does not come on the days a cart comes with anything else. It comes on its own, and it comes about once in ten days, and it is at the back of the cart and not under a load.

On the twenty-third she stood at the far end of the plank at the fourth hour and put her eye close enough to the join to see the edge of the last strip going under the prop, and it was torn off square and not cut, which means somebody tears it against a rule and not with a knife.

That is the whole of what she got for three days of standing at the far end of a board.

## Her Hand Was On It

Her hand was on it that evening, in a hut with a mat and three boards in it, and she did not order it, and there was nobody in that hut to know she had wanted to.

That is the whole of it and it is nine seconds long and there is no price attached to it anywhere, and it was not the second time she had refused a thing and it was not going to be explained here what the standing is or where it came from or what it costs, because it is hers to order and his to hold only on her word and she had not said the word.

What she went over afterwards, on the mat, in the dark, was not the wanting.

It was how quick it was.

She had gone to that plank on the seventeenth with about an hour of a working morning and a whole question in her head, and she had come off it at about the seventh hour with the question still in her head and nothing at all spent, and there had not been a moment in it where she had argued with anybody.

That was the cold of it.

She had expected — she had got a coat on for it, in her mind, the way she gets a coat on for weather — to find that refusing was heavy. She had found out in the time it takes to count eleven that it is the easiest thing she does.

That is what it is to have something on the edge of you that you have not used. It sits there. You can get at it whenever you want. You do not have to decide anything to have it, and the not-deciding is free, and it is free, and that was the whole price of it, and she had been carrying a free thing around inside her for four years and had never once made it do anything.

A piece of information about herself, going into a book that somebody past the last stake keeps, in a form that is not a reading, dated the day she took a stage.

Nine years of lines in a column that nobody on this course can read and that nobody on this course has ever asked about, and a keeper on the far end of the working reach who has been putting them up by hand, off a cart, for nine years.

And the last of them is hers.

She does not know what a thing is when it goes in that column. She has four years of documents in nine counties and not one of them is about anything except what she has done, and every one of them was made by somebody else and put in a room she could not get into, and every one of them was read out to nine counties at once.

Whatever this is, it is not announced. She knows that much, standing at a plank at the seventh hour, with about two hundred yards of the whole country on each side of her.

She had a licence in an oilcloth against her ribs with a ceiling on the front of it she had never touched, and a claim on the back of it, and a stage four past the front of the paper.

And somewhere past the last stake, on a board in the open, in a hand that nine years of this country cannot read, there is a date, with a figure in it that is not yards, on the day she took a stage.

She went over the wire at the fourth hour in the morning and worked the low side of the forty for eleven hours, and the ground under the bar made the noise it makes, and she did not say one word about any of it to anybody.
