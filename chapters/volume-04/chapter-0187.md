# The Price of a Name

She waited a day.

That was the whole of the waiting. She had named a price on a mat four nights earlier and had not said the word, and the next day came and went with a bar in her right hand and forty feet of a setting bench that does not get the sun until the second hour.

At about the ninth hour on the twelfth night of this month she lay down on the mat in the second bunk with nine men asleep and the fire a bed of ash and said it.

Price first. Again. Out loud, in the ordinary voice, to nobody.

“Eight days out of about ten.”

“Yes,” said Yuan.

“Eight days,” said Ning Yan, “and about two after it, and I know what two is worth on the day after and not before.”

“Price before the word,” he said. “Say the word.”

“Ledger.”

## What It Is Like

It was not like anything.

That is the honest account and she had been told four times over two summers that it would be like something, and it was not. There was no light and no cold and no place. There was a man in her head going further away at a steady rate for what turned out to be most of a night, and she lay on a mat with her right hand open on the oilcloth and felt the exact moment at which he stopped going.

It stopped on its own somewhere in the small hours.

It had never done that. At the reading in the middle of the summer it had run for most of a day and stopped by itself then as well, and a man who has been doing a thing for a year and a half would expect the stopping to be a thing he did, and it was not.

He put it down.

That is what a Ledger does when it is finished. He stopped holding it, and it stopped being held, and it was gone into whatever the layer accounts him in, and there was no moment at which he said *I am putting it down*.

He said nothing about it at all. He did not say he was sorry and he did not say thank you and he did not offer to hold it again.

That last part she noticed. She noticed it in the dark, on a mat, at about the fourth hour, lying on her side with nine men breathing, and she lay there and understood that she had spent eight days of him on a name and the purchase had included, at no charge, a promise that he would not do it twice.

## What Came Back

It came back in about a quarter of a day, which is to say that there was a gap and then there was the thing, and the gap is not part of it.

A hand.

Not her hand, not a hand at the tower, not a hand in the county office on the square, not the roundhouse hand that wrote in a room in this county twenty years ago. A working hand, a right hand, a hand that has written several thousand dates and has never once written a name.

It wrote one date into a counting room in Lowwater on a sheet that belonged to a creditor she had never met, against an eight hundred jin claim that was the seventh and the last thing on the back of a licence nailed over her head in a room four miles up a hill.

The room is in this county. The room is in this town. It is about nine minutes' walk from a wash house where a girl with a crooked leg is paid four coppers a week, and she had never been inside it, and she could walk to its door this morning in the cold and would be turned out of it by a man with a list of who is not admitted.

## The Name

**Luo Ping.**

Thirty-nine. A schedule-man, which is a working trade in the nine counties and is not a rank of anything: a man who keeps a schedule for somebody else's office and is sent to put the dates into it. He had been doing it since he was about twenty-three, which was sixteen years, and he did four or five offices in a season and there were men who did it for forty years and were still doing it.

He had a wife. He had a boy of eleven.

And they were not here.

That is the part of it that took her an hour to get round to on a mat in the cold, and it is the part that the eight days were for, and she had not known when she said the word that it was going to be this.

The household was past this county's boundary. East, on the working side of a line this county draws and seals and posts dates about on a public shelf. He was at the counting room on this side and his wife and his boy were on the other, and that is ordinary, and it is not ordinary, and it is a fact about a man's household and not about anything else, and there was no line in what she bought that separated the two.

He was a man with a family. That is what eight days came back with.

## What It Did Not Buy

She went through it on the mat, out loud, in the ordinary voice, one at a time, so that it would be in the room and not only in her head.

Not her.

Not the eight hundred jin.

Not the date, which is still in the counting room in Lowwater in the hand of a man whose wife and boy live past this county's boundary, and the date is a date to answer by, and it is still there and it has not been torn off and nothing about it has changed.

Not the reason. Not who sent him to write it. Not why a working man with a family was in a creditor's counting room at all with a stranger's claim on a sheet and a date against it, which is not a thing a schedule-man does for nothing and not a thing anybody has told her.

And not the answer on the shelf. Not the space the width of a line on a public shelf in a county office on a square in this town. That space belongs to an office above this county and an office above this county does not read a stranger's ledgers.

“Nothing,” said Ning Yan. “Tell me the whole of what that bought and be exact about it, because I am going to be carrying it for a season and I do not want to be carrying something that is not there.”

“It is a name, and a household on the far side of a line that is a line to a county book and not to a man,” said Yuan. “That is the whole of it. That is what is there. There is no more of it and I have not kept anything back from you, and I want that on the record because you are about to do something very stupid with it and I would rather you did the stupid thing knowing the size of what you are holding.”

## The Price On The Page

She said it out loud, which she had not intended to do.

“Eight days out of about ten,” she said, “and about two, and that is what is left of him.”

“Yes,” said Yuan. “About two.”

“No,” said Ning Yan. “You are not going to have that number. That number is mine and I will spend it on a day and a time and I will not say it out loud on a mat at the fourth hour so that there is a second person in the world who has heard it.”

He did not argue.

## The Afternoon

She got up at about the eighth hour because the crew gets up at about the sixth and there is no version of a woman sleeping past the sixth in a room that holds ten.

She had been on the mat since the ninth hour of the night before with the cold coming through the floor and the fire gone twice, and the light was on the mountain by the time she came out of it, and the day was an ordinary day and went the way ordinary days go when a person has had no sleep in them.

She went down the hill with an empty hand.

She did not go to the county office. She did not go to the counting room, which she had never been inside, and she stood at the bottom of the hill in the second hour of the afternoon and worked out, before she had her breath back, that she was not going to be let in, and that even if she were let in there would be nothing in the room for her.

So she went to the wash house and stood in the girl's light for an hour and a half without buying anything, and the girl let her, and at the end of it the girl said:

“You have not bought anything and you have not asked me anything and you have stood in my light for ninety minutes.”

“Yes.”

“You are going to tell me about it another day,” said the girl, “and I am not going to be here when you do, because I am on the trough turn until the end of this week and then the district fund pays me and you may stand in it for nothing.”

“I know,” said Ning Yan.

## The Flatness

The flatness came about half an hour later and she had not expected it.

She had bought a name. She had said a price out loud before the word in the ordinary voice to nobody, and the price had gone, and a man called Luo Ping with a wife and a boy had come back, and she was lying on a mat in the second bunk with a fire that was not there and nine men asleep and a licence over her head.

That is all that happened. There was no sound, and no cold, and nothing in her body, and she had got up two winters ago in a warehouse off a lane with two witnesses in the room and had been sick afterward for a day and a half, and she was not sick now and she was not elated and there was nothing at all in her hands.

It was the flatness of a person who has bought a thing at a price she agreed to in advance and is holding it.

That was the first part.

The second part came after that, and it was worse, and it came in the small hours when the room was at its coldest and she had been awake for about three hours with it.

## A Name Is Not A Ledger Entry

A name is not a ledger entry.

Everything she had done for nineteen months had been a ledger entry. Her name was an entry. Her hours were entries. The claim was an entry. The stage column was an entry with three holes in it and the holes were entries too, because a county writes down an absence as carefully as it writes down a stage, and she had learned that in a low room in front of twenty people.

She had bought an entry this morning. That was what eight days bought: one line in a place she could not go and read, about a man she had never met, in exchange for most of the only voice she had.

And what came back was not a line.

It was a man of thirty-nine with a wife and a boy of eleven who live on the other side of this county's boundary, and it is not possible to hold that as a line, and it is not possible to spend it as a line, and it is not possible to put it in a column and cross a day off against it.

He was somebody's husband and somebody's father. He had been at that for eleven years and for six of them he had been going in and out of a boundary to do it. Somebody in that house washes his clothes. Somebody in that house knows what he is doing today.

And she had bought his name out of a man in her head at eight days of him, on her word, in a cold room, and said the price out loud first, and now she had to decide what she was going to do with it, and the decision was hers and could not be given back.

She lay on her side in the cold and did not say anything for about as long as it takes to count nine.

Then she got up and fed the fire, because it was down, and lay down again, and did not sleep, and did not write anything down, because she had nothing to write it on except a dead weight card and a rule about what the dead weight card was for.

Eight days of him, gone.

A name, in her head, that was not an entry.

Luo Ping, thirty-nine, a schedule-man with a boy of eleven and a wife who is not in this county, somewhere east of a boundary that has a date on it on a public shelf in a building four miles from where she was lying.
