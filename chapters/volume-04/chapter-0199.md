# The Wage-Book

On the second morning the foreman put the book down on the plank in front of her, open, at a page with about forty entries in it, and turned round and walked away.

He was under no obligation to do that.

A book that a foreman keeps is his own instrument and the wages in it are his own money, and a hired hand is a hired hand until the page is squared, and the ordinary thing about a book is that it is shut when it is not being used.

He had opened it because her hours were worth having straight.

That is his whole reason and he did not dress it up, and when she said so later in the season he said that dressing it up was for men who needed to be liked.

## Four Columns

She read the whole page and then she stopped reading it and stood very still.

Four columns.

The first was a date. The second was a stretch, written in a shorthand with numbers in it, and beside every stretch on the page was a small painted mark of some kind, a bar and a cross and a ring, and she worked out standing there that a mark stood for a place.

The third was hours.

The fourth was a number of coppers, and beside it, in the same width of the page, a wider space with nothing in it on most lines.

She read the fourth column across nine lines and then a hundredth of what she had learned in four years arrived at once, standing at a trestle in the open air.

The price column and the reason column are the same column.

That is what a fourth column with nothing in it on most lines is. It is not a place for a second number. It is a place where somebody may put a word, and it is the only place on the page where a word fits, and the hours go in the page on the same lines.

She read it twice and did not know what it was going to cost her, and that is a thing that only happens to a person about four times in a life.

## What They Said About It

She asked about the marks, because it was the ordinary thing to ask, and the man with the broken thumbnail told her, and then the foreman heard the question from about twenty feet away and came over and answered it himself, which she understood at the time and appreciated much later.

“You are asking what the marks are,” he said.

“I am.”

He put his hand flat on the page.

“That is the line.”

Nobody in that camp had used the word to her before and she had walked nine days without having heard it.

“It is not stone,” he said. “I want that in first, because everybody who comes out here has it in their head wrong within a day and then they go home and tell people. It is not stone and it has never been in the same place on both sides of itself, and I have been doing this for twenty-six years and I have never once seen it move a foot in a night.”

“Move it where.”

“Nowhere,” he said. “It does not move. I am saying it is not a thing in the ground that a man put there. It is a line of work.”

He tapped the painted marks with a knuckle, one of them, the bar.

“You keep it,” he said. “That is the whole of the job. The work is tending. A man goes out and he attends a stretch and he puts the marks fresh and he fills what has gone soft and he does it on the day the stretch is due and not on the day it is convenient.”

“And if he does not.”

“Then it leaks.” The foreman took his hand off the page. “And what comes out of a stretch that has not been kept is drift. And you will find the drift is thicker where the work is worst, and that is not a mystery, that is a schedule that was not met.”

There were three other men inside about twenty feet of that and they had all stopped what they were doing, and one of them said, “He has been saying that for nine years,” and the foreman did not look round, and did not stop.

“That is not a secret and it is not an opinion. It is what it is. Write it down if you have a hand that works.”

## The Other One Who Talks About It

The man who said that was about thirty and he had the pay-book's companion, which was a board with the week's work and the numbers on it, and he was the one who read the carriers' schedule, and he had nine years of it.

He waited until the foreman had gone back over the wire and then he came and leaned on the trestle beside her, and he did not say a greeting and neither did she.

“You have a licence,” he said, looking at her hands and not at her face. “I am not asking and I am not going to.”

He nodded out along the ground inside the wire.

“That is drift on the low side of that stretch. It is about a foot of it and it is wet under and it comes up over the top of a boot in about four places, and we cleared it on the ninth day of last month and it was back by the twelfth.”

“That is three days.”

“That is three days,” he said, “on that stretch, which is the worst stretch on the nine days, and it is because the work above it was not done in the season before, and the season before that, and the season before that, and no one of them is the man who is standing here now.”

He looked at the trestle.

“What nobody out here will tell you is where it comes out of. I will tell you, because you will hear it anyway and you are going to be out here all season and I would rather you heard it from a man holding a board than work it out for yourself and get it wrong.”

She did not ask. She stood exactly where she was.

“It is what gets put in the ground on the other side. Everything that is said inside goes in the ground on the other side, and then it comes along, and where the work is done it goes past, because there is nothing there for it to hold to.

Where the work is not done it has got soft ground to sit in and it sits in it and it thickens, and that is what a foot of it on the low side of the worst stretch on nine days is.”

He said it in the ordinary voice of a man who has said it before to somebody.

“Ninety parts of it is that,” he said. “I have been out here nine years and I have never seen the tenth part. It may be out there. I have got no way to find out and neither have you, and I am not going to stand on this trestle and tell you a thing about the ground I cannot show you on a piece of paper.”

Then he picked the board up and went to the trestle and wrote a man's name against a stretch, and that was the entire conversation they had, and he did not say another word to her for four days.

## What Nobody Explained

She stood there with a feeling she did not have a name for and worked out what it was before she left the trestle.

She was in a room with a man who was telling her the rules, and the man telling her the rules was also the man who had to live inside them, and this had never happened to her before.

In four years she had been in rooms where somebody explained the rules to her: a hall with no roof and a column of nine lines and three holes in it; a county office where the ceiling of her licence turned out to be a permission and not a wage; a tower where a keeper who wanted to be asked would not ask.

Every one of those people was inside the rule and looking out of it, and had a reason to have the rule the way it was.

This man had the rule the way it was because the rule was the work and the work was the pay and he had a daughter on it.

And he was right.

She could see the proof of it from where she was standing, and the proof was on about sixty yards of ground inside that wire, and it was the worst ground she had ever looked at and there were eleven men on it.

## What She Did Not Do

She worked a full day.

She did not ask the foreman a second question about the drift and she did not ask him where the drift comes from, and she did not ask him what makes it, and she did not ask him whether anybody had ever put it there.

It was not discipline. She wanted to. She had never in her life wanted a thing the way she wanted that, and she wanted it because it was six days of walking from the only answer that would have been worth anything, and because if he said something true about it she would have had to carry it for the rest of a season on ground where nobody would believe her.

What stopped her was simpler and much worse than discipline, and it was this: the man had told her the rules, and he was inside the rules, and if she went back at him about the rules in the tone she had used on every office in nine counties she would put him in the position of having to defend himself to a licensed one.

He had not needed defending. Nobody had accused him of anything.

So she worked the ground and said nothing, and it was the first time in four years she had met a man who was telling her the truth about her own situation and she let it stand.

## What She Spent

She spent nothing of the day's ration in that, and the reason was not a speech.

There was a question she had wanted to ask all morning and it was the same question in four different shapes, and it was: *who is behind the stretch you are not getting to?*

She wanted to spend one sentence on it. She wanted it the way a woman wants a thing she cannot have.

And the reason she did not spend it is that she had four days of ground in front of her at that point and she had no idea what was on them, and a voice that spends somebody else's hours out of a book does not get to pick which hours, and if she spent one that morning it would have come out of the column.

And that was before the fourth column.

She had not seen the fourth column's meaning yet either, standing there at the trestle in the morning. That came later, at a trestle, on a morning when the sheet came round, and it cost her an attendance, and she did not see it coming.

## The Coin

She was paid the same as everybody. Six coppers, counted onto the plank, the book open beside it, her line in a hand that was not hers with the date on it and the stretch marked with a painted bar.

The stretch was a cross, and she went and looked at the cross after the pay, on the way past, and it was about two hundred yards out along the ground, and there was a stake at the north end of it with the mark painted on the face of it.

She put the coin in the tin and the tin came out lighter by the end of the day, the same as the first day, and she did not think about that because there was something else in her.

The something else was this.

She had been under a ceiling of nine with nothing above it on the front of the document in any office in the world, for four months, because a registrar in a county nine days west had written in the ordinary hand that that county holds no schedule of work done past its boundary.

There were about forty entries in this book with dates on them and a column of hours out in a place that a county's paper has never crossed, and no office in nine counties has any hand on a book like it, and an office past this county could take it.

She had walked nine days to reach a counter that keeps her hours in order, and she had reached it, and the man who keeps it was right about everything she had asked him.

That was the shape of the morning, and she stood at a trestle with six coppers in a hand and a page open in front of her and did not know which of the two things about it was going to turn out to matter more.

## What She Felt About It, Precisely

It was respect, and the particular unhappiness of respecting something that is keeping her under.

She had spent four years being careful about the difference between a thing that was true because it was convenient and a thing that was true because somebody who had to live inside it had checked. That difference was the entire content of everything she had been for four years, and she had never once in those four years met a man on the other side of it, because the people who tell you the rules are always inside the rules and looking out of them.

These men were inside the rules and had nothing to look out at.

The foreman had not told her a single thing to get something. He had not told her that he was tired, or that the drift was somebody else's fault, or that he wished the county did more. He had told her the shape of the work and the shape of the leak and the shape of the consequence, in that order, and then he had gone back over the wire.

And he was right. About all of it. About the drift, about the schedule, about the fact that a line which is not kept is not kept, and about the fact that the whole of what keeps it is eleven men and a girl of nineteen and a tender with a board, paid by the day, on a stretch nine days long, with nobody upstream of them at all.

She could not use any of it.

That was what made it hard.

It was hard in a way she had not been hit by in four years, because it was the first time she had met a rule she could not argue with and could not even find the seam in — a rule that would still be the whole of the truth after she had spent the rest of her life on it, and that was keeping her at nine, under a ceiling of nine, on the far side of a county that does not certify hours.

She did not turn it into an advantage. She wanted to, at about the fourth hour, for about a minute, and what stopped her was that a man with a broken thumbnail had told her what the job was called, out of ordinaryness, without any idea what he had handed her.

She worked the rest of the day at the cross.
