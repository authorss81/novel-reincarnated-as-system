# What Furnace Materials Cost

On the sixteenth of the month she priced the furnace, and the far side told her what it thought of people with two whole hands.

She had carried the want since the crossing, under the work, the way she had carried every stage since the fourth of a month four months back. Foundation the ninth was the top of the second realm and there was nothing above nine anywhere in the world, and the next stage was not a thing any county paper would licence, because the paper she carried still said Foundation the first stage on the front and she had never opened it.

What she needed was not permission. It was material. A furnace is built out of things before it is built out of a body, and things on the far side have prices said out loud at counters by people who do not know her name.

She felt a hunger that was not hunger, because she had not been hungry since the first Foundation. It was the furnace want, specific and physical, and it sat under her ribs beside the plate and did not move when she breathed.

It had been under her ribs since the clearing, on bad flat with nobody watching, and it had never once been a wish for anything a county could hand her. It was a want for brick, in a shed, at a price, on a board, and every part of it she could get to with a bar over her shoulder.

## The Shed

The seller kept a shed a mile past the mill with a yard of sorted stock, crucibles and firebrick and iron wire and clay of three grades, and a board with prices on it in a hand nobody needed to read twice.

She walked there after the day’s work with the bar over her shoulder, because a woman who walks with a bar is a worker and a woman who walks without one is a question, and she had no question she could afford to ask.

The track to the mill ran two miles with water standing at the bottom where it always stood in the season, and the mill wheel stood off to the east not turning, and hedges ran two-thirds thorn along both sides.

She passed the cart turn where she had once been offered twenty coppers for a rate and a road and had not taken it up, and she did not stop, because the twenty-nine on the board ahead was the only number with nothing behind it but wheels and brick, and she trusted numbers with nobody behind them.

The seller was a woman of about fifty with burned hands and a short manner, and she looked at Ning Yan’s hands once, the way people on that reach looked at hands, and priced accordingly.

“Two hands work,” the seller said, pointing with a stick. “Brick there. Wire there. Clay at the back. Crucible small and large.”

She put her own hands on the bench under the board and turned them over, and Ning Yan watched her do it and did not take her own hands off her sides.

“That is a right,” the seller said, “with a heel gone out of it. And that is a left with two things missing at the front of it.”

“Yes,” Ning Yan said.

“Then you have heard the price,” the seller said, “and it does not change for the telling.”

Ning Yan added it the way she added everything, on her fingers and then again to check, and the number came out past anything five coppers a day could hold in one hand.

Brick and wire and a small crucible and a measure of the middle clay came to twenty-nine coppers at the board prices. The large crucible she did not need. The fine clay she could not afford to want.

Twenty-nine coppers. Six days of the only thing she could sell, with bread at five a day coming out of the same hand.

## The Arithmetic

She stood in the shed with stone dust still on her coat and worked it straight through.

Five in and five out left the number the same at the start and the end of every day. That had been true since the fourth and it was true now, and it meant there was nothing behind her but days.

Booked to the end of the season, pays on the seventh and the seventeenth and the twenty-seventh, a day not worked a day not paid, no day movable by anybody. The seventeenth was tomorrow. The twenty-seventh was eleven days off.

She had nothing in the oilcloth above the plate that was not bread money, because five in and five out left the number the same at the start and the end of every day, and bread took what the days gave.

Twenty-nine wanted. Nothing had. And the road west at ninety standing behind all of it like a wall, eighteen days of wages for a person and one bag, and the coming a different number nobody had quoted.

She wanted the furnace the way she had wanted the ninth Foundation, with her whole body and no ceremony. That was the emotion on the page of that shed, and it frightened her more than the price, because want on that scale had cost her a finger once and eleven holds were all she had left to spend.

## The Decision

“I will take the brick and the wire and the small crucible and the middle clay,” she said. “I have nothing now. I will bring twenty-three on the seventeenth and the rest after.”

The seller looked at her hands again, the left with the heel gone and the right with four fingers’ worth of grip, and said the thing sellers say to workers buying furnace stock with labourers’ hands.

“People with two whole hands break one crucible in three,” the seller said. “Price is for whole hands. Breakage is yours.”

“Mine,” Ning Yan said.

“Pay-day is the seventeenth,” the seller said. “If the day is not worked the pay is not paid and I do not carry the shortfall. Bring what the days give.”

“On the seventeenth,” Ning Yan said. “And the twenty-seventh.”

The seller marked the board with a stub of chalk, three strokes against a measure, and did not ask her name, and did not ask what the furnace was for, and did not ask where she worked.

That was the whole of the dealing on that reach. The price, and nothing else beside it.

She did not ask what happened to a person who took the stock and could not pay. She had a bar over her shoulder and a booking in a book four hundred yards behind her, and both of those were answers, and the seller had already given the only one she was going to get.

## The Hook Sets

She walked back to the hut in the dark with no coppers in it but bread money and a tally in her head instead, three strokes on a board a mile past the mill, and she sat against the boards.

## The Night Count

She counted the season on her knee with a stub of charcoal on the back of a dead weight ticket, clear of the ration marks, in the space above, with the flat of the second finger slow in the cold.

Nothing paid. Twenty-nine short. Seventeenth tomorrow with its pay, day money for days worked, unmovable. Twenty-seventh eleven days off. Road at ninety standing behind all of it, eighteen days of wages for a person and one bag.

The ticket took the figures the way tickets take everything, without judgment, and she folded it and put it with the rest, and did not do a sum of it, because there was nothing within nine days of road to compare a furnace to.

She sat with the strip of chalk strokes in her hand and felt the hook set deeper, the way hooks set when the fish stops pulling and starts carrying.

This was the irreversible decision of the season, hers, and it closed an option she could not reopen.

The ninety for the road west had been eighteen days away when the road opened. It was now the price of brick and wire and clay she had already taken the measure of, owed against pays on the seventeenth and the twenty-seventh that she had not worked yet.

She could not save the road and feed the furnace. She had put money she had not got behind material priced for people with two whole hands, and the chalk strokes would not wash off the board in the shed even if she stopped eating, which she could not do, because bread was five a day and the number in her hand was the same morning and night.

She had bought the first stage of the third realm out of far-side wages before it was bought out of anything else. That was what forty-seven days after the ninth looked like on paper, and the paper was a chalk mark in a shed a mile past a mill with a wheel that was not turning.

He said one thing that night and it was in the present tense and it bought nothing.

“You are building,” he said. “And it is costing you the road.”

She did not answer. She laid the bar by the door and held her right hand up in the dark, four fingers and a thumb, and worked out what a crucible felt like in it.

It felt like a thing that would break. That was the price of whole hands, and she had agreed to pay it with half of one.
