# The Coming Price

On the twenty-second she left the far reach on the road that had reopened, and came back inside the yard fence before dark, and it cost her the day.

She decided it at the fourth hour standing at the plank with the bar over her shoulder, the way she decided everything that could not be undone, quickly and with her hands still.

The carrier’s round had gone west the day before with the office’s draft on it, no date on the round, nine days hard going. The coming round, the one that brought papers and persons east, was due through the far end of the reach some time in the coming days, and nobody had quoted the coming and nobody was going to.

She wanted to see the road with her own eyes. That was the want, concrete and unadorned, and under it was the older one, to know whether nine days of road was still the road she had crossed in the dark on the second of a month with her hands cut.

## Leave-Taking

She told the man at the plank before the day was worked, standing at the trestle with the book open, because a booked woman does not take a day without saying it.

“I am off the bank today,” she said. “A day not worked.”

He wrote nothing, because there was nothing to write. A day not worked is a day not paid and no hours can be moved in either direction by anybody, and the pay-day on the twenty-seventh would arrive whether or not she worked the twenty-second.

He turned the page so the run below her line was clear before she had asked for it, which was the only kindness in the exchange and he did it without looking up and would not have called it anything.

“The road,” he said. It was not a question.

“The road,” she said. “Back before dark.”

He nodded once and went back down the column, and she set the bar against the leg of the trestle and walked up the track toward the post with nothing in her hands, and that was the first morning since the second of the month she had gone up the track without the bar.

The track was strange under her feet without the weight, the rise steeper for being unloaded, the wind with nothing to lean against. She walked it the way she had walked the bad flat in the dark, one stretch at a time, counting breath in four.

At the post she stopped the way she had decided two hundred yards back she would not, and looked at the board for about two seconds, at the strips going up every nine or ten days whether or not there was anything to put in them, and the strip that had gone up this month that she would never be able to read.

The last strip on it was four days old and damp at the lower corner, and the paste had not taken properly at that corner and the cloth had lifted about the width of a thumb, and she put her hand out and pressed it down.

It was not courage and it was not a decision. It was a thing a person does with a loose corner when her hands are empty and she has four hundred yards of track to walk.

Then she walked on, because she was not going to stand in front of it again, and the road west was past it and not in it.

## The Ride

The mill carrier took her for four coppers to the cart turn and back, two miles of track with water standing at the bottom, the mill wheel off to the east not turning, hedges two-thirds thorn along the track.

She rode on the back with her feet against a load of seconds, and the driver was a man of about thirty who talked about the track the way men talk about weather, ruts and water and where the wheels caught.

At the cart turn she got down and stood on the track and looked west, past the post four hundred yards back, past the working ground, toward nine days of road she had never walked in daylight.

The road went west through farmed country, hedged and lit, ordinary and settled, and somewhere nine days along it was a floor with nine men on it and a tower with a light in it and a counting room with a date in it, none of which she could see and all of which she carried.

## The Turn

The cart turn was a widening in the track with a stone marker and room for a team to come about, and she had stood at one like it two months back at the cart turn inside the line’s country, with shelves and a pot and a woman behind a counter who had told her to stop standing in the middle of the floor.

This one had no shelves and no pot and no woman. It had ruts and water and a marker, and a driver tightening a harness, and that was the whole of it, and the difference sat in her like a stone.

She walked to the marker and put her hand flat on it for about two seconds, the way she put her hand flat on books, to feel whether the road was still there.

It was. Ruts west as far as she could see, wet at the bottom, passable. Nine days hard going. Ninety for a person and one bag off the far end of the reach. The coming a different number nobody had quoted.

A cart came out of the west while she stood there, loaded and covered, going east at a walk, and the driver did not slow for her and did not look at her either, and she stood in the wet and let it go past the way a thing goes past that has no part in her.

Three days to the coming round, if it came when it came, which it would not. She had wanted a date since the fourth hour and the road had given her a loaded cart going the other way and nothing else.

She felt love go through her like a hand, for the nine men and the floor and the light, and under it grief, and under that the hook of the booking holding her where she stood, and she stood until the driver called time.

“What does the coming cost,” she asked on the ride back, because a rate asked on a cart is a rate in the register she trusted, plain and with nobody behind it.

“Different,” the driver said. “Depends on the load and the season and who is coming. Nobody quotes it standing here.”

A different number and nobody had quoted it. That held.

## The Cost

She was back inside the yard fence before dark with four coppers gone and the day’s five gone with it, nine coppers the day had cost against a furnace already paid and a debt already carrying.

A day not worked is a day not paid. The twenty-seventh would pay for the days in the column and not for the twenty-second, and nothing could move the hours across, and there was nothing to borrow against and nobody to borrow from.

She went to the plank in the dusk and stood at the book with empty hands, and the man at the plank did not look up, and the column stood with the twenty-second absent from it the way an unworked day stands, present as a gap and nothing else.

She put her finger on the day above the gap and the day below it and measured the space with her thumbnail, about the width of a nail, and there was nothing written in it and nothing to write in it, and she took her hand off the book and left it alone.

## The Morning After

She went out at the fourth hour with the bar over her shoulder and took the middle bank without being told, because the heavy end was earned back and not assumed, and Da watched three stones land and pointed at the ramp without a word.

She took the heavy end back the way yards take back labour that spent a day on a road and came back, without comment and without welcome, stone after stone, set early, move by hand.

A man at the easy end asked where the road went, the way men ask things they have no stake in, and she answered it the way workers answer, short and with stone in her hands.

“West. Hard. Wet at the bottom.”

“Coming back,” he said, and went back to his stone, and that was the whole of the yard’s accounting of her absence.

She worked the day with the gap of the twenty-second standing in the column behind her, unmovable, a day not paid, nine coppers gone with the looking, and felt the loss settle into the work the way losses settle, part of the load.

She felt the loss as lightness, which was strange, and then as weight, which was true. She had left the far reach on a road that reopened and come back inside the fence, deliberately, at a cost, and the booking held, and the road held, and neither had moved for her.

He said one thing that night and it was present tense, and it bought nothing, because she had walked nine coppers of road to know it herself.

“You are back,” he said. “And the road is behind you.”

She banked the furnace and sat against the boards, and the twenty-second closed on a woman who had spent nine coppers to look west down a road, and had seen nothing but hedges and water, and had come back.

The road was open. She was kept. Both things were true, and neither answered the other.
