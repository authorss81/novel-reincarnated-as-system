# The Hour It Is Written In

On the fourth of the month she wrote the date in at the plank and felt the new measure the way she felt everything the ground took, plainly and in the hands.

The flat of the second finger moved across the paper, strokes the same, time wider, and the man at the plank kept the book flat with his palm because the plank was not level, and he did it without being asked, and he did not look up. What had been eleven minutes was longer now, permanently, and every hour she wrote after the second stage cost her the hour it was written in.

She finished and stood with the pen down, breathing in four counts, and the hour was gone into the writing, staying there. That was the arithmetic and it was hers.

She put her hand flat on the book for a line’s length, feeling the paper under the four fingers, and the column on the near side was hers, dates in order, hours against them, gaps clean, season at each end. One line, in a morning that had a whole day in it, and the day had come out an hour shorter than the day before it, and it would come out an hour shorter than this one tomorrow, for as long as she wrote.

## The Shed

In the afternoon she walked to the shed a mile past the mill with two coppers in the cloth, because the furnace wire had worked loose at the binding and needed new twist, and the seller with the board cut the length without being asked and watched her bind it with the four fingers.

“Slower hands still bind,” the seller said.

“Slower hands still bind,” Ning Yan said.

The wire came true under the four fingers, twist over twist, seated firm. Whole hands would have done it faster and she had done it truer. That was the sentence the bank used for everything she did, and the shed used it now, and she took it without pride and without humility, the way she took true sentences.

She paid the two coppers out of the day’s bread money and went hungry-adjacent, which was nothing, because hunger had left her at the first Foundation and never came back. The wire rode back in her coat, and the furnace took it that evening without complaint, brick and wire and clay holding steady.

The shed was a lean-to of oiled sacking on poles with the goods on boards hung off the back wall, and the boards were the only part of it that was written on. Firebrick in ones and fours with the rates against them. Clay by the bucket. Wire by the length, cut to order, the price the same whatever length you asked for because cutting it was the whole of what it cost her.

She had bought at that board twice now, both times on a promise against booked pays, and both times she had come back with the money in her hand and put it on the board flat, and the seller had counted it twice.

“Whole hands get the same price,” the seller said. “Short hands get the same price. That is the whole of the arguing there is.”

“Then nobody argues,” Ning Yan said.

“Nobody argues,” the seller said, “except about the length.”

What she felt at the shed was grief, quiet and unadorned, for a speed she had owned since she was sixteen and would not get back. She wanted, concretely, to work the day without letting the grief into the bar. That was the want, and it held through the afternoon.

## The Bank

She worked the middle of the bank setting down early, moving stone by hand, the furnace steady through the middle of every stroke, heavier to carry and the ground not easier.

By midday Da moved her to the easy end without a word, the way foremen move labour that has found a new shape and needs a day to learn it, pointing at the bank and walking away. She took the easy end with nine holds and four fingers and no turn and a slowed finger, and turned stone the rest of the day the new way, lift with the furnace, set early, move by hand, and the easy end held.

A man at the ramp watched one stone land true and nodded once, the way men nod at work that needs no comment, and she nodded back, and that was the whole of what the bank said about the longer line.

She worked the easy end in fours. The easy end is not the light end, it is the end where a stone sits true when it lands instead of rolling, and the whole of the skill is knowing which stones those are before the bar goes under them, which is a thing you learn with your hands and not with your eyes.

She had it. She had had it since the fourth of a month four months back, and she had never needed it, because until the day before the turn had gone she could lift anything and set it where she liked.

So the easy end was not a kindness. It was a foreman moving a body that had lost a thing onto the work that did not need the thing, and it was the correct decision, and she took it the way she took everything that was correct and cost her something.

She ate bread outside with her back to the wind. Five in and five out. The number the same both times. The writing hour gone on top of it, unpriced, unasked, unmoved.

She counted the day before dark and found that it came out whole anyway. That was the thing she had wanted to find out and had not expected to find out so cheaply, and she stood at the end of the bank with the bar across her sledge and let it stand: a day with a stage in it and an hour gone out of it still ended at the same hour as a day with neither.

## The Evening

At the plank she wrote nothing more that day, because one line had cost its hour and the column needed no second one. She put her hand flat on the book for a line’s length, feeling the paper under the four fingers, and the column on the near side was hers, dates in order, hours against them, gaps clean, season at each end.

The woman with the scarred forearm was two places along the stones from her at the fire that evening, inside the warmth while eleven men held it, and tonight she moved one place along without asking and without comment, and put her bag down in the gap, nearer than before. The furnace ticked under her foundation the way it ticked at night under the boards, steady and banked, forty-five days and a slowed finger, paid and done and hers.

She sat with the pay-day hours behind her in the column and the heavy end lost for the day and regained in the knowledge that it would come back when the hands learned, and said nothing.

Da talked about the ramp boards with the man from the mill end and the talk went the way talk goes at fires, wedges and rails and where the water sat after the last rain, and the woman with the scarred forearm listened with her hands round her cup and did not say anything either, and the silence between those two women was not unfriendly and was not friendly, and it had a place in it where a word about a furnace might have gone and did not go.

What she felt at the fire was not despair. It was anger, low and steady, at a price that took speed the way prices take order, and under the anger a tenderness for the hands that paid it, four fingers and a slowed flat, doing more in a day than any man on that yard with two whole hands.

Under the noise of eleven men:

“You are slower,” he said. “And you are here.”

The fourth closed on a woman with a furnace of her own, a slowed finger, an hour gone into a line, twelve stubs, column clean, season holding, and a board on a post holding a line that was his.

She would write slower for the rest of her life. Every date would cost her the hour it took. She wrote anyway.
