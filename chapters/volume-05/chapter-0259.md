# What The Hand Cannot Do

On the twentieth she learned the furnace the way she had learned every stage since the thaw, by working under it.

She went out at the fourth hour with the bar over her shoulder and the furnace new under her foundation, and the bank looked the same as it had looked for six weeks, low soil over stone, sledge, ramp, carts, eleven men, Da at the top with one hand on the rail.

The first stone told her the shape of the change. The bar went under and came up with the furnace behind it, stronger through the middle of the stroke than she had ever been, stone rising the way it rose for men with two whole hands and whole shoulders.

Then the end of the stroke came, and the turn was not there, and the stone went flat off the bar onto the sledge a hand’s width wrong, and she had to move it with the heel-gone hand before it would ride.

## The Day

She turned it into method by the sixth hour, the way she turned everything into method. Lift with the furnace through the middle. Set down early. Move the stone by hand at the end instead of by turn.

It cost time on every stone. It cost the heel-gone palm a fresh opening by midday, bound with the spare strip. It cost her the standing she had held on the heavy end, because Da watched three stones land wrong and moved her to the middle of the bank without a word, the way foremen move labour that has changed shape.

The three stones were not bad stones. They were the same stones she had turned forty of the day before and the day before that. The bar simply no longer ended where she meant it to end, and no amount of arm made up the last hand’s width.

She felt shame go through her like weather, hot and complete, and under it anger, and under that a hard, small gladness that the furnace was hers and lit and paid for, and none of the three moved the stones any faster.

The middle of the bank was honest work and she did it honestly, stone after stone, setting down early, moving by hand, breathing in four counts, the furnace steady under her like a second spine.

## The Middle Bank

The middle bank was lighter stone and tighter footing, and the men there worked a rhythm she had to learn by watching, shorter lifts, quicker sets, no time between.

It ran two stones in five light and three in five heavy, and the light ones went fast and the heavy ones took the hand round, and by midday she had worked out that the place Da had put her was the place she would have chosen, which was the part she had not expected.

A woman of about thirty with a scarred forearm worked the next stretch, and she watched Ning Yan for half the morning the way workers watch changed labour, and at midday she said it plainly, sitting on the sledge with empty hands, without weight.

## The Count

The woman counted too, in her own way, and at about the third hour she said a number over the top of her own stone.

“Nineteen.”

“Nineteen what.”

“Ours,” the woman said. “You are one under me and I am one under the man at the far rail. Yesterday you were three over me.”

Ning Yan set her stone down early and looked at the two stretches either side without meaning to, and the woman went back to her own rhythm and did not say one word more about it that day, and that was how the middle bank told her where she stood.

“You lost the turn.”

“Yes.”

“Furnace work does that,” the woman said. “Takes the fine and leaves the strong. My brother’s the same since his second firing.”

It was the first time any person on the reach had named what the furnace took, and the naming steadied her more than a morning of stones, because a thing with a name could be worked round, and a thing without one could only be carried.

“How long,” Ning Yan said.

“To work round it,” the woman said. “A month. Maybe more with half hands.”

She ate the rest of the bread with her back to the wind and went back to the middle bank, and worked the afternoon the new way without testing the old one, lift, set early, move by hand, and the stones went where she put them.

By the end of the day she had turned nearly what she had turned on the heavy end, and it had cost her the most careful day she had worked since the crossing, and no man on the yard said one word about the move.

## The Fire

She sat at the eight feet that evening the way she sat every night, inside the warmth while eleven men held it, and felt the furnace answer the fire under the trestle.

The trestle fire was free and belonged to the eleven men, and it burned on their hours, and at about the eighth hour four of them let it down into the stones without asking anybody, the way they always did.

She watched it go down with the furnace steady under her own foundation, one fire banked into stones and one banked under boards four hundred yards east, and worked out the difference the way she worked out everything, plainly.

The trestle fire warmed whoever stood in it while it burned. The furnace warmed nobody but her and never went out. That was what twenty-nine coppers bought, and the price of whole hands, and breakage hers and done.

A man across the fire said something about a wheel to Da, and Da answered it, and the talk went on the way talk goes on at fires, and she sat inside the eight feet with stone dust on her coat and said nothing, because the arrangement stood and the word was available in no form.

A man from the easy end shifted over on the stones to make room for her the way he had made room a hundred times, and she sat where she had sat for six weeks at the edge of the eight feet, and he did not ask why she was not closer, and she did not say, and the not-asking was the whole of what the yard had given her since the crossing.

## The Test

At dusk she took the bar behind the hut and tried the turn forty times, alone, the way she had tried holds after the heel went.

Forty strokes. Forty flat endings. The turn that had made the end of every stroke since she was sixteen was gone into the furnace, and the furnace gave strength through the middle and nothing at the end, and that was the bargain and it was permanent.

## The Forty-First

She set the bar down and stood with her hands on her knees breathing in four counts, and then she picked it up and worked the forty-first stroke the new way, lift with the furnace through the middle, set down early, move the stone by hand at the end.

It landed true. Not the old true, the flick-true of the turn, but a new true, placed and solid, the stone where she meant it by hand and not by bar.

She stood in the dusk behind the hut with a furnace of her own under her and forty-one strokes behind her, and felt grief and gladness stand side by side the way they had stood since the crossing, neither moving the other.

She sat against the boards with the bar across her knees and held up her right hand in the last light, four fingers and a thumb, and worked out what it meant.

It meant one-handed bar work was done. It meant every stone from the twentieth on would be a two-handed stone, set early, moved by hand. It meant the strongest body within nine days of road had got stronger through the middle and lost the finest thing it could do, and nobody on the reach could measure either half of it.

She wanted to be back on the heavy end before the twenty-seventh. That was the concrete want, and it was small and it was hers, and she held it the way she held the bar, with both hands and no turn.

## The Column

At the plank she wrote the date in, eleven minutes, the page held flat, and the man at the plank went down the column in the ordinary way.

Golden Core the first stage stood in her body and nowhere else. The licence still said Foundation the first stage on the front and she had not opened it. The column held days and hours and no stage. The ground held what the column could not.

She put her hand flat on the book for a line’s length and felt the strangeness go through her, the strangeness of being a person with a furnace no county had licensed and no county could hear.

He said one thing that evening and it was present tense and it bought nothing.

“You are carrying it,” he said. “And it is carrying you.”

She went to the hut and banked the little furnace with ash the way the seller had told her, three handfuls over the mouth, and lay on the boards listening to it tick as it cooled.

The twentieth closed on a woman with a furnace of her own doing middle-bank work she had not done since the second week, paid the same five coppers for it, wanting the heavy end back the way she had wanted everything worth having, one day at a time.
