# What The Rule Is For

She worked the fifth and the sixth and she was at the plank at about the seventh hour on the seventh with the wind behind the rise and four men at the fire and the day written in.

He read the run on the near side of the plank from the far date down to today, the way a man reads a line he has written himself a hundred times, and he did not read it out loud because nobody at that trestle has ever asked him to read anything out loud.

Then he went along the column with the rule and tore the strip off at the rule and put it on the plank face down and went and got a pot.

## The Seventh

The seventh is a short one for a pay-day.

That is not a thing anybody would say out loud. The pays are the seventh, the seventeenth and the twenty-seventh, and the seventh is the first of the three and the one where the money runs out, and the trestle at about the seventh hour on the seventh is quieter than it is on the other two because about half the men on that yard had nothing standing in the column since the seventeenth and had spent the week on their own ground or on somebody else's.

He still did it at the seventh hour. He read the run, and he took the rule off the ledger and tore the strip, and he put it on the plank face down, and it took him about as long as the other two.

She has been on this side of the post for about four weeks and she has watched that man do that four times and there is not one thing in the way he does it that has ever changed, and she has started to look forward to the seventh about as much as she looks forward to anything.

That is the whole of what the far side has given her that could be called a good thing, and it is a man putting a strip on a plank face down at the same hour on the same three dates.

## The Rule

It is a strip of the same paper as the book, about nine inches by two, and it has the date on it and the stretch and the hours and the rate and his mark in the corner, and there is a painted mark beside the mark.

He sets the rule against the line and tears and puts it face down and the face down is the whole of the arrangement: nobody at that trestle has ever wanted to read one and nobody has ever been asked to.

The strip goes into a coat and the coat goes to a hut and the strip comes out again in ten days. That is the entire route a piece of paper takes on this reach.

On the other side of a line of work a wedge of these went up a stair once, out of nine days of a contractor's book, and a clerk read it in a room and a ceiling came down. Here there is no stair. Here it goes in a coat and stays there.

On the far side of that post there is one office within nine days of road that has ever been in a position to do anything with an hour of hers, and it is four hundred yards up the track, and the paper has been in a coat.

On this side of the post there is not one person within nine days of road who could take nine of those strips and say what any of them is worth.

The one person within nine days of road who could have told anybody what an hour of hers is worth has never heard of her, and he closed his ground against her lawfully and he was right to.

And the office that could is the one with a sheet on a spike in a low room four hundred yards up that track, and it does not know she exists, and there is a figure on that sheet that has been sitting there for a month waiting for somebody to act on it and it is hers and it is eight hundred jin.

## Nine

She took them out at about the eighth hour on the seventh, on the step of the store with the bar across her knees, and she put them out in the order they were torn off.

Nine of them, in four marks.

Three are the far side of the post and they are one man. One is the first and it is the first strip that has ever come out of a book on this side of a line of work, and it is the one she is holding against her ribs at this moment with everything else in it.

The rest came in a hand that is not hers over two months, and they are the fifth and the sixth of them and they are in two different hands and a different paint.

She laid them out against each other the way a clerk lays two columns out, and the dates came out clean, and the hours came out at eleven on every one of them where the stretch was the same, and not one of the nine refers to anything else that she has ever been in.

There are three dates in a sub-contractor's book nine days east that she worked and that are not in it, and they are true, and she did not fill them and she did not write them here either.

## The Ninth Of Nine

She read them against each other twice.

The first time the way she read five of them on a step two months ago, which is date against date and stretch against stretch and rate against rate, looking for the place where two pieces of paper say different things about the same day.

They do not. There are no two of the nine that overlap and there are no two that contradict and there is not one hour in nine of them that is claimed by anybody else.

The second time she read them the other way, which is the way a clerk reads a column and not two, top to bottom, checking for a gap.

There is no gap.

On the far side of a line of work, since the second of a month, a woman has been at a bank every day that has come, and there is a strip of paper off a rule for every one of those pays, and the run of them is continuous, and not one office within nine days of road has ever seen a single one of them.

Inside the bound it took her four years and two counties and a licence and a hearing and a name on a form and a man with a notebook to get a document that put her at Foundation the first stage, and that document does not have one hour of work in it and has never had.

## What The Back Half Of It Bought

Two books in two hands.

She took them apart herself on purpose in daylight at a trestle in the time it takes a man to turn a bar over, so that neither one carried a continuous run, because a continuous run was the whole of the instrument.

That was in the spring. She squared the two of them in the summer and then she let them go and she has not asked for either of them to be put back together and she is not going to.

And here, past the post, since the second of a month, there has been one hand, in order, with dates, and a run of it, and a name at the top of the column, and a second date at the far end of it, and she has not had to split a single thing and there has been no reason to.

Four years is a long time to want one instrument.

## What It Is Worth

She did not write down what a stub is worth.

There is nothing within nine days of road to compare one to. The course's stubs are in a county book nine days east and go up a stair in a wedge of paper read out loud by a clerk in a room. These are in an oilcloth against her ribs and they are in four marks, written by four different men in four different hands, and not one of the four knows what his mark is worth and not one of the four has ever been asked.

She put the ninth one in the oilcloth above the plate with the other eight, and the plate took the weight of it the way it has taken the weight of all of it for two months.

Then she folded the oilcloth and put it back and went out and got the bar.

## What It Is Worth In A Different Country

Inside the bound a wedge of paper had gone up a stair out of nine days of a contractor's book on the seventh of a month, and a clerk in a room she was nine seconds from had read it aloud to people who had never seen the man and never seen the ground, and the number that came back down it was the number that moved her ceiling.

That is what a stub is worth on that side of a line of work. It is worth exactly the height of the office that reads it.

This one goes up a stair on the seventh of a month into a low room with a counter and a stove and six men, and there is no office on the far side of that room that answers anything, and the thing the far side does with it is nothing at all.

So it is worth whatever the four men who wrote the marks are worth, and not one of the four has ever been asked.

That is the same answer she got on a stone two months ago and it is the same answer and it is worth exactly the same thing, and the entire difference between the two countries is that in one of them there is a stair.

## The Small Win

For four years the only instrument this world has given her is a continuous run of her own hours in one hand in order with dates.

She broke it on purpose with her own hands in daylight to stop a man in a plain coat from executing a piece of paper, and she has been carrying the pieces of it in an oilcloth ever since, and on the seventh of a month at about the eighth hour on a step on the far side of a post she laid out nine of them and they were in order.

For the first time in this book she can prove she was somewhere.

That is the whole of it and it took four marks and four men who do not know each other and have never met her and would not care.

She went out at the fourth hour the next morning with the bar in her left hand, and there is a fire under that trestle that she does not pay for and has never had to.
