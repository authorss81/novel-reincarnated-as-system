# The Country Asks Nothing

On the fourth evening she walked the reach east past the trestle and did not stop, and on the fifth she walked it again, and on the sixth she walked it until the light went off the low ground, and by the seventh it was ground she walked.

It is about two miles of it from the yard to the mill track and back, and it is hedged on both sides where the hedgerows have not gone over to thorn, and there is a field that has been worked this year and a field that has not, and the one that has not is not lying fallow, it is in grass that has been left two years, and somebody mows the top of it once a year and takes the hay off it and leaves the rest.

The cart track goes in past the mill and comes out on the far side of the mill and there is no other way off this ground that she has found.

There are windows lit at the mill because there is a man in the mill, and there are windows lit along the track because the people in those houses wanted them lit, and a woman on a reach past the last stake is a thing that is lit at by a woman who is going to bed.

## The Mill And The Field

The mill is a low building with a wheel on it that is not turning, because there is no water on it in the cold, and it is the only building within two miles of that yard that has more than one storey.

She went in on the fifth because the door was open and nobody came at her.

Inside there were two men and a bench and a stove and a smell of oil and cut stone, and one of them looked up and one of them did not, and neither of them asked her anything, and she stood in there for about ten minutes and looked at a wheel with iron on it.

One of them said, “It wants about four days of rain and then it runs and nobody has the wood for it.”

That was the whole of what was said in there by anybody, and she went out and shut the door behind her.

The field that is not worked is the one that matters out here. She worked that out on the fourth walk without asking anybody. A field in grass two years in is not a field that failed and it is not a field being rested, because a rested field has a different colour in it, and this one is cut at the top and left at the bottom and the bottom is a year older than the top.

Somebody is mowing a strip a year off a field for nine or ten years and leaving the rest to go, and at the far edge of it there is a gate that goes nowhere and has a hinge that has been oiled at some point this decade.

That is what ordinary country is. It is nine hundred decisions a year, taken by people who are not thinking about anything while they take them, and not one of the nine hundred is a fact about a person four hundred yards from a bank.

## What She Kept Waiting For

She kept looking for the thing that would go wrong.

That is the part of the far side that took four days and not one hour, because there is nothing on it. Nobody looked at her for two days running. Nobody knocked on anything. Nothing came up the cart track at a run with a paper in it, and nothing came down, and not one man at eleven bars a day said one word to her about who she was or where she had been or what was under her.

She worked out what she had been waiting for on about the second evening, standing on a rise with the bar across her knees.

The thing that would go wrong is a stage.

Everything she has ever done has made a noise that goes through the ground and out to nine counties and is written in four of them.

If the ninth one she took had been taken here instead of there, this is what would have happened. The sound would have gone along the drift under the rise about a quarter of a second after she let it go, and it would have arrived at that trestle and at that mill and in those lit windows.

And every one of the people standing at that fire on the second of a month would have heard a Foundation stage go up in a country where there is no ceiling over anything at all.

Nobody heard it.

She was the only person within nine days of road who would have noticed that it had not been heard, and there was not one other person on this reach within nine days of road who could have told the difference between a stage and a cart coming off a rut.

That is the whole of what the far side is, and it took four days to be sure of it.

## Who Owns The Bank

There is a fire under the trestle and it belongs to the eleven men, and she stands in it because she is employed there, and on the fourth evening at about the seventh hour there were four men at it and a gap at the side of the trestle about the size of a person.

She sat in the gap.

She had been on this ground four days and had not asked anybody anything, and the discipline of it had gotten into her the way a discipline gets into a person, and she asked one question, out loud, in the ordinary voice, to a man of about forty with his sleeves rolled who had a pot beside his elbow and no reason on earth to answer it.

“Who owns the bank.”

He looked at her for about as long as it takes to read a line.

“A man called Tao,” he said. “He is seventy-one and he is four miles east and he has not been on it since the spring. The yard is worked under him and Da takes a cut of it, and Da is the one that stands at the ramp, and the stone goes down the track to a yard at the mill that is not his and not ours either.”

“That is all of it.”

“That is all of it,” the man said, and put his hands back to the pot.

She did not ask him a second question to make it longer, and he did not add anything, and it took about as long as the answer itself.

## What It Told Her

It told her the shape of the whole thing in one breath and she stood there with it.

There is a man who owns it, and a man who stands at the ramp, and a yard at the mill that owns none of it, and the bank is worked because those three are arranged that way, and the man at the plank writes down the hours because a yard at the mill has to pay for the stone and the only way a yard at the mill knows what it has got is off a piece of paper.

She has been reading books like this since she was sixteen. She has never before been in one that was not also writing her down at the same time.

The reach is farmed and lit and hedged and ordinary, and eleven men at it have never heard a stage announced in their lives and would not know one if it came up out of the ground at their feet, and the strongest body within nine days of road is paid five coppers a day.

“You have been expecting something to go wrong for four days,” he said, “and nothing has gone wrong, and you have not noticed that you keep waiting.”

She did not answer him and she was not going to, and she sat in the gap at the trestle for about an hour after that with her back to the wind and the fire on one side of her.

## The Thing She Wanted

She wanted one of them to want something from her.

Not a document and not a signature and not the reading of a thing out loud in a room. A thing. Somebody on this reach who needed a thing done and had to ask her for it, so that she would have stood there and been asked, and would have said a name into a room that had not heard it before.

She wanted it for about an hour and a half on the seventh evening, sitting in the gap at a fire that cost her nothing, and it did not come, and it was not going to come, and there was no version of this reach on which it was going to come.

The crews tend the line to the west of the rise on their own time and for their own pay and no one at that trestle has ever asked her a thing about it and no one is going to.

## The Third Walk

On the sixth evening she walked it a third time and she went further, out past the mill, until the hedgerows gave out and it was low ploughed ground with nothing on it at all.

There is nothing past that. Not a field and not a hedge and not a fence, just ground that has been turned and left, and it goes out flat for about a mile and then it starts up very gently toward something that is not a hill, and she stood on it for about ten minutes with the bar across her knees and looked at it.

She has never seen ground with nothing on it before in her life.

Inside a bound, every square foot of it is somebody's. That is not a law, it is what a bound is, and she had gone four years without ever standing on ground that was not worked or hedged or built or fenced, and she had not known that was what a life looked like until about the fourth hour of the sixth evening on the far side of a line of work.

She turned round and went back past the mill with the last of the light behind her, and the mill was lit, and the track was empty, and she was the only person within nine days of road walking on it.

The seventeenth is nine days off and every one of them has to be worked, because the book does not wait for her.

She wanted, on that walk, for one of them to want something from her.
