# The Sledge And The Bar

On the eighteenth she went at the heavy end and she was at the face of the bank by about the fourth hour and the other end of the yard was still empty behind her.

Nobody gave it to her. She went to it.

The heavy end is where the slabs come out whole. The easy end is gravel and small stuff and a man with a bar can go at it for a day without thinking, and eleven men had been on the easy end since the fourth hour and had been on it for the four days before that, and none of them was going to walk across a yard of stone and say anything to a woman about it.

So she went at the heavy end. That is the whole of how that happened.

## What The Heavy End Is

A slab comes out of the face at about the thickness of two fingers and about the size of a door and it weighs more than a door, and it has to be got down onto the sledge and the sledge has to be walked up the ramp and the cart has to be high enough that the sledge will go over the side of it.

The ramp is plank on earth and it is used by everybody at that yard all day, and it is not level across, and the earth under it is stone dust and wet stone dust packs.

It was cold and there was a wind coming off the rise that went through a coat as if the coat were not there. The stone was wet from the night and a wet slab does not hold a boot the way a dry one does, and that cost her time and nothing else, and it was the same for eleven other people and it did not make her special and it did not make her slow.

Four fingers' worth of grip on the right hand. Eleven holds in the other one that hold to the end of a stroke out of about seventy that have been tried, and the order is gone out of that hand with the heel of it, so every grip is built from nothing, one at a time, in the second it takes to decide what to do with a bar.

Right arm open a third of the time, and about a third more of the time than that by the sixth hour.

Four counts of breath everywhere, including on a ramp, including going up it twice loaded.

She did more of that day than any man on that yard and it was visible from the top of the ramp and she did not once ask for the work and was not refused it and was not thanked for it.

The day also ran long. It ran long past the part of it she is at the plank for, and she was still on the ramp at the seventh hour on the second day and still on it at the seventh hour on the third, and the cart went up after her, and that is the ordinary shape of a stone yard and nobody at that trestle is paid for the hours after the cart goes.

## The Yard At The Ramp

The man at the ramp is called Da and everybody at that yard calls him Da and he has been doing it since before the man of fifty-five had the cart.

He stood at the top of the ramp all day with a hand on the side of a cart and he is the only man on that ground who has ever said one word to her about the work, and what he said was on the second day at about the fifth hour.

"You are putting them down flat," he said. "Put them down on the edge. They go round if they go round and a cart that will not shut is a cart you do not get paid for."

She put the next four down on the edge, and the cart shut on all four, and he did not say one word about the four, and neither of them said anything else that day.

That is the extent of what has been said to her by anybody at that yard since the second of a month, and it was correct, and she took it.

## The Third Day

It came down across the right hand while the bar was under the load.

The cart above the ramp moved. That is all. It was a wheel on wet ground and a man who did not see her under the bar because she was under the bar, and it moved about the width of a hand, and about nine hundred pounds of wet stone came the last of the way down onto a slab that was against the ramp with the bar under it and the bar was under it and her right hand was on the bar.

It took about a second and a half.

She got it clear.

That is the plain way of putting it and it is the true way. She was on the off side of the bar before the slab touched the ramp, and she took the weight on the left, and she walked it, and the slab went onto the sledge and she got the sledge down off the ramp and out onto flat ground and it stopped there.

Nothing broke in her that she has not had back once. The hand did not come apart. It went on doing what it does.

She stood on the flat at the bottom of the ramp for about as long as it takes to count eleven and then she picked the bar up again and went back up.

## What She Did Not Do

She did not say anything about it.

Not to the eleven men, who were four hundred feet away at the easy end and had not looked up and would not have known what they were looking at if they had. Not to the man at the ramp. Not to the man at the plank, who writes down hours and not injuries. Not to him, who cannot touch anything and cannot help with stone.

She did not tell anybody and she did not go and stand somewhere and put her hand where anybody could see it, and there was nobody inside nine days of road she could have told who was going to do anything with it.

That is not courage and it is not discipline. It is that there was nowhere to put it.

She has spent four years saying out loud what her hands do. At a trestle on a course with eleven men on it, on the second of a month, standing at the near end of a plank, in the ordinary voice, because a man with a boot once took the weight of a thing on a plank and she decided afterwards that the saying of it was what the four years of it had been worth.

There is not one person within nine days of road who would do anything with it now. So it happened to her, and she kept it, and she went back up the ramp.

## What It Cost

She lost the rest of that day's ramp work.

That is the whole of the price and it is a real one: the heavy end is where the day's worth is and she was not on it after the second and a half, and the rest of the day she did the easy end with the eleven men at the easy end, at five coppers, the same as everybody.

The day is paid at five coppers like every other day.

That is what the book thinks about four fingers. It has a column and the column takes the rate and the rate does not have a second column, and a day with a hand in it and a day without one come out at the same figure at the rule, in the same hand, and go into the same oilcloth.

She stood at the plank at about the seventh hour on the third day and the man wrote the date and the hours and the stretch and did not look up, and did not ask her why the hours were short, because they were not short, she worked the whole day.

He tore the stub off at the rule and put it face down on the plank.

## The Yard At The End Of It

She went at the easy end for the rest of that day and she did the same work as four men who have a bad shoulder between them, and at about the seventh hour Da came down the ramp and looked at the cart and looked at the bank and did not say anything, and went up again.

That is what happened to her day. It came down to a cart that was half shut.

She did not go and stand somewhere and look at her hand. She took it to the water at the end house where the men wash at the end of a day and she put it in and the water was cold and it is the same water for everybody and there is nobody standing there watching a woman wash her hand.

There are four men at that yard and none of them had come over at any point during it, and the man with the bad shoulder had not even looked up when the sound of the slab coming down went across the yard, and she has thought about that more than about the slab.

## What She Wants

She wanted, for about a day and a half afterwards, for it to have been one of the hundred things that happen at a yard.

A hand goes on a ramp. It happens, and a man puts a cloth on it, and a man at the pot says something about somebody else's hand, and on the seventh of the month it is a thing that happened to somebody else and she has heard about it. That is what a yard is for.

She got the other thing. She was the only person within nine days of road who would remember that it had happened on the ramp on the twentieth of a month, and there was not one other pair of eyes on this reach that had seen it happen.

That is not a bad thing. She has worse.

But it is the whole of what it is to be the strongest body on nine days of road in a country where the strongest thing anybody can measure about you is what you turn over with a bar.

“Nobody on that yard is going to put a cloth on it,” he said, “and you are not going to tell one of them, and so the only person who is going to remember it is you.”

She did not answer him and she did not spend a mark on it.

A team went past the yard on the twenty-second at about the second hour with a load on it, and the carter had been up and down this reach in four years.
