# Nine Days With A Bag

On the thirtieth she did not go to the bank at first light. She went to the plank and stood in the short queue the way workers stand when they have something to ask that is not work, hands empty, bar left at the hut.

The man at the plank kept the book flat with his palm because the plank was not level, and he did it without being asked, and he did not look up. She wrote nothing that morning. The flat of the second finger stayed at her side.

“I am going west,” Ning Yan said. “Nine days. I will miss days no book can move.”

He nodded once, the way men nod at facts that are not theirs to weigh. A day not worked was a day not paid and no day could be moved in either direction by anybody, and the pay-day arrived whether or not she worked it. The seventeenth would come on the thirtieth of the month’s count and the twenty-seventh after it, and the column would show the gaps the way unworked days stand, present as gaps and nothing else.

“What holds here holds,” he said. “Season at each end. Name at the top.”

She felt the sentence land the way true sentences land on that reach, without comfort and without threat. Booking the season to the end of it had closed the option of leaving on a morning’s notice, and yesterday’s yes had closed another one beside it, and now she was going to walk out through both of them with a bag and come back if the road let her.

What she felt was fear, plain and physical, the kind that sits low in the stomach before a road. She wanted, concretely, to stand one attendance she had already lost, in person, in her own hand, and put a paper where somebody had to look at it. That was the want, and it had a date and a floor and nine men in it.

## The Bag

She packed the way she packed everything on that reach, sparingly and by weight. Bread for three days at five coppers a day, bought at the store at the seventh hour and eaten outside with her back to the wind. A spare strip of cloth. The knife. The oilcloth with eleven stubs in four marks above the plate she never described, beside the dead weight tickets with the ration marks and the folded figures of rates, six a load and eight a day and ninety west for a person and one bag, written clear of every mark in the space above.

Ninety coppers for a person and one bag. Wei Ran had given the going once, on the road, as rate and road and not a favour, and the coming was a different number nobody had quoted. Ning Yan counted the coppers out of the cloth where she kept the pay-day strips, face down, contractor’s marks on the back, and put them in a separate fold. The furnace money was done and the bread money was separate and the road money was this.

The furnace she banked low before she left, the way she banked it at night, three handfuls of ash over steady heat, brick coursed and clayed, wire bound, the small crucible seated in the middle like a cupped hand. Eight days since the nineteenth and the earth behind the hut had not given any of it back. It would hold banked for days if the weather held. If it went out she would relight it. That was the arithmetic and it was hers.

The woman with the scarred forearm watched her tie the bag from two places along the stones and said nothing, and then moved one place nearer without asking and without comment, and put her own bag down in the gap.

“West,” Ning Yan said.

“West,” the woman said. “Nine days is nine days.”

That was the whole of the farewell the yard got. Nobody within nine days of road could be told what the paper was for, because the paper was for a name in a book, in ink, where somebody had to look at it, and that was family paper and not yard business.

He said one thing before she stepped off and it was present tense and it bought nothing.

“You are going,” he said. “And I am here.”

## The First Day

The road west began at the cart turn past the mill, where the settled country thinned into working ground and the working ground thinned into the bad flat. She had ridden it a day and come back inside the fence once, on the twenty-second, costing the day, and now she walked it the way carriers walk it, steady, breath in four counts, right arm open a third of the time.

The first day was dust and wheel ruts and hedges giving way to open bank. She walked with the bag over the good shoulder and the bar left behind, because a bar was yard business and the road was not. Four fingers’ worth of grip on one hand and nine holds in the other, and the holds were for the bag strap and the bread and the oilcloth, and that was enough.

At dusk she slept at a carrier’s shelter with three other walkers, none of them talking, bread eaten with her back to the wind, oilcloth against her ribs. The ground was cold the way stone is cold. The furnace ticked under her foundation from nine days away, steady and banked, and she held it the way she held the bar, with both hands, until sleep.

What she felt at the shelter was not loneliness. It was the heaviness of a thing carried straight, a season with weight in it, a figure on a far-side book against hours she had not yet worked, and a road under her that charged ninety coppers and asked nothing else.

## The Second Day

On the second day the rain came and went the way rain comes and goes on working ground, without asking, and the ruts ran water and the hedges dripped. She walked through it with the oilcloth inside her coat, paper and stubs and marks kept dry against her ribs, and the bread got damp at the edges and she ate it anyway.

A carrier passed her at midday with an empty cart going east, and the driver nodded the way drivers nod at walkers, without slowing. The draft west had gone on a carrier’s round with no date on it, office to office, eleven days back, and this cart was not that one, and she did not ask. Asking was a kind of revealing and she had nothing to reveal that the road needed.

She counted days on her fingers at dusk the way she counted everything the rain could take. First of a month tomorrow. Then the seventeenth eleven days after that, a pay-day she would miss, standing as a gap. Then the twenty-seventh. The column would hold the gaps and the season at each end, and the name at the top in two hands, and that was the whole of what she knew.

She wanted, concretely, to keep the paper dry through the next seven days. That was the want, small and hers, and it sat beside the larger one the way stone sits beside stone, plain and unmovable.

The thirtieth closed on a woman two days west with ninety coppers spent, bread damp, oilcloth dry, furnace banked nine days east, column holding gaps she could not move, and a paper for her brother’s name folded clear of every mark in the space above the tickets.

Nine days of road each way was a deadline no office could move. She walked inside it.
