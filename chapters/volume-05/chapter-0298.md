# Mortal Ash

The seventh of the month came clear and hard and she went to the plank at the seventh hour out of a habit five months old, with nothing in her hands, because there was nothing else to take.

The man at the plank had the book open and he kept it flat with his palm because the plank was not level, and he did it without being asked, and he did not look up.

There was no queue. That was the first thing. Eleven men worked past the trestle every morning of a working season and the seventh was a pay-day and there was no queue, because there was no season and there was nobody on the reach who was owed anything.

She put her hand flat on the book.

The column was where she had left it, running down in order from the second of a month four months back to the thirtieth, with twelve gaps in it and every one of them entered by her, and the far date at the top of the last entry in the small hand of a clerk with a slate who had put his own mark beside it five months ago and had not asked her a single question about either end of it.

He turned the page back about four inches, the way he turned it back for the days, and there was the thirtieth, and that was all it said, and there was nothing after it.

“That is the season,” he said.

“That is the season,” Ning Yan said.

“Book shuts at the dark tonight and it goes in the box at the mill and the box goes out on the first cart.”

“All right.”

He did not say anything else, and he did not tear anything, and there was no strip, and there was nothing to put in her coat.

She stood at the plank for the length of about eleven counts after that, because there was nothing else to do with her hands, and then she went and worked, because there was a yard and she could set stone on it whatever else was true.

## The First Thing

The first thing she had to do in it was nothing, and she found that she did not know how to do nothing.

It is not rest. She had had four hours a day of nothing since the second of a month four months back, folded into the hours around the work, and that had been a shape a body could live in. What she had on the seventh of the month was a whole day of it, from the fourth hour to the dark, with a bar over her shoulder and nowhere to take it.

She walked out past the trestle to the end of the reach and stood at the place where the bank had been, which was a flat scar with a scrape through it and nine feet of lines gone to weather, and there was nothing in the world for her to do there.

That was the whole of it. Not hardship. Not despair. **The first thing she had to do in a season of her life with nothing in it was to stand at the end of a reach with her hands in her coat, because there was no work, because the work was finished, because the season had a date at each end and the second one had come.**

## The Things That Were True

She went through it at the store step at midday, standing in the sun, with the days off from the seventh.

Eighteen counterfoils in the oilcloth in four marks, torn off a plank by a man who never once asked her name, with dates that came out clean and hours against them and a contractor’s mark on the back of each one, and not one of them checkable by anybody within nine days of road.

An eight hundred jin figure, answered, discharged, and paid, in a room with a stove in it, and read out once in front of nine men, and drafted west to a counting room in Lowwater by name, with no date on it, on a cart that went on the twenty-second.

A date off a page in that room, taken off and not struck out, by a hand she had bought for eight days of a man’s life and had never once spoken to.

A furnace-man’s account, six years carried, discharged where it could be discharged, four hundred yards behind her, in a book with a stove in it, and nine days of road from the only place in the world it would have meant anything, which it does not.

A line with her brother’s name on it and ten years of his own against it, in the same book, put in on the fifth of the month without a mark on it.

Three Golden Core stages in about five months, taken at her own order, in daylight, where nothing is announced because there is nobody inside to be told.

The turn at the end of a bar stroke. An index finger at the far end of a bad flat, at about the sixth hour of a day she cannot number. The flat of a second finger longer than eleven minutes a line for the rest of her life. And a word in a book she cannot reach and cannot be taken out of.

Four fingers and a thumb on the right hand. Nine holds of eleven in the left. A right arm open half the time. A count of breath at four, and three gone, and not coming back. An aperture cracked through and permanent.

A book at a plank with a date at each end of her column, shutting at the dark.

And in the oilcloth against her ribs: a plate she had surrendered to nobody and described to nobody and would take off for nobody, above the tickets, with the stub of it gone the way a coin will go across a flat finger and not across a tip.

## The Register

She thought about the register once, standing in the sun at the store step, and let it go.

It read *reconciled*. Three registers in three counties read it and one page had her brother’s name behind it, and the word had been put there by three men who wanted her signature and had said they would have said whatever she wanted it to say, and it would go on reading exactly that for as long as the county that held it existed.

There was no instrument in nine days of road that would make it say anything else. There had not been one since she was seventeen. There was not going to be one now that she had no wages, no season, no county, no room, and a body that a far-side office had entered as good four hundred yards behind her in a country that could not be argued with.

She had kept her own column true. That was the thing she had, and it was days, and days were the only currency in the country she had reached, and it was in a box at a mill going out on the first cart, and it would stay true wherever the box went, and nothing in the world could put a word on it that she had not put there herself.

## Mortal Ash

She banked the ring that evening for the last time in that way.

Then she went and opened it, because she wanted to see what fifty days of a furnace leaves, and she was allowed to want that. It came out grey and light and dead in about the quantity she had dug out on the twenty-eighth of the month before, and she spread it on the flat stone by the door and looked at it.

That is what a fire leaves.

Every hour of it, every stage, every copper of brick and wire and clay, two days out of a column and twenty-nine coppers on a promise and two holds out of eleven and a turn at the end of a bar stroke and a plate surrendered in a hall with no roof and an arm gone at a shoulder in a winter and a finger gone at the end of a bad flat and an hour a line going forward for the rest of her life.

It all went into a hole in a field four hundred yards east of a post, and the field kept none of it, and there was no instrument that could tell her what a field does with what is put into it.

She had known that since the nineteenth of a month four months back. She had never once said it. She had gone on lighting it every morning anyway, every single morning, out of wages she earned a day at a time, and she was not going to be able to explain that to anybody and she was not going to try.

She shook the ash off her coat at the door.

It came down through the light in a flat grey fall and it landed on the stones and on the beaten earth and on the sledge runners and it did not settle. That is what ash does; it comes down and it does not stay where it lands, and a person standing in the middle of a reach in the dark with her arms out has to keep shaking or it will go back into the cloth.

She shook it out twice. Then she stopped and left it where it fell.

There was a bound nine days west that had burned its books to keep a ceiling at nine, and every one of its counties still read what it read, and no county in nine had ever heard a thing that had happened past its own boundary, and the price of the ceiling was paid in people and in years and in a word on a page with a signature under it.

That was a trade. It was the same trade as a ring of brick and clay and wire four hundred yards behind her, and it had been the same trade all her life, and she had been inside both of them, and the ash of both of them came down in the dark and did not settle.

## The Road Behind

Nine days of road behind her.

A floor and nine men. A wash house with a door standing open for anybody off the road. A tower with a light in it that burned at the fourth hour and did not stop.

A counting room in Lowwater with a desk a man owned and a step worn pale in a narrow band. And a paper she had put under a date on a board four months ago, which two of the nine had read upside down while they worked near it, and neither of them had said a word about it.

Nine days of road behind her, and she had walked it twice and would not walk it again in her life, and nothing on that reach — the yard, the bank, the plank, the post, the low building, the store, the mill, the eleven men and the woman with the scarred forearm and the man with the notebook — had ever been going to take her west again.

She was eighteen years old. She was past the last working point of a line of work, in somebody’s ordinary country, at five coppers a day a season ago and nothing now, and the only thing in the world she had that anybody could read was a column in a box and a line about her brother in a book with a stove in it.

And there was not one person within nine days of road who could ask her a question, and there was not one person inside nine days of road who had ever known she existed, and both of those things had been worth a great deal to her and she was not going to be able to say what they had cost, and she had stopped keeping a figure on that.

## The Silence

Everything that had ever happened to her inside that boundary had been announced.

Every stage, in order, in a flat sentence, in a voice that was not a voice, to every living person inside the line at once, with a price on it by morning. The bound had treated it as a gift. It was a receipt.

Being told was never the same as being charged, and she had worked that out on a reach in the last week of a season from a worker’s mouth in short sentences, and nobody inside the boundary had heard a word of it and nobody ever would.

She was standing past the last working point with a furnace of her own under her that nobody had given her, that no county had licensed and no county could hear and that she had paid for out of her own wages a day at a time, and she had lit it three times and banked it every night and had dug out its dead ash twice.

She was standing in a place where nothing was said when it happened.

It was the first silence she had ever stood in that was hers.

## The Way On

She walked out past the trestle in the dark with nothing in her hands, because that was all she had to walk out with.

She went along the track to the cart turn the way she had walked it twice going west and once coming back. She did not stop at the post and she did not go near it.

She had kept four hundred yards between herself and a board with a strip on it since the seventeenth of a month two months back, and she was going to go on doing that for as long as she was on that reach.

Past the cart turn the ground changed and the hedgerow thinned, and there was the mill two miles east with its wheel not turning, which it had not been turning when she first came past it at about the eighth hour of the second day four months ago, and which she had not looked at properly once in all that time because looking at it had never been a thing she could do about.

She stood on the track in the dark about half a mile short of it and looked at it.

Past the mill the ground went flat, and there was nothing on it and nothing on the far side of it, and nobody within nine days of road had any reason to go on.

She had four fingers and a thumb and an arm at half and nine holds. She had three Golden Core stages under a furnace no county has heard of. She had no wages and no season, a book in a box, and a line in a book four hundred yards back.

And nine days of road in the other direction that she was never going to walk.

She stood there for about as long as it takes to count eleven and found, to her own considerable surprise, that she was not afraid of it.

That was what frightened her.

She turned round and walked back up the track to the hut with her hands empty and the ash still on her coat, and she did not go past the mill, and the mill did not turn.
