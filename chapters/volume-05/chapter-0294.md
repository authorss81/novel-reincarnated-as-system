# The Answer In Ink

She went up the track at the second hour on the twenty-second because there were eight days left and an office with a stove in it had told her plainly what it needed, and she was a person who did what she had been told to do at the times she had been told.

The counter. The book open flat. Six men at their cups. The sheet on the spike, which had been there every day for five months and had a figure on it that had not changed since the twenty-ninth of a month five weeks back.

“I want it answered.”

He put his hand on the book and looked at her and did not ask what she meant, because in five months she had not said a single word in this room that could have been meant by anybody else.

“Sit down,” he said. “It is not a thing that takes a minute.”

## The Reading

There were nine men in the room. Six at the counter with their cups, two on the bench by the stove who had been there when she came in, and the man behind the counter, and her.

He read it out once, and he read it out the way an answer is read out in a room with a stove in it, which is to say slowly enough that a man at the back can hear it and not one inch slower than that.

“Eight hundred jin. Entered on a licence that travels. Answered on this office’s own book against hours worked past the last working point, the run being from the second of a month four months back to the thirtieth of this month.”

“Dates in order. Twelve gaps, entered by the wage-holder and true. The wage-holder entered at the seventeenth as good. The seventh condition is discharged. The figure is answered at eight hundred jin and is payable in Lowwater at the counting room of Hou Sanliang and nowhere else.”

He stopped, and picked the pen up, and wrote the whole of it into the book in about as long as it takes to count eleven, and he had not looked down once while he read it and he did not look up after.

Nine men in a room with a stove in it had just heard the figure of a bounty that had been standing in four counties for four years answered by an office four hundred yards past the last working point of a line of work, on the word of a woman standing at the counter who had four fingers on one hand.

Not one of the nine said anything. They went back to their cups, because an answer that has been read out in a room is finished, and a man who has heard the reading of an entry goes back to his tea, and that is the whole of what an office is for.

## What Was Not Said

He did not say the seven conditions. He did not say the first, the second, the third, the fourth, the fifth or the sixth. He said the seventh, and the seventh was discharged, and there was nothing else on the paper behind it, and the paper had been the reason she had carried four fingers and nine holds and an hour a line for four hundred days.

He did not say the Hunter’s name, because that was not in this room and had never been in this room and never would be. He did not say anything about the register nine days west. He did not say anything about a brother or a page reading *reconciled* or a plate against her ribs, and he did not know about any of it, and none of it was a figure an office could pay.

He said the seventh was discharged, and he wrote it down, and it was done.

## The Nothing

That was the part she had not been ready for and it arrived about a minute later, standing at the counter, and it was not grief and it was not anger.

It was nothing.

Nine counties heard nine stages in order over four years, and four counties had written them down in four books, and every one of those stages had been announced, and being announced was the only thing that had ever made any of it real to anybody who was not her.

The bound had a way of taking a thing that happened to a person and putting it in a sentence that every person inside the boundary could hear at once, and it was the cruellest and the most generous thing the machinery ever did, and she had had four years of it.

She had also been announced, once, and there had been a price on her by morning.

And now eight hundred jin had been answered, and the date in Hou Sanliang’s counting room was going to come off the page, and a furnace-man’s account that had stood open since before she was born was going to be written off in a county nine days west — and not one county in nine had any way of knowing it.

The bound would do nothing about it. The bound would go on reading *reconciled*, with a signature under the word, for as long as the county that held it existed. Nine counties would go on hearing nothing and four would go on writing nothing, and there would be no column anywhere in any of them for a bill closed four hundred yards past the last working point of a line of work.

She had known that. She had known it since the fourth of a month four months back, when a sound had gone about a quarter of a mile into a field and a keeper of about fifty-five had written it on a board without asking her what it was.

Knowing it had never once felt like this.

## What It Cost Her

He drew the draft out of the tray under the counter and wrote on it for about the length of time she had been waiting five months to spend, and the pen went fast, because an office writes a draft the way it writes a draft, which is as a thing it has done a hundred times and does not put itself into.

She watched him write it and understood every single part of what was happening.

It was irreversible. She had not been able to un-enter a column and she had not been able to un-book a season and she was not going to be able to un-answer this.

From the moment the draft was folded there would be an eight hundred jin figure answered in a room with a stove in it, in ink, in a book, with her name on it as good.

There would be a paper going west to pay a man she had never met in a room she had never entered. The one thing that would make it stop was a county doing something, and no county in nine days of road was ever going to do anything at all.

That was the price and she had known it was coming since the twenty-ninth of a month five weeks back, and she had said yes in that room with stone dust on her sleeves, and it had been the right answer, and knowing that did not make it cheaper.

She wanted, standing there at the second hour of the twenty-second with the stove ticking behind her, to be anywhere on earth else. She wanted the four hundred yards between this counter and the hut to be nine hundred and then nine days and then a season and then a lifetime.

She wanted to be a woman with a bar on a reach with nobody asking her anything about any of it, and there was no such woman, and there had not been since a boy of seventeen had said her name out loud in a hall with no roof.

“Where does it go.”

“Lowwater. Hou Sanliang’s counting room, by name, and to nobody else, because a draft that goes to a name goes to a room and a draft that goes to a room goes to a person.” He folded it and put his thumb on it. “The round goes east and west and takes nine days each way and there is no date on it.”

“No date on it.”

“There is never a date on it. A dated draft is held for a date. An undated one goes when the cart goes.”

## Out

He gave it to a boy who came in off the track at about that time with a cart that was going west whether it was full or not, and the boy put it inside his coat against his chest, and the boy was about fourteen, and the boy did not ask what it was and would have said so if he had.

She watched the cart go down the track past the post, and past the post that had a board on it with nine years of a column on it that she had never once been able to read, and out along the road past the mill, and out along nine days of hard going to a county that could not hear her.

She stood in the middle of the reach and watched it go and felt something she had not felt since she was a girl in a hall with no roof and four years of a life standing on her account, which was the feeling of having got somewhere by a road that had cost her the thing she had most wanted to keep.

The answer was in ink. The claim was answered. The date was still on the page nine days west and would be off it in nine days, by a hand she had bought for eight days of a man’s life and had never spoken to since, in a room she had stood in the street of and not knocked on.

The twenty-second closed on a woman with eight hundred jin discharged, eight days of a season left, seventeen stubs in four marks, a paper on a cart going west with no date on it, and a figure in four county books nine days west that would never learn what had happened to it while any of the nine counties still existed.

She went back to the bank and worked the rest of the day with the bar in her left hand and lifted four stones to the younger man’s four, and she did not lose once, and she said nothing at all at the fire, and the eleven men said nothing to her, and that was the whole of what the far side did with the largest thing that had ever happened on it.

Under her, at the boards, with the ring going:

“It is answered,” he said. “And I am still holding what you earned.”

Eight days to the thirtieth. Nine days to Lowwater. And the season’s last stone had been on a cart for six days and she was on the ordinary end of a bank for the rest of it, and she was not going to walk west again in her life, and the only thing crossing nine days of road on her account was a piece of paper with no date on it.
