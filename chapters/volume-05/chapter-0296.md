# The Furnace Column

He spent it on the second of the month, at about the fourth hour, while she was sitting against the boards with the oilcloth across her knees and the ring ticking three inches away through a course of brick she had laid herself.

She felt it go the way she had felt the other things go, which is to say she did not feel it go, and then she felt the place where it had been, which is a different thing and much worse.

It was not the breath. Four counts.

Not the arm. Half.

Not the finger. Four fingers and a thumb, and she turned the hand over and looked at it in the light off the doorway and it was exactly the hand it had been at the seventh hour the day before.

Not the holds, not the aperture, not the crack in it, not the writing hour, not the ramp, not the course of brick.

Nothing.

She sat on the boards with her hands open in her lap and went through the whole of it, item by item, the way she had gone through it at the second hour of the seventeenth of a month a week ago, and everything came back true, and that was the finding, and the finding was that there was nothing there.

“You have taken it,” she said.

“I have spent what you earned,” he said. “It went where it was going. I cannot show you where it went and neither can I tell you how much of it there was, and I have told you that before and it has not stopped being true.”

“Look at my hand.”

“I have looked at your hand.”

## Two Days

She looked for two days.

That is what a person does with a price she cannot find: she looks. She worked the ordinary end on the second and the third of the month and she went through the list at the store step both evenings in the sun, and she put the bar under a stone about four hundred times a day and lifted it with the left hand four fingers and a thumb and could not find one single thing that had gone.

Nine holds. Four counts. Half an arm. A flat of the second finger that took longer than eleven minutes and not one minute longer than it had taken the day before. A crack in the aperture exactly the width it had been. A body that was not hungry and was not warm and was not full, and would be none of those for ever, and had been none of them for five months.

Nothing was wrong with her. She was better than she had been the day before, in the only sense available to her, which was that a thing had been taken out of the ground she was standing on and had not been taken out of her.

That was the part she could not hold.

She had been given four costs in five months and every one of them had been on the page, in ink, in a body, named: a turn at the end of a stroke; an index finger; a count of breath; an hour a line for the rest of her life. She had pointed at them. She had said them out loud to men at a fire in the ordinary voice. She had been proud, at the bottom of it, of being able to say what it was.

And now there was nothing to point at, and the nothing was sitting exactly where the thing had been, and she was going to have it for the rest of her life and there was no instrument in the world that would ever be able to tell her whether it was large.

Under her, on the third evening, at the boards:

“It is not in you.”

“Then where is it.”

“In you,” he said. “It is in you and you cannot find it, and that is what it is, and I have watched you look for two days and I am not going to tell you to stop.”

## The Counter

On the fourth of the month she went up the track at the second hour, because she was not going to spend the rest of a season not knowing what had happened.

The counter. The book open flat. Six men at their cups and two on the bench. The sheet on the spike.

“There is a line in your book,” Ning Yan said, “that nobody in this room wrote.”

He did not ask how she knew. He turned the book and put his thumb on it, and she came round the side of the counter and stood beside him and looked at it, which she had not done in five months and which was permitted because a person who is entered in a book is a person who may be shown it, and that was a thing she had learned that week.

It was a line near the top, in the ordinary hand, with an ordinary date against it and no office name at the end of it, because it had not come from an office.

*Balance carried forward, Ning Yan, of Lowwater, against the furnace account of Ning Bo, discharged. Six years carried. No balance standing. Nothing carried against the name.*

“It came into the book on the second,” he said, “and I read it on the third, the way I read everything on this page, and I entered it in my own hand at the bottom, which is the line under it with the date.”

“Why did you enter it.”

“Because it answers something on my page.” He took his thumb off it. “I have eight hundred jin on this book answered and discharged and paid, and that line says the balance that carried them is gone. One of those two things makes the other true, and an office enters a thing that makes a thing on its own page true. That is not discretion. That is the whole of the job.”

## What It Does In Lowwater

“What does it do in the county.”

He looked at her for a moment before he answered, and she understood that he was going to give her the whole of it and that the whole of it was going to be the true thing.

“Nothing,” he said. “I cannot write in Lowwater. I have never held an instrument in Lowwater. I do not know that I hold one in this county either, and I would not put that in the book unless it was in the book.” He closed the book on his palm.

“What I have done is put it in a book that keeps it, four hundred yards from where you are standing. It is in there now and it will be in there for as long as this room is a room.”

“Nine days of road west.”

“Nine days of road west and one county, and in that county a register that reads *reconciled* with a signature under it, and three registers in three other counties that read the same, and a page your brother’s name is behind, and a furnace column in a book with six years of your father in it that says unsettled, balance carried forward, credit held.”

“And you have closed the account.”

“I have closed the account where it can be closed,” he said. “That is the whole of what there is. It is not in the county and it never will be and I have not pretended to you for one minute that it is.”

## The Cost She Could Not Name

She stood at the side of the counter with a book open under her eyes that had her father’s name in it and six years and the word discharged, in the ordinary hand, with an ordinary date against it.

And she understood at that moment — standing there, at the second hour of the fourth of a month, in a room with a stove in it — that she had lost something, and that it was not the price, and that the price had already been taken and would never be named, and that this was the other thing.

The account had been there since she was eleven years old.

That was the whole of it and it had been underneath every single thing she had ever paid. Her plate, given up in a hall with no roof against a signature. Her right arm, gone at a shoulder in a winter. Nine coppers a day of bread out of a book that had been whole hands and two holds and a turn. Twenty-nine coppers of brick and wire and clay.

Ten days of a column gone to a road and back. A finger at the end of a bad flat. An hour a line, going forward, for the rest of her life.

All of it a payment. All of it on an account. And an account means that somebody is owed, and being owed means that what was taken had a shape and a place and a direction it had been taken *for*, and a girl of seventeen standing in a hall with no roof could put all of it on one line and say *I am paying this* and believe it, and be right.

There was no account any more.

That was what it cost. There was no longer a page anywhere in the world with her name on it saying what Ning Bo was owed and by whom and against what, and every single thing that had ever been taken off her had been taken against it, and the taking had been a payment and not a robbery, and now there was nothing left to pay it *on*.

She stood at the side of a counter in a country four hundred yards past the last working point and could not have named what she had lost. She had been eleven years old when it stopped being true that anybody was going to pay it. She had been carrying it since before she could read the column it was written in.

And underneath the not being able to name it, which was the real shape of the thing and not the reason for it, was this.

The last thing standing in her life that was about her father had been a debt. The debt had been hers since she was eleven. It had made him a person with a page, and there was no page now, and the man she had spent nine days of road twice reaching for was gone off the page she had reached him on.

She put her hand flat on the book for a line’s length, feeling the paper under four fingers.

“There,” she said. “That is enough. That is the whole of what I came for.”

Going down the track with the stove behind her and nothing on that reach in front of her at all:

“It is done,” he said. “And I did it.”

She had known that when she said no out loud in a low room in front of twenty people, and had known it when she did not say no on the fourteenth of a month five weeks back, and had known it every hour since.

She had not said no out loud to a man at a counter and she had not said yes and she had not thanked him, and he had done it anyway, in a day where he had no hands and no road and no way of ever being anywhere she was not, and she was going to carry that for as long as she carried anything at all.

The fourth closed on a woman standing in the middle of a reach past the last working point with eighteen stubs in a fold of cloth, three stages under a furnace no county had heard of, and an arm at half with four fingers on one hand.

Four hundred yards behind her, in a room with a stove in it, was the last page in the world with her father’s name on it, holding six years and the word discharged. And there was no way on earth of making it travel nine days of road to a county that will go on reading what it reads for ever.

She had gone there as a girl to find a name in a book.

She had found the book.
