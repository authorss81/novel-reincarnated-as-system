# To The End Of The Season

She worked the twenty-fifth and the twenty-sixth and got to the trestle at about the sixth hour on the twenty-seventh with the bar over her shoulder and the wind off the rise.

The book was open and the man at the plank was on the pay, which he does on the seventh and the seventeenth and the twenty-seventh, and it is a longer thing on the twenty-seventh because it is the last one in the month.

She put five coppers of bread on the counter at the seventh hour and the counter took it and gave her a loaf and there was not one word passed in it, and she came back down the track and stood at the plank and waited for him to be off the pay.

Then she asked him the question she had walked four hundred yards down the track to ask, and she asked it in the ordinary voice because that is the voice questions are asked in at a plank.

“What happens to the book when the carts stop.”

## The Twenty-Seventh

The pay on the twenty-seventh is a longer thing than the pay on the seventeenth.

That is not sentiment, it is the shape of a month. Three runs of ten days go in, and the third one has nine months of the same column behind it and a hundred men who have been paid on the same three dates since before any of them could lift a sledge, and the man at the plank does not rush it and does not talk through it.

She stood at the near side of the plank and waited the whole of it and did not go and do something else, because the pay is the only time in ten days that the book stops being a thing people pass on the way past and becomes a thing somebody reads out loud.

At about the seventh hour he came down off the last page and squared it against the plank and put the pen down, and there were five men standing about the trestle who were owed money and they came at it one at a time, and she was last because she was hired last, and that was correct.

She put five coppers in his hand out of about thirty he was holding and he put it in a fold and wrote nothing about it, and the fold went away into a coat.

## What He Told Her

He finished the line he was on and squared the page against the plank with the heel of his hand.

“The book closes for a stretch,” he said. “When the carts stop there is no work and nobody’s hours, and the men go home to their own ground and the book sits, and when they come back it opens again on the same page and I go on down the column.”

“And after that.”

“After that it is a thing you can ask for,” he said. “It goes in at the rule, the same as a day, with a date at each end and one name on the line, in my hand and not in yours. It runs from the day you take it to the end of the season and the days inside it come in the ordinary way and get their own stubs.”

He put the pen down.

“It is not usual here,” he said, “and I am not going to tell you it is usual and I am not going to tell you it is not. A man can ask for it and a man can not ask for it and the book does either one the same.”

Then he stopped, and he looked at the column and he went back to the pay, and he did not say one word more about it for about half a quarter of an hour.

That is the whole of what he told her, and she had asked one question and got one true thing, and she did not ask a second one to make it longer.

## Why He Stopped

He does not volunteer facts about his own book. She has worked twenty-three days under that plank and he has told her the rate because the rate is a thing that is said out loud, and she has watched him write a date and hold a page and tear a stub and not one of those is anything that is said out loud.

He has told her what a booking is because she asked.

He is not withholding a thing and he is not telling her about a thing to do with her. He has a book and there is a way to put a line in it and she asked.

There is also the other half of it, which is that the eleven men at that yard have been paid by the day since before he came and not one of them has ever asked him for anything else, and he is about fifty and he has a rule and a board and a fire he is allowed to be under, and a man in that position who starts offering arrangements is a man who is offering something he wants.

He said the whole of it in about nine sentences and then he went back to the pay, and she has thought about that more than about the arrangement.

## The Arithmetic

She stood at the side of the plank at about the sixth hour and did it, and she did it the way she does everything, which is in her own arithmetic and not in her feelings.

A booking is a date at one end of a line and a date at the other end, and everything between them is hers to fill, and the wage does not change by a single copper and the stubs do not change by a single mark.

What changes is what a day is.

A day is a thing she can miss. A day she misses is five coppers she does not get and bread she does not buy and a line in a book that stays empty, and she has wanted a day in hand since the sixteenth and has not been able to have one and is not going to be able to have one.

A booking is the opposite of that. A booking is a line that has her name on it and a far end, and every day inside it is a day that is already owed to somebody before she gets up.

She worked that out standing up, at a plank, in about as long as it takes to count out a day’s coppers, and it came out that a season is more days than she has worked on this reach.

And she knew, standing there, exactly what that number was for, and what it was for was that on the twenty-seventh of every month for the rest of the run there would be a line in a book four hundred yards from a room with a stove in it that had a woman at the top of it and a woman’s days in it and a date at the far end.

That is what a booking buys. It is not wages. It is a thing that can be set against a figure.

## What She Asked

She worked the rest of the day on the heavy end and she went up the ramp three times loaded and she came back down at the seventh hour and the man at the plank was still on the pay and she stood at the near side of the plank and waited until he put the pen down.

“I want the season,” she said.

She said it in the ordinary voice. She said it in daylight with four men at the fire within nine feet of her and she said it in the voice of a woman asking for a number at a counter, and she did not explain it and she did not give a reason and nobody persuaded her and nobody talked her into it.

The man at the plank looked at the column on the near side of the plank.

He did not answer her.

He picked the pen up and went back down the column from the fourth of the month, and she stood there for about as long as a man reads a line, and then he tore a stub off at the rule and put it face down on the plank and went and got a pot.

Eight of those in the oilcloth now, in four marks.

## The Walk Back

She walked the four hundred yards down the track at about the eighth hour with the bar over her shoulder and the fire under the trestle behind her.

There were eleven men and every one of them was owed a wage on that day and every one of them had got it, and the fold had gone into a coat and the book had been squared against the plank, and that is the whole of a pay on this reach.

Somewhere in the two months she had been on the far side of a line of work she had stopped being surprised by getting paid, and had started being surprised by the reverse, which is that she was owed nothing at the end of a day and did not have to want it to be otherwise, and that had happened about six days after she crossed.

She wanted it back for about eleven minutes in a hut with three boards in it on the twenty-seventh of a month, and then she stopped wanting it.

## What It Is

She wanted it, and wanting it is the part that should have stopped her.

Four years of making sure that nothing on any paper anywhere can hold her, and this morning at about the sixth hour she asked a man with a book to hold her until the carts stop.

She did not say to herself why.

There is a reason and it is arithmetic and it is that a woman who is owed nothing at the end of a day has to turn up at the fourth hour every day of a cold season to be worth something, and she is eighteen years old and she has been the strongest body on nine days of road since she was fourteen and neither of those things is going to happen again by itself.

And the other reason is the old one and it is not arithmetic at all and she did not look at it, and it is that she has been on her own in a country where not one thing she does is written down anywhere except a plank, for twenty-five days, and a book that says she was here is worth more than a day of not having to ask.

She did not say either of those out loud and neither of them goes on the page of anybody’s book.

The man at the plank had a rule and a book and a second date to write at the far end of a column, and he had not written it yet, and he had not said no.
