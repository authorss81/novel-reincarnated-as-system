# Cold Ash

On the twenty-eighth she woke at the second hour because the ground under the boards had stopped ticking.

She lay a while with her hands clear of the oilcloth and listened for a thing that was not there, and then she went out behind the hut in the dark and put the back of her wrist against the outside of the ring the way a person tests something that will not say whether it is hot.

Stone cold. The cold of a thing that was not a thing until somebody made it one again.

She had banked it every night since the third of a month and it had been hot every morning since, twenty-five days without a single morning cold, and that had become the kind of fact a body stops checking. The wire was slack where she had twisted it on the seventeenth.

The seam she had clayed at the twentieth had gone dark and opened a finger’s width along the join, and clay does that when the heat goes out of what is holding it, because clay holds its shape with heat and not with itself.

What she felt first was not fear. It was the particular grief of a person who has been relying on a thing for twenty-five days and finds out that it was never theirs to rely on, because it had been a fire and fires go out and this one had gone out in her sleep.

She wanted, concretely, to have heat under her again before the third hour. That was the want, and it had a wage in it, because the bank was waiting and a bank waited for nobody.

## The Old Ash

She took the bar to the ring and opened it, and what came out was grey and dead and light, and it came out in a quantity she had not expected, because a thing that has been burning for nine weeks has made more ash than a thing that has been burning for one, and the two stages and the heat and the weather had all gone into that ground behind the hut and had not given one grain of it back.

She dug it out to the beaten earth and felt the ground under it, and the ground was cold the whole three feet across, and under the cold earth was ordinary earth, and the ordinary earth was the same ordinary earth that had been there before she ever made a ring in it.

Sixty-nine days and sixty-nine nights of it had gone into a hole in a field four hundred yards east of a post with a board on it, and the field had kept none of it, and there was no instrument that could tell her what a field does with what is put into it.

She had known that since the nineteenth of a month four months back. She had never once said it.

The crucible came up out of the seat with two hands, four fingers on one of them, and she set it on the flat stone and looked at the inside of it, and the inside was scorched the way it had been scorched at the start and there was nothing in it but cold.

She put the wire back on the ring and twisted it tight with the flat of her thumb and the second finger together, and the wire bit, and the ring came true as a ring and not as a furnace, and the difference between those two things is heat and nothing else.

## The Relight

She banked a fire in the ring at about the third hour with four sticks and a handful of the dead ash to start it, the way she had started it on the nineteenth, and the heat took slowly, the way heat takes through three inches of clay that has been cold a night.

Then she went to the bank, at the fourth hour, with the bar over her shoulder, and the yard had been on the low bank for an hour already and the man at the ramp had counted her absence the way a foreman counts an absence, by not counting her.

Da walked the sledge line at the fifth hour, stopped behind her, looked at the four stones on it, and said one thing.

“Ring out?”

“Ring out in the night.”

He nodded once and went and pointed at the ramp, and the ramp had three wedges in it that had to be driven before the wet came up, and it was a half-day’s work done by two men, and he gave two men to it, and did not say why, and she did not ask.

That was the whole of what the yard said about a furnace going out in the night. A man whose fire goes out is a man who will be a half day short on the morrow, and a foreman does not lose half a day over a fire, and that is all the interest anybody has in it.

At midday she went back and put her hand into the ring, and it was warm the way banked ash is warm, which is not the same as hot, and would do for a body that is cold the way a stone is cold and has been since a stage she could still count.

She sat down on the flat stone with her back against the hut and let the warmth come up through the base and sat inside it for the length of eleven counts, which was the measure she had for everything now, and did not want to move for the rest of the afternoon.

## What The Heat Takes

What she felt through the rest of the day was a thing she could not put anywhere, and she turned it over twice looking for the shape of it before she gave up and went back to the bank with the bar.

The ring had been hot for fifty days. It was hot again now. And the ground underneath it was exactly as it had been before, which meant that the fifty days had gone into the ring and not into the ground, and the ring was brick and wire and clay that she had paid for in days and could have made again this morning with a bar and a morning.

There was no way to ask the ground what it had done with it. She had been doing this for five months and had asked it nothing, and the reason she had asked it nothing was that she already knew, and had known since the first week, and had gone on lighting it every morning anyway.

It was the only thing in her life she had never once been told was a waste.

That was not new. She had worked that out in the second week. What was new, that afternoon, was that she had begun to be able to carry it, and that carrying it had got easier, and that the ease of it was the part that should have frightened her.

She wanted, at dusk, to have the ring hot enough to bank and to have one whole day of work behind her and one whole day in front of her, and she had the second of those and not the first, and she knew before she reached the hut that she would not get the first.

## The Seam

The seam wanted a course. That was the fact she had been walking toward all afternoon without knowing it.

She had a length of wire and she had clay, and she had a ring, and what she did not have was the thing that holds a seam together when the thing holding it goes cold and comes hot again forty times. The clay would carry once. It had carried eight days and on the last of them it had not, and it would carry perhaps another twenty.

After that the seam would open in a night and put the cold through the base the way it had that morning.

The shed a mile past the mill sold firebrick in ones and fours and clay by the bucket and wire by the length, and the seller with the board took the same price from a person with two whole hands and a person with four fingers on one, and she had bought there twice on a promise and paid both times in full, and the board stood clear and the man was owed nothing and was owed nothing further.

She stood at the hut in the dark with the ring cold in front of her and did the arithmetic, which was the arithmetic she had been doing since the second of a month and which had got simpler and worse.

Five coppers a day in. Five coppers a day out at the store. The number the same at the start and the end of every day, and it had been the same for a hundred and sixteen days, and it was going to be the same until the season ended whatever she did with it.

She was not hungry. She had not been hungry since the first Foundation, four months back, and hunger would have made the arithmetic into food and food into a reason, and there was no hunger and there was no reason. Every copper she earned had exactly one use. There was nothing else on this reach it could buy.

The ring wanted a course of brick and clay and a morning to lay it, and she had no money to speak of and a morning she had already lost today, and the only currency left to her was days.

She banked what heat the ring had and sat against the boards with the oilcloth against her ribs — the stubs in four marks above the plate she never described, the dead weight tickets, the folded figures of rates — and felt the cold come up through the base where the heat had been all night and was not there any more.

Under her, with the boards against her back:

“It has gone out,” he said. “And you are still here.”

The twenty-eighth closed on a woman whose furnace had gone out in the night, whose morning had gone with it, and who had one thing left that she could pay with, and it was the only thing in the world she owned that the far side could check.
