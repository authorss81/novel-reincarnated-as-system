# Named At The Plank

The first of a month was a clear one with a wind behind the rise and the trestle had four men at it at about the sixth hour and the rest of the yard was dark behind them.

She went up to the plank with the bar over her shoulder and she put her hand flat on the board for about two seconds to feel whether the wind was behind it, and then she took the bar off and set it down against the leg of the trestle, because you do not hold a bar when you are putting a thing in a book.

“My name,” she said. “Ning Yan. The same one that is at the top of that column.”

That is the second time she has said her name on this ground. The first was at the sixth hour on the second of a month with her hands cut and the whole of the reason for it being that a man with a book had to have one.

## What He Did

He did not look up.

He did not say it was usual. He did not say it was not usual. He did not put the pen down and look at her the way a man does when he is going to tell somebody something about his own book.

He went along the line on the near side of the plank until he was at the bottom of the column, at the thirtieth of the month that ended the day before, and he turned up about a foot of a fresh page and he wrote the date at the left of it in his own hand.

Then he said, “There is a name at the top of it.”

“So there is.”

“Write it,” he said.

## Eleven Minutes

It took eleven minutes with the flat of the second finger on the left hand and the heel of that hand gone, and the plank was not level, and the man at the plank put his palm flat on the page above her hand and held it there for the whole of it without being asked and without saying anything.

She held the near corner down with four fingers and a thumb.

The wind came off the rise and went through her and she did not stop and the corner went twice and the second time it went it went about a finger’s width further than the first and she held it and finished the name.

She did not ask him to hold it the second time and he was already holding it, and neither of them said one word about either of those two things, and after about forty seconds he took his hand off the page and went back down the column and wrote the days in beside the dates in the ordinary way, from the first, and the second, and the third.

## The First Of A Month

There is more of a month about the first than about any other day, and it took her about an hour to work out why the far side of a line of work had taught it to her in three weeks.

On the first, a man writes a day’s line before the day is worked.

That is how the course’s book worked and that is how this one works, and it is the whole of what a first of a month is in a country that keeps hours on paper, and it meant that at about the seventh hour on the first there was a line in that column with a date at the left of it and no hours against it and no way at all for anybody to know whether it would get any.

She had watched a hundred people not care about that. She had watched four men in two counties care about it very much.

So she knew, standing at that plank with the wind behind her and the bar against the leg of the trestle, exactly what she was about to do with a page and a date at the left of it.

## The Second Date

He wrote the far date himself.

That was on the fourth and it was in his hand and not hers, and the two of them are on one line of one book about eleven inches apart and about forty minutes apart in the day, and she wrote one end of it and he wrote the other.

That is what a booking is on paper. It is not a promise and it is not a wage and it does not pay one copper more than the day did. It is a second date at the far end of a run, in a different hand, under a name that a woman wrote herself with the flat of a second finger.

And everything between the two dates is hers by name now, and not by days alone.

## What It Closed

There was nothing to close, because nothing had been open.

She had been working twenty-six days at five coppers and she could have walked off that yard at the fourth hour on any morning of those twenty-six days and gone east up the cart track and down the rise and along nine days of hard road, and nobody on this reach would have known until some time after she had stopped being paid, and there would have been nothing to stop her and nothing to chase her and no document on nine days of road with a term in it.

That is the option she had.

It was the only one she had that was worth nothing and the last one that was hers, and this morning at about the sixth hour with four men at a fire within nine feet of her she gave it away.

A woman who takes a term and walks off it is a thing that goes round this reach before it goes in a book.

There are eleven men at that yard and a foreman at the ramp and a man at a counter four hundred yards up the track who writes down what he is told, and a reach is small, and the fact would be true, and it would be true inside a month, and she would be the woman at that yard who took a season and left it standing.

She knew both halves of it when she wrote her name and she did not write it any slower and she did not write it any faster and she did not look up while she was doing it.

Nobody persuaded her. Nobody said one word to her at the plank at all except the four he had to say.

## The Days It Bought Her

She worked the first and the second and the third and the days after that, on the days it bought her, at five coppers, on the heavy end, because the booking has not changed what her hand does on a ramp and the wage has not changed and the stone is the same stone.

What changed on the fourth of the month, at about the seventh hour, is that from the fourth of the month to the end of the season there is a run in a book with her name on both ends of it.

And on the seventh of a month at about the seventh hour the man at the plank is going to read that run and tear one more strip off it at the rule, and it is the ninth one, and the four marks on it will not be the same four marks.

## The Two Days After

On the fifth of the month at about the sixth hour a man of about thirty at the easy end of the bank asked her whether she was going to be here at the fourth hour for the rest of it.

That was not a question about anything. It was a man on a job checking a thing about the labour on a job, the way a man checks the weather in the morning.

“Yes,” she said.

“All of it.”

He went back to the easy end and did not say one word else, and that was the first question anybody on that reach had put to her in about a month, and it was about the fourth hour, and the answer cost her nothing and told him everything he needed to know.

She has been unasked at for a month and it turns out that what she wants is not to be asked anything, except that.

## What A Reach Is

Nine days of road and she has never walked one of them. She came through it on the second of a month at the fifth hour in the dark with her hands cut and it took her four hours and she went down once, at about a third of the way along, and got up.

Since then she has been about six hundred yards from a working face in a country she had never heard of, and she has got to know it the way a person gets to know a room: by what is in it, by when the light comes off the wall, by which board complains.

There is a field at the bottom of the yard that belongs to somebody who is not here and comes in the spring and takes it out in the autumn, and there is a hedgerow along the track that is two-thirds thorn and one-third something else, and there is a track to the mill that floods at the bottom of it in the season, and a mill with a wheel on it that is not turning.

None of that is on a board she can read. All of it is on a board four hundred yards up that track, in a hand none of the people standing in this country can write.

## What Arrived Two Days Late

The relief came first. She had asked on the twenty-seventh and she had not been refused and she had not been answered, and the not-being-refused had sat on her for a week and a bit, and on the fourth of the month at about the seventh hour it came out of her the way water comes out of a bar that has been lifted.

And then about two days late, in the ordinary way, on the heavy end, with the wind off the rise and a slab under the bar, she worked out what she had actually got.

What she had got was not a hold.

A hold is a thing that keeps you where you are because it is holding you. A booking is a hook. It is set in the same place and it goes in the same way and the difference is that a hold you can take off and a hook you cannot, and she has been off hooks her whole life and she knew the difference before she asked and she did it anyway.

She wanted to be a woman who works to the end of a season somewhere. That is all she wanted at the seventh hour on the twenty-seventh and it is a small thing to want.

She has made it impossible to have for free.

And there is a man on this side of the line who has been standing nine feet off since the fourth of a month three months back and has not put a question to her.

She went up the ramp at about the sixth hour on the sixth of a month with a slab under the bar and eleven holds in her left hand and four fingers on the right and there was a man on this side of the line who had been standing nine feet off since the fourth of a month three months back and had not asked her a question.
