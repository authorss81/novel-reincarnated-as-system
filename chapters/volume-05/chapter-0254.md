# The Second Asking

On the fifteenth of the month the office came to the yard, and that had never happened before in the two months she had worked past the post.

It was not the man at the counter. It was a younger clerk with a satchel and a slate, about twenty-five, with ink on two fingers and a strap over his shoulder, and he stood at the trestle in daylight with the book open in front of him and six yards of working ground on every side.

She saw him from the bank at about the fourth hour, and she knew before she was off the slope what he was.

The yard saw him too. Stone kept coming up the sledge and going on carts, because pays fall on the seventh and the seventeenth and the twenty-seventh and no clerk stops them, but the rhythm changed the way rhythm changes when an office is standing at the book.

Da watched from the ramp with one hand on the rail and said nothing. The man at the plank stood with his palm on the page, waiting, the way he waited for everything, without looking up.

She came off the slope with stone dust on her coat and the bar over her shoulder, and counted her breath in four, and felt anger warming under the cold the way a banked fire warms, steady and without flame.

## The Trestle

She came up with the bar over her shoulder and set it against the leg of the trestle, because a bar in the hands is a refusal before anything is said and she was not going to refuse a thing she had not heard.

The clerk waited until she had her hands down. He had manners, or an office had trained manners into him, and the difference did not matter.

He set the slate on the trestle and turned it round so the marks faced her, and she could not read the marks, because they were in a shorthand nobody teaches in a stone yard and nobody at eighteen in one has ever had cause to learn. He turned it back without noticing that she had tried.

“The room sent me about the sheet,” he said. “The one on the spike with the figure on it.”

Behind him the yard worked on. Stone came up the sledge and went on carts. Nobody stopped to listen, because a clerk at a trestle is a thing that happens on a working reach and men who are paid by the day do not spend the day on it.

“What about it,” she said.

“It stands,” he said. “It is entered in good order and it travels and it stands. The room can act on it.”

He said it in the ordinary voice, at the speed a man reads, and he was not reading. He had it by heart, the way clerks have things by heart when a room has said them enough times.

She felt the anger come up in her the way it had come up at the counter on the tenth, plain and complete, and she put it where she had put everything for four years.

## The Price In Daylight

“What would it take,” she said, and it was not the question she had asked at the counter. At the counter she had asked what it would take of the world. Here she asked what it would take of her, standing at her own book with her name at the top of it.

He put his hand on the column. Not on a sheet four hundred yards away. On the plank, on the page, on the run of days with dates in it.

“Hours,” he said. “Worked past the last working point. With dates. There is one run on this reach and it is this one.”

Four sentences and no adjective, the same price said in a different room with a different voice in it, in daylight, with stone dust on the air and eleven men working within hearing and none of them hearing.

“And who gives them,” she said.

“She does,” he said. “The woman at the top of it. Nobody takes them off a paper somebody else read out. She says it and the room takes them. Until she says it the figure stands and it costs the room nothing to stand.”

“And if I said it,” she said. “Would the room read my hours onto that sheet.”

“Does not matter what I do with it,” the clerk said. “She says it and the room takes it. What the room does with it is the room’s business, and all of it is after she has said it.”

“Then it is all after,” she said, “and before is still mine.”

“Before is yours,” the clerk said.

He closed the satchel. He had said the whole of what he had come to say, and he had said it to the woman at the top of the column without knowing she was the woman, because he had never asked her name and the book had it at the top in a hand he could not read.

## The Refusal

She said no.

Out loud this time, at a trestle, in daylight, in two words, with her hands down and the bar against the leg and the book open between them.

“No,” she said. “Not my hours. Not on that spike.”

It took less than two seconds and there was nothing in it but the refusal, and the clerk wrote nothing down, because a refusal at a trestle is not a paper and an office does not write down what a woman says about her own column until she says the other thing.

He did not argue. He was doing the only thing he was built to do, and she had given him the only answer that left his room standing, and both of them knew it.

“The room will be there,” he said. “Four hundred yards up the track. When the answer is yes.”

Then he went up the track with the satchel over his shoulder, and the yard worked on, and the man at the plank went back down the column with his pen, and nobody within nine days of road had heard anything but a clerk talking to a woman about a book.

## The Afternoon

She went back to the bank and worked the afternoon with the refusal standing beside her like a second bar, present in every stone.

The clerk’s visit went round the yard the way things go round yards, without words. A man at the easy end gave her more room at the sledge. Another left a stone half-turned for her instead of turning it himself. Small things, the small change of men who had heard a clerk talk to a woman and did not know what it meant.

She wanted to tell them it meant nothing. That was the want that sat under the work all afternoon, to turn round at the sledge and say it out loud, that the office had asked and she had said no and the yard was the yard and nothing had changed.

She did not. A refusal at a trestle is not yard business until the woman makes it yard business, and she was not going to make eleven men carry a figure that had been hers since she was sixteen.

Da came by the bank near the fourth hour to move a stone off her line and did it in one lift without a word, and went back up the ramp. It was the only thing that had happened on the yard all day that she had not had to pay for.

So she worked, stone after stone, setting down early, moving by hand, nine holds and four fingers, the furnace unlit and the body what it was, breath in four counts, arm open a third of the time.

By the end of the day the refusal had settled into the work the way stone settles into a sledge, part of the load and not the whole of it.

## What It Cost

It cost her the morning’s ease, and that was all, and it was enough.

For the rest of the day the heavy end felt heavier, not in the bar but in the hands on it, eleven holds in the left and four fingers and a thumb on the right, and every stone she turned over went into a column somebody four hundred yards away had now priced twice.

She had said no once in her own head at a counter on the twelfth. She had said it again out loud at a trestle on the fifteenth, in a different room with a different voice in it.

A refusal given twice is a thing with a shape. It stands the way a figure stands, and it costs the person standing it nothing until somebody starts charging for the ground under it.

At the end of the day she wrote the date in, eleven minutes, the page held flat, and she did not look up the track once.

He said one thing that evening and it was in the ordinary voice and it bought nothing.

“You have said it in two rooms now,” he said, “and the second one heard it.”

She laid the bar by the door of the hut and sat with her back against the boards and worked out what she wanted.

She wanted the figure off the spike and the column left alone. She wanted what she had wanted since she was sixteen, and it had followed her nine days of road and three-quarters of a mile of bad flat to stand four hundred yards up a cart track.

Wanting it did not move it.
