# The Reconciled Page

On the twentieth she worked the bank through a wind that took the dust sideways, and the stone came true under the bar, lift with the furnace, set early, move by hand, and the day held.

At about the sixth hour a word came down the bank from the low building, said the way offices say things that are not summons, without weight. The register still read reconciled. That was all. No correction. No reading. No hand put to it.

It came from a clerk of about thirty who had come down the track with a slate and said the six words and was gone again before she had put the bar down, and it was not a question and it was not an order and it was not addressed to her in particular, and she stood on the bank with the bar across the sledge and understood every single part of what she had just been told.

The register was a page in a book in that county. Her brother’s name had been entered on it. At some point in the years before she was born the thing that should have been done to that name had been done, and what had been written in the doing was a word. *Reconciled* means closed. It means the account has been settled. It means there is nothing further to stand in and nobody has to stand in it.

Her brother was alive. Her brother was somewhere in a county that had put a word on a page in his place and closed the page, and the page had been closed by somebody who had put a signature under the word, and the signature was what made it hold, because a page closed with nothing under the closing can be opened by any clerk with a dry hand. This one could not be opened by anybody. It would stand the way a laid stone stands.

She heard it the way she heard everything about books she could not touch, plainly and without machinery, and worked on. The register still read reconciled and was not corrected. She had asked for one hour and one paper and had got both, and the paper had been for the furnace-man’s account, which was a different book in a different county and had nothing to do with this page, and the page had gone on reading exactly what it read before she set foot in Lowwater with a sheet of paper under her arm.

What it was, was a lie with a signature under it. She knew it the way she knew her own breath. Her brother’s name stood reconciled in a book that had never held him, and the truth was unchanged, and the reconciling stood because a signature stood under it.

She could not touch it. She had no instrument, no office, no standing in that county, no second sheet of paper, and nine days of road between here and a counting room she did not knock on. And she had one thing, which was a column of hours with her name at the top of it, kept by people who were not a county and were not obliged to keep it, and it could not carry a word. It could only carry days.

## The Sledge

On the twenty-first the sledge runner worked loose again on the near side, and she spent the morning with the younger man seating it true, bar for the lifting, wedges for the seating, hands black with grease and dust. The work was slow and exact, and the exactness suited the day.

The younger man talked while they worked, about a sister married to a carrier past the mill, a child expected before the cold, a room wanted near the yard. She listened the way she listened to everything that was ordinary and farmed and lit, without envy and without turning away. Ordinary country asked nothing. That was the whole of what it asked.

She found she wanted a room near a yard. Not this yard. She stood there with a wedge in her hand at a girl’s own life and understood that she had been thinking about it for some days without giving herself the words, and that it was the first time in four months she had wanted a thing that could not be had by standing still in it.

When the runner sat true Da walked past and looked at the seating and nodded once, and the younger man grinned with grease on his teeth. That was the whole of what the morning said about mending, and it said it without words.

At midday she ate bread with her back to the wind and wrote nothing, because the date had been written in the morning, longer, hour gone, and one line a day was enough for a column that charged its hours. The oilcloth sat heavy against her ribs. Fourteen stubs. Tickets. Rates. A season carried in a fold of cloth.

What she felt was anger, cold and complete, at every reconciled page that had ever closed a name it could not close. She wanted, concretely, to keep her own column unreconciled and true, dates clean, gaps present as gaps, no lie in it. That was the want, small and hers, and it held through the afternoon.

## The Column

At the plank she wrote the date in, longer, hour gone, page held flat, and the column went down in order, true wherever it was true, blank where it went west blank. No correction. No signature she had not put there herself. Season at each end. Name at the top in two hands.

She put her hand flat on the book for a line’s length, and the book was kept by people who were not a county and were not obliged to keep it, and the column was hers, and that was the whole of what she knew.

At the head of her column there were two hands. The first was the foreman who had taken her on in the second of a month and wrote her down without asking her anything, in a flat clerk’s hand that did not care who she was. The second was the clerk on the trestle with the slate, who had written out the season at each end in a small hand and had put his own mark beside it.

She had wondered about that second hand once and had not wondered about it again. A man puts his name to a run of hours and then the run of hours is his too, in the sense that if it is ever called in to account he is the one who has to answer for it. He had known what he was doing on the second of a month four months back and he had done it anyway, for reasons of his own, and she had not asked and was not going to now.

Two hands at the top of a column was the whole difference between a wage-book and a county register. A county register is closed by somebody else and a signature underneath.

The pay-day of the twenty-seventh stood seven days ahead, unmovable, and the days between were workable, and she would work them. Ning Bo’s account worked and not settled. Ning Wu’s decade reachable and not met. The claim on a far-side book and not answered. Golden Core the second held and the third not taken. Hou unpaid. Date untouched. That was the shape of the season, and it was hers, and it held.

## The Evening

She ate bread outside with her back to the wind and counted on her knee with charcoal, clear of the ration marks. Furnace banked. Road paid. Gaps clean. Fourteen stubs. Slowed finger. Heavy end regained. Paper west. Decade reachable. Figure on a far-side book. Board holding two lines.

She folded the ticket and put it in the oilcloth, above the plate she never described, and felt the weight of it against her ribs, paper and stubs and marks, all of it hers and none of it checkable.

Under her, at the boards:

“You are true,” he said. “And it is staying true.”

The twentieth closed on a woman with a true column in a book nobody was obliged to keep, a reconciled page in a book somebody had signed, and the knowledge that one was hers and the other was not, and that the difference was the whole of what family paper meant.

She had not corrected what she could not correct. She had kept what she could keep, which was days, and days were the only currency in the country she had reached.

And a page with a signature under it was going to keep saying what it said for as long as the county that held it existed, and there was not one thing in her hands or her book or her foundation or her ground that could make it say anything else. She had known that since the sixth hour. She was going to know it for the rest of the season.
