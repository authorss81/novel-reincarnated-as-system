# The Reconciled Page

On the twentieth she worked the bank through a wind that took the dust sideways, and the stone came true under the bar, lift with the furnace, set early, move by hand, and the day held.

At about the sixth hour a word came down the bank from the low building, said the way offices say things that are not summons, without weight. The register still read reconciled. That was all. No correction. No reading. No hand put to it.

She heard it the way she heard everything about books she could not touch, plainly and without machinery, and worked on. The register still read reconciled and was not corrected. It would be read once at the close for what it was, and never corrected, and this was the reading, once, here, because the batch could carry it and no other page needed to.

What it was, was a lie with a signature under it. She knew it the way she knew her own breath. Her brother’s name stood reconciled in a book that had never held him, and the truth was unchanged, and the reconciling stood because a signature stood under it. That was the whole of what she knew, and it sat in her the way stone sits in a sledge.

## The Sledge

On the twenty-first the sledge runner worked loose again on the near side, and she spent the morning with the younger man seating it true, bar for the lifting, wedges for the seating, hands black with grease and dust. The work was slow and exact, and the exactness suited the day.

The younger man talked while they worked, about a sister married to a carrier past the mill, a child expected before the cold, a room wanted near the yard. She listened the way she listened to everything that was ordinary and farmed and lit, without envy and without turning away. Ordinary country asked nothing. That was the whole of what it asked.

When the runner sat true Da walked past and looked at the seating and nodded once, and the younger man grinned with grease on his teeth. That was the whole of what the morning said about mending, and it said it without words.

At midday she ate bread with her back to the wind and wrote nothing, because the date had been written in the morning, longer, hour gone, and one line a day was enough for a column that charged its hours. The oilcloth sat heavy against her ribs. Fourteen stubs. Tickets. Rates. A season carried in a fold of cloth.

What she felt was anger, cold and complete, at every reconciled page that had ever closed a name it could not close. She wanted, concretely, to keep her own column unreconciled and true, dates clean, gaps present as gaps, no lie in it. That was the want, small and hers, and it held through the afternoon.

## The Column

At the plank she wrote the date in, longer, hour gone, page held flat, and the column went down in order, true wherever it was true, blank where it went west blank. No correction. No signature she had not put there herself. Season at each end. Name at the top in two hands.

She put her hand flat on the book for a line’s length, and the book was kept by people who were not a county and were not obliged to keep it, and the column was hers, and that was the whole of what she knew.

The pay-day of the twenty-seventh stood seven days ahead, unmovable, and the days between were workable, and she would work them. Ning Bo’s account worked and not settled. Ning Wu’s decade reachable and not met. The claim on a far-side book and not answered. Golden Core the second held and the third not taken. Hou unpaid. Date untouched. That was the shape of the season, and it was hers, and it held.

## The Evening

She ate bread outside with her back to the wind and counted on her knee with charcoal, clear of the ration marks. Furnace banked. Road paid. Gaps clean. Fourteen stubs. Slowed finger. Heavy end regained. Paper west. Decade reachable. Figure on a far-side book. Board holding two lines.

She folded the ticket and put it in the oilcloth, above the plate she never described, and felt the weight of it against her ribs, paper and stubs and marks, all of it hers and none of it checkable.

He said one thing that night and it was present tense and it bought nothing.

“You are true,” he said. “And it is staying true.”

The twentieth closed on a woman with a true column in a book nobody was obliged to keep, a reconciled page in a book somebody had signed, and the knowledge that one was hers and the other was not, and that the difference was the whole of what family paper meant.

She had not corrected what she could not correct. She had kept what she could keep.
