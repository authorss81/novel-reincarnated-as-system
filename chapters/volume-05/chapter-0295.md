# The Hand That Wrote The Date

Nine days is a long time to stand in a reach and wait for a piece of paper to do a thing.

She worked every one of them. The ordinary end, four to the younger man’s four, the bar in her left hand and the right arm at half and the ring ticking under the boards every night.

Da had the yard on ordinary stone and the cart runs going up the track twice a day, and she was part of that, and a woman with four fingers on one hand and an arm that would not go over her shoulder was part of it, and nobody said otherwise and nobody needed to.

On the twenty-seventh the pay-day came and she stood in the queue with stone dust on her coat and the man at the plank read her run from the seventh to the twenty-seventh, days in order, hours against them, the writing hours gone on top and unpriced, the two gaps from the course of brick in plain ink in front of him.

He tore the strip off at the rule, face down, and put it into her coat the way he had put the last one there, which was without ceremony and without once looking up.

Eighteen stubs, in the oilcloth, in four marks, none checkable by anybody within nine days of road.

It was the last pay-day of the season. She knew that standing in the queue, and the man at the plank knew it standing on the other side of the plank, and neither of them said so, because a book that runs to a date at its far end does not need two people to announce it.

## The Three Days

She worked the twenty-eighth and the twenty-ninth and the thirtieth.

The pay-day is the seventh. The season ends on the thirtieth. She did that arithmetic in the middle of the third of those days, standing at the ordinary end with the bar in her left hand, and then she went on working, and then she did it again in the afternoon, and then she did it a third time at the store step, and each time it came out the same.

Fifteen coppers. Three days of a body turning stone. Gone to nobody and not to her, because the next pay-day was the seventh of the next month and by then the column would be shut and finished and she would not be standing at a plank with it.

She wanted to hear, on the twenty-eighth, that a season could be worked out to a date and then not be paid for the last three days of it. Nobody could tell her that, because everybody within nine days of road already knew it and nobody says a thing like that out loud to another person on a working reach.

## The Thirtieth

At the seventh hour on the thirtieth she wrote the date in with the flat of her second finger, longer than eleven minutes, the hour gone into the writing and staying there, and the page held flat by a palm that had not to be asked.

The column took the day in order under the far date in the other man’s hand. Days in order from the second of a month four months back. Hours against them. Twelve gaps, every one of them entered by her, every one of them true.

Then she put her hand flat on the book for a line’s length, feeling the paper under four fingers, and the book was a wage-book kept by people who were not a county and were not obliged to keep it, and the column on the near side was hers, and the season was finished.

The man at the plank turned back to the thirtieth and looked at the far date in the other hand’s writing and then at her.

“That’s the season,” he said.

“That’s the season,” Ning Yan said.

He closed the book on his palm and squared it with the heel of his hand against the plank, because the plank was not level, and that was the whole of the ceremony, and eleven men and a foreman and a clerk with a slate had put a season at each end of a woman’s column about five months ago and none of them had ever asked her a single question about either end of it.

She stood at the plank after that for a while with her hands at her sides, because there was nothing to do with them, and she had been on that reach for a hundred and forty-nine days and she was not going to be on it tomorrow.

## The Word

It came the next morning.

A hauler off the coming round stopped at the yard for water, a man she had never seen with dust in his hair and a carrier’s mark on his sleeve, and drank with his hands cupped, and talked the way haulers talk on a road, without weight. He was off the round that had gone west on the twenty-second and he was about to go west again.

“Floor west still on?” Da said.

“On. Nine men. Same nine.”

That was the ordinary end of an ordinary question and she heard it at the sledge with the bar in her hands and did not stop what she was doing, because it was not news and asking anything else would have been the end of the only road there was.

Then the man said, to nobody in particular, in the way a man says the three things he was asked to say and then goes:

“Draft came into Lowwater. Went to a counting room, took it, paid the figure. Eight hundred.”

The yard went on the way a yard goes when a hauler says a figure.

Then he said the last of it.

“Date came off the page too. Not struck out. Off.”

## The Not Struck

She stood at the sledge and did not move for about eleven counts, and in those eleven counts the whole of four years arrived in her at once and had nowhere to go.

Not struck out. Off.

A clerk strikes out. A clerk strikes out and writes beside it why, and the why is the office’s reason and the why stays in the room for ever. That is what clerks do and every clerk in nine counties does it, and she had watched it done to her own line on a member sheet and had been offered a word for the reason column and had not written one.

That was not a clerk’s hand.

That was a man who deals in dates taking a date off a page because a date against a figure that has been answered is not a fact. It is a lie. And a man whose trade is truth — a schedule-man, a man who writes schedules and knows what a date is *for* — will not sit in a room with his own hand on a lie in a book.

She knew the shape of that hand.

She had bought it once for eight days out of about ten of a man’s life, sitting at a counter with a dead weight ticket and the flat of a second finger. She had taken four sentences off it and had used it for four words. She had never once seen the man it belonged to and she was not going to see him now.

He had taken the date off with the hand that had written it, because a piece of paper had come into a room with a far-side seal on it and said the figure was answered, and the date had nothing left under it.

Luo Ping. Thirty-nine. A wife and a boy of eleven past the county’s boundary.

She did not say the name out loud. She did not say anything out loud.

## What She Could Not Ask

What she wanted was the eighth question.

She wanted to know whether he had torn it or whether he had cut it and whether the page was left standing or whether he had taken the sheet out of the book and whether anybody had looked at him while he did it. She wanted to know whether Hou Sanliang had been in the room.

She wanted to know whether Hou Sanliang had been paid, and whether being paid had been the end of anything for him, and what a man does in a counting room when eight hundred jin arrives that was never going to arrive.

She wanted to know whether the account of a furnace-man had been written off in the same room in the same week.

That was six questions and she was not going to ask one of them.

A second question is the end of the only road there is, and she had learned that on a reach nine days east of a county that had never heard of her. She was going to learn it here in the same way: standing at a sledge with the bar in her hands, saying nothing at all, while the one road she had gone down for four years came back with an answer in it.

The hauler did two sledge-loads of hauling for wedges and was paid for them in kind at the ramp and went out along the track east without looking back at the bank, which was the correct thing and cost her nothing.

What she felt, standing at the sledge, was not relief and it was not the end of anything.

It was something much smaller and much stranger, which was that a thing she had wanted since she was a girl of seventeen with a plate against her ribs and a licence she had never opened had happened on a page, in a room, on a morning, at the hands of two men neither of whom had ever met her and one of whom she had bought by name and never spoken to.

Eight hundred jin, answered. Hou Sanliang, paid, and never met, and never going to be met. The date, off the page, by the hand that wrote it.

And the four counties nine days west that had carried the figure for four years still carrying it, and would carry it for ever, because a county book has no column for a thing done past its own boundary and never has had one.

## The First

She wrote nothing that day. There was no column to write in.

She put her hand flat on the book at the seventh hour out of the habit of five months and found there was no page for it under her hand, because the run was shut at the thirtieth and a book that is shut is shut.

The first of the month closed on a woman who was no longer on a wage-book, with eighteen stubs in four marks that would never be matched to anything by anybody, a furnace of her own in the ground behind a hut, three stages that no county had heard of, four fingers on one hand and an arm at half, and no day in front of her that anybody owed her for.

She banked the ring with three handfuls of ash and sat against the boards with the oilcloth against her ribs and did not know what to do with her hands, which had had something to do with them every single hour of every single day since a day in the second month of a year four months back.
