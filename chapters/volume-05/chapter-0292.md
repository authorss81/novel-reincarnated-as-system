# Entered As Good

On the seventeenth of the month she went out at the second hour with the bar over her shoulder and stopped in the middle of the reach, in daylight, where eleven men could see her, and gave the order out loud.

She had done it twice. Both times she had done it in the open and both times she had done it at her own order and neither time she had done it in a room or under cover or at night, and there was a reason for that which she had not examined for four months and examined now in about a second and a half.

She had spent the whole of the last five months being a person no book within nine days of road could check. Stubs in four marks that nobody could read. A furnace no county had heard of. Hours in a wage-book kept by people who were not a county. A claim nobody had answered. A brother on a list she had never seen. A page reading *reconciled* with a signature under it.

Every one of those had been safety, and the safety had been made out of the fact that nothing could be proved about her. She had got across a boundary on it and had built a furnace on it and had gone nine days of road twice on it.

She wanted to be checked. That was the whole of the want, at the second hour of the seventeenth of a month, standing in the middle of a reach with a bar in her left hand. She wanted to be checked by somebody, once, and she knew that the only thing on that reach that could check a woman was a stage that eleven men could see her take.

“Now,” she said, out loud, in the ordinary voice. “At my order. Here.”

## The Third

She emptied herself the way the order required, empty first and then take, and the furnace came up under her the way a floor comes up under feet, and it was heavier than the second time and the ground was not easier.

It went through the crack in her aperture and did not widen it and did not mend it. It went through the right arm at half and left it at half. It went through the four counts of her breath and settled them without changing them, the way it had done twice.

It took her about nine counts, and there was no weather, and no trial, and nothing in the reach that made a sound.

The first time she had taken a stage, the sound had gone out of her into nine counties and every county had heard it. The second time there had been nobody within nine days of road to hear anything.

This time there were eleven men two hundred yards behind her who were looking up off their bars, and there was no sound, and none of them would ever know that anything had happened to a woman in the middle of a reach at the second hour of a working day.

That was not a loss. That was the whole trade. She was the only person on the ground who knew, and she had been the only person on the ground who knew twice before, and every one of those three hours had cost her something and given her something and never once given anybody else anything at all.

The heat came up through the base and stayed there, thinner than it had been, and thinner than it would be in a week, and running through her in four counts like the floor of a room.

## The Cost

She stood in the middle of the reach with the bar in her left hand and went through it, because there was a habit in her now of counting what had been taken and she would not stop doing it now, and the count came out different for the first time in five months.

Not the breath. Four counts, four counts.

Not the arm. Half, half.

Not the finger. Four fingers and a thumb, exactly as they had been at the second hour an hour ago.

Not the holds, not the aperture, not the crack in it, not the turn, not the writing hour, not the two days, not the course of brick.

It was not in her body at all.

That was what the third stage took, and she stood on a reach in the cold with the bar in her hand and understood it the way a person understands a step that is not there: by putting a foot out and finding nothing under it.

From this hour she was a person an office would vouch for, and she could not be un-entered, and the word good would follow her into every room she had not yet stood in, and there was not one thing in her hands that could tell her whether it was true.

She waited for it the way she waited for the ground to give something back, which was the habit of a woman who had been feeding a hole in a field for fifty days and getting nothing.

Nothing came back and nothing was taken, and she stood there and let about four minutes of that go by, and then Da, two hundred yards back, put his hand flat on the air once in her direction, which is not a word and is not a signal they had agreed on, and picked up his bar, and went back to the stone.

That was the reach’s entire account of a woman standing still in the middle of it for four minutes at the second hour. It cost nothing and it was correct.

The layer said it once in the far-side house form and stopped. It said nothing else, and nothing else was available, and it had never in four years explained or advised anything and was not going to start.

## The Seventh Hour

She went to the plank at the seventh hour with the bar over her shoulder and stone dust on her coat, because a day is a day and a stage does not move a pay-day, and she stood in the queue with three men before her who had been working since the light and had not looked at her once.

The man at the plank kept the book flat with his palm because the plank was not level, and he did it without being asked, and he did not look up.

Her run read from the seventh to the seventeenth. Days in order. Hours against them. Two gaps from the course of brick, and a stretch of ten from the road, all of it true, none of it in anybody’s hand but hers.

He tore one more strip off at the rule, face down on the plank, into her coat.

Seventeen stubs in the oilcloth in four marks, none checkable by anybody within nine days of road.

She held it for a moment before she put it away. Tear-edge straight and clean. The contractor’s mark on the back, and on the front a run of ten days with twelve holes in it, entered true by the woman the holes belonged to, on the seventeenth of a month, in the seventh hour, after she had stood still in the middle of a reach for four minutes.

Five coppers of the day into her hand. Five out at the store. The number the same both times.

## The Seventeenth

She wanted, concretely, to know whether the third stage had left anything in her hands that she could feel, and she went through the whole day looking for it.

She lifted four stones at the ordinary end, four to the younger man’s four. She sat at the trestle with a cup of water and did not drink it. At the seventh hour she put the flat of the second finger against a page and found that a line still took longer than eleven minutes, and not one minute longer than it had taken at the seventh hour the day before.

Nothing.

Her body had come through the third one exactly as it had been at the second hour before it, and the only thing that had changed was standing on a ground that now held her differently, in a record she could not reach and would never be able to reach and could never be taken out of.

What she felt through the rest of that day was a fear she had not felt since the fourth of a month four months back, and it was not the fear of a body that had been made to pay. It was the fear of a body that had been let off, because a thing that costs nothing on the day is a thing whose bill comes to somebody else, and she had been reading bills since she was eleven.

The seventeenth closed on a woman with three Golden Core stages under a furnace no county has heard of, an arm at half, four fingers on one hand, a slowed writing hour, seventeen stubs in four marks, thirteen days to a thirtieth, and a figure in a room four hundred yards behind her standing against a column that was thirteen days from having an end on it.

She banked the ring at the dark with three handfuls of ash and put the bar by the door, and she did not sleep much, and she did not dream of lines.
