# Ten Years In Another Hand

On the seventh the pay-day arrived and she stood in the short queue the way pay-day queues stand, bar over the shoulder, four fingers bound against the dust, and the man at the plank paid each in turn, strips torn at the rule, face down, into coats.

Her own run read from the twenty-seventh to the seventh, days in order, hours against them, the writing hours gone on top of them, unpriced. He tore one more strip off at the rule, face down on the plank, into her coat. Thirteen stubs now in the oilcloth in four marks, none checkable by anybody within nine days of road.

She put the strip with the rest, the thirteenth in order, no overlap and no gap, and ate bread outside with her back to the wind. Five in and five out. The number the same both times.

The run she had been paid on was nine days long and honest. Nine days of the bank, the ramp, the low stones, the writing hour gone on top of all of it and unpriced, and a line at the top in her own hand that had been the same hand since the second of a month. There was no lie in it anywhere. She had understood by now that this was the only thing in her life she had built herself, and that nobody would ever come and check it, and that she had built it anyway.

What she felt at the store step was restlessness, the kind that comes when one paper is placed and another is still unplaced. She wanted, concretely, to put ten years where somebody had to look at them without meeting the man who lived them. That was the want, and it had a brother in it she had not seen since she was eleven.

## The List

In the afternoon she worked the easy end beside the woman with the scarred forearm, who had been two places along the stones since the twentieth and now one place, and the woman talked the way women talk on banks, without looking, stone by stone.

“Carrier’s lists west carry names that aren’t crew,” the woman said. “Men kept on paper. Years against them.”

Ning Yan set a stone and waited, because waiting was a kind of asking that revealed nothing.

“One list has ten years on it,” the woman said. “Same name down the margin. Not crew. Not floor. Kept somewhere.”

She stood there with the bar under her arm and the stone not lifted and understood that she had just been handed the shape of her brother, by a woman with a scarred forearm who knew nothing about her, on a bank, in the rain-free middle of a working afternoon.

Ten years. She was eleven when he went. Ten years was a child turning into a man and a man turning into somebody she would not recognise standing at a counter, and she had spent every one of those years working out what he owed her and had never once spent a single hour working out what ten years had done to him.

That was the whole of what Ning Wu’s decade said on that reach. It was said once, by mouth, on a bank. Ten years of his own, put where somebody had to look at them, would be a later thing and a colder season, and the years on it would be his to fold and not hers to fold for him, and she would have to do it standing in a room with him in it or not at all.

She turned stone the rest of the day with the sentence under the work, and the sentence held. A name in a book, in ink, where somebody has to look at it, with ten years of his own on it. He did not appear. He did not forgive her. There was nothing yet to forgive.

## The Rain

On the eighth the rain came hard and the bank went soft, and Da called the yard off the high cuts and put them to the low bank where the stone came cleaner. She worked the low bank with water running off her sleeves, four fingers pink with cold, furnace steady through the middle of every stroke.

At midday they ate under the lean-to with rain drumming on the boards, eleven men and her, bread damp, hands steaming. The talk was about the low bank and the ramp boards and a team that had gone down the cart road in the middle of the season and taken a fortnight to come out of the cut again. A man told the whole story twice and it got worse the second time. She laughed with them at the right places, briefly, the way workers laugh at other people’s troubles that are not theirs.

The woman with the scarred forearm sat beside her with rain in her hair and had nothing to add about carriers’ lists, and did not add it, and Ning Yan was grateful to her for the not adding of it in a way she had no way to show.

In the afternoon the rain eased and the bank steamed, and the stone came easier, and she turned four for the younger man’s three, the old shape back for an hour, lift with the furnace, set early, move by hand. The man grinned without speaking. That was the whole of what the rain said about being slowed, and it said it without words.

She had a way of thinking that she had not given anybody on this reach, not because she was keeping it, but because there was nobody to give it to. On the fourth of a month four months back she would have known that on a bank four men and a woman could talk for an hour and be wrong about half of it.

Now she listened to all of it and kept none of it, and the keeping was the part that made her hands hurt in the cold, because there was no one to hand the half she had to the half that did not.

What she felt through the rain was anger, sudden and clean, at every list that kept a boy thirteen years old for a decade without letting his sister see him, and under the anger a love so sharp it sat in the hands like cold. She did not let either into the bar. The bar was yard business. The list was family paper.

## The Evening

At the plank she wrote the date in, longer than eleven minutes, permanently, the page held flat without being asked, and the hour went into the writing, staying there.

She put her hand flat on the book for a line’s length, and the book was a wage-book kept by people who were not a county, and the column on the near side was hers, and that was the whole of what she knew.

Then she stood at the plank a while longer with her hand off the page, in the ordinary way a person stands at a counter, and let herself do the one thing she had no instrument for.

She could not go west. She could not send word. She could not pay a carrier ninety coppers to carry a question to a man whose name she did not know in a place she had never stood. She had a paper on a board nine days off and a list she had not seen and a body that would do what it was told for five coppers a day, and the sum of those three was nothing at all, and she stood there and let the nothing be nothing instead of turning it into something she could carry.

Under her, at the plank:

“You are near,” he said. “And you are not there.”

The seventh closed on a woman with thirteen stubs, a furnace, a slowed finger, a father’s paper west, a brother’s decade made reachable and not met, and a season holding through gaps and writing hours.

She had not met him. She had put him where somebody would have to look. That was enough for now, and it was not enough, and both were true.
