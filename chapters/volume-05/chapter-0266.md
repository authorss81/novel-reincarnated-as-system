# The Weight Of A Season

The twenty-seventh came clear after rain, and the pay-day arrived whether or not she had worked it, and she had worked all of it but one day.

She went to the plank at about the seventh hour with the four fingers bound and the bar over her shoulder, and stood in the short queue the way pay-day queues stand, three men before her with stone dust on their coats, none of them talking.

The man at the plank paid each in turn, strips torn at the rule, face down, into coats, and read the run for each from the book, days in order, hours against them, and not one man asked a question, because pay-day answers were in the column and not in the asking.

Her own run read from the seventeenth to the twenty-seventh, days in order, hours against them, the twenty-second absent the way an unworked day stands, present as a gap and nothing else.

He tore one more strip off at the rule, face down on the plank, into her coat. Eleven stubs now in the oilcloth in four marks, none checkable by anybody within nine days of road.

She held the new stub a moment before it went into the coat, feeling the tear-edge where it had come off the book, straight and clean, the contractor’s mark on the back, and put it with the rest, the eleventh in order, no overlap and no gap.

Three men had gone before her and each of them had gone up the track afterward without looking back at the plank, and the queue behind her had grown while she stood in it, and nobody in the queue spoke, and the not-speaking had a shape she could have described to anybody who asked.

Five coppers of the day into her hand and five out for bread at the store. The number the same both times, the way it always was. The twenty-second cost what an unworked day costs, a day not paid, and nothing could move it, and she did not ask.

## The Stubs

She laid the eleven out on her knee at the store step in the order they were torn off, the way she had laid nine on the seventh, and checked them against each other by touch in the morning light.

No overlap. No contradiction. No gap. Five off the far-side plank, the seventeenth and the twenty-seventh and the seventh and the seventeenth and the twenty-seventh again, one man and one mark. Six from inside the bound over two months, hands she had stopped trying to match.

The dates came out clean. That was the rule of stubs on that reach, that dates came out clean or the stub was nothing, and all eleven came out clean.

She folded them back into the oilcloth in four marks, above the plate she never described, beside the dead weight tickets with the ration marks and the folded figures of rates, six a load and eight a day and ninety west for a person and one bag, written clear of every mark in the space above.

The oilcloth had got heavier and it had got no bigger. That was the arithmetic of a season carried in a fold of cloth against the ribs, and she had felt it every day for seven weeks without once having a name for what she was carrying, and now that it had a name she found she missed it less than she had expected to.

## The Clearing

She ate the bread outside with her back to the wind and counted on her knee with a stub of charcoal, clear of the ration marks on the ticket.

The pay-day had taken the seventh and the twenty-seventh together in one line and she had had to split them in her own head to see what was left, which took about as long as the writing of the line had taken, and which was the closest thing to a surprise the far-side book had given her.

Furnace clear. Twenty-nine coppers, twenty-three on the seventeenth and six on the eighteenth, debt done, chalk gone, and the board wiped on the nineteenth by a hand with nothing left to wipe.

Road at ninety standing eighteen days away when the road opened, now the price of a furnace already built and lit and banked behind a hut. The coming a different number nobody had quoted. The draft nine days west with no date on it.

Eleven stubs. Nine holds. Four fingers. A furnace of her own. A column with a date at each end and her name at the top in two hands.

She folded the ticket and put it in the oilcloth, above the plate she never described, and felt the weight of the season in the cloth against her ribs, paper and stubs and marks, all of it hers and none of it checkable.

What she felt was not lightness. It was the heaviness of a thing carried straight, debt paid where debt could be paid, the furnace fed, the column clean, the season holding.

## The Yard

She worked the day in the middle of the bank, the hand bound, setting down early, moving stone by hand, the furnace steady through the middle of every stroke.

By midday Da moved her back to the heavy end without a word, the way foremen move labour that has found its shape again, pointing at the ramp and walking away.

She took the heavy end with nine holds and four bound fingers and no turn at the end of the stroke, and turned stone the rest of the day the new way, lift with the furnace, set early, move by hand, and the heavy end held.

A man of about forty with a bad shoulder, who had been on the next stretch since the fourteenth and had watched her turn three for his two by the sixth on the first day, came past her on the way to the water with his bar over his shoulder and stopped, and looked at the four on the sledge in front of her, and counted them with his eyes the way a man counts stones.

“Nine now,” he said.

“Nine now,” Ning Yan said.

He went on to the water, and that was the whole of what the heavy end said about being got back.

## The Fire

She sat at the eight feet that evening with the binding still on, inside the warmth while eleven men held it, and felt the day settle the way days settle at fires, stone by stone, into something carried.

Da talked about the ramp boards with a man from the mill end, wedges and rails and where the water sat, and the talk went the way talk goes at fires, and she sat with the pay-day hours behind her in the column and the heavy end regained under her, and said nothing.

The woman with the scarred forearm was two places along the stones from her and had been two places along the stones from her since the twentieth, and tonight she moved one place along the stones, without asking and without comment, and put her bag down in the gap, and that was a thing that had been done and could not be undone by anybody.

The furnace ticked under her foundation the way it ticked at night under the boards, steady and banked, twenty-nine coppers and two holds and the turn out of her stroke, paid and done and hers.

She wanted to finish the season on the heavy end. That was the concrete want, small and hers, and it sat beside the furnace want the way stone sits beside stone, plain and unmovable.

## The Evening

At the plank she wrote the date in, eleven minutes with the flat of the second finger, the page held flat without being asked, and the column went down in order under the far date in his hand.

She put her hand flat on the book for a line’s length, and the book was a wage-book kept by people who were not a county and were not obliged to keep it, and the column on the near side was hers, and that was the whole of what she knew.

He said one thing that evening and it was present tense and it bought nothing.

“You are paid,” he said. “And you are kept.”

She went to the hut and banked the furnace with three handfuls of ash, and sat against the boards with the oilcloth against her ribs, paper and stubs and marks, all of it hers and none of it checkable.

## The Season’s Shape

She counted the season on her fingers in the dark, the way she counted everything the rain could take, days worked and days paid, and the count came out the way columns come out, in order.

Fourteenth open road. Fifteenth second asking. Sixteenth chalk strokes. Seventeenth pay and kiln six. Eighteenth six at the shed. Nineteenth furnace lit. Twentieth middle bank. Twenty-first draft west. Twenty-second road and return. Twenty-third fair hand. Twenty-fourth third refusal. Twenty-fifth news. Twenty-sixth slab. Twenty-seventh pay.

Fourteen days. Thirteen worked. One gap that stood as a gap. Twenty-nine coppers paid. Eleven stubs. Three refusals. One furnace. That was the shape of the fortnight, and it was hers, and it held.

## The Binding Off

That night the binding came off, stiff with dried sweat and stone dust, and the four fingers moved free underneath, swollen and tender and whole.

She worked each one before she stood, the way she worked everything before she trusted it, and all four held, and she folded the strip and put it in the oilcloth with the stubs, because cloth on that reach was never thrown away, and a strip that had held a hand through a pay-day was worth keeping.

The hand would hold to the end of the season. That was the knowing, plain and physical, and it sat beside the furnace want the way stone sits beside stone.

The twenty-seventh closed on a woman booked to the end of a season, paid to the day, furnace lit, debt to the shed cleared, draft west with no date, fair copy in a far-side book, three refusals standing, hand bound, heavy end regained.

The season had weight now. She carried it the way she carried the bar, with both hands, setting down early, moving by hand.
