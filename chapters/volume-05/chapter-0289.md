# What The Cut Took

The two men came at the fourth hour and did the clearing for a half day each and went back up the reach without talking about it, because Da had told them what the bank had done and had told them not to stand in the cut, and they were not the kind of men who need a second telling of anything.

She worked beside them with a bar and a wedge and no sledge, because a sledge does not come into a cut and because the bank was on the flat and a sledge is for a road.

What she could not do was lift. Four fingers on one hand could turn a stone on the flat, and could drag it, and could get a stone up onto a low bed by walking it up on the bar’s end like a step.

She could not get a stone over her shoulder at all, and the right arm came up to the height of the stone and stood there, and there was no way round that which did not take four times as long and put four times as much through the shoulder.

So she built it. Two-foot bank, broken down to the size a body can move, laid out in lines on the flat of the reach with the gaps between them, and then the men came along the lines with sledges and barrows and took it up the reach to the cart, and she walked ahead of them and put the stones where a sledge could get at them.

That is what a person is on a reach who cannot lift. She is not the strongest body within nine days of road any more. She is the person who knows where the stones are supposed to be, which is worth less than being the person who can lift them and more than nothing, and the eleven men on that yard understood both halves of it inside a morning without either half being said out loud.

## The Yard Without Talking

The yard did what a yard does, which is arrange itself around a thing nobody discussed.

By the middle of the second day there were two sledges going up the reach on a fixed rhythm and a man waiting at the top of each one, and the stones were coming off the flat in lines with the gaps between them, and nobody had said out loud that it was a system and nobody had to.

A man brought water up to where she was laying lines, because a man brings water up. A man took a bar off a wedge and used it on a stone she could not lift, and put it back, and carried on.

She worked fourteen hours that day. It was the longest she had worked in five months and she could remember exactly what it had cost, because the cost arrived at about the ninth hour and it was the four fingers and the four fingers told her about it every single time she put a hand flat on a stone to walk it up the bar’s end.

At about the seventh hour the man from the mill end crouched down in the lines with her without being asked and worked a row out to the bar, on his hands, at her pace and not his, and neither of them said anything for about half an hour.

Afterwards he stood up, looked down the reach at the eleven feet of bank they had between them, and said: “You could not do this on the first day.”

“No,” she said.

“You will not be able to do it in a week.”

“No,” she said.

He looked at her for about as long as a man looks at a bar he has just used and put back down.

“So it is worth doing now,” he said, and went to his end.

That was the whole of what eleven men and a foreman said to a woman about the fact that she was getting weaker at the only thing she had ever been able to do, and it was the whole of what they said about anything else that week, and she carried it down to the sledge in the dark and put the bar by the door.

## The Heavy End

The man with the bad shoulder found her on the second day and stood at the edge of the lines looking at her right arm for about as long as a man looks at a thing he has already worked out.

“Ramp’s done,” he said.

“Ramp’s done.”

“I’ll take the heavy end when the bank’s off.”

“You can have the heavy end when the bank’s off.”

That was all. Two men do not discuss a woman’s arm on a working reach. They state the fact it has made and they move on, and what she felt when he said it was something she had not been expecting at all, which was a small clean relief, because a number said out loud by a man who has counted your loads for five months is a different animal from a number she has arrived at alone at four in the morning with her own hand.

Then the anger came, and it came the way it always came, later and lower, sitting down under the first thing.

## The Anger

It came at midday on the second day, on the flat of the reach, with a bar in her left hand, watching a man of thirty lift a stone twice her size and put it on a sledge without appearing to think about it.

She had gone to the bank with a season’s wages in a column and four fingers and nine holds. The bank had taken the ramp. It had not taken the fingers and it had not taken the holds and it had not taken the breath or the aperture or the turn or the hour a line or the two holds or the turn out of her bar stroke or the twenty-nine coppers or the two days she had given to a course of brick.

It had taken the one thing she had got back three times in five months.

She stood on the flat of a reach with the anger going through her in the ordinary way it went through her, which was in the shoulders and nowhere else and without any heat in it at all, and she let it go through, because the alternative was carrying it up the reach and the alternative had never once worked.

And then, under the anger, the thing that was actually there, which she had known for a day and a half and had not let up yet.

There was not one person in nine days of road who knew that she was not on the bank that day. There was no entry in any book with her name in it saying where she had been between the fourth and the fifth hour.

The bar had taken the weight and the dust had gone off the fallen bank in a grey sheet and eleven counts had gone by, and no man on this reach had come, and none of them would have come, and the whole of it had been one woman and one arm and one piece of wood.

That was the reach. That was what the crossing had bought: a place where nothing could reach her and nothing could find her and nothing at all would happen to her that a person with a book could afterwards write down.

She had wanted that. In the second month of a year she could not name she had wanted it so much that she had walked three-quarters of a mile of bad flat to get it.

## Eleven Days

On the second day of the clearing the bank was down to about nine feet and the lines were laid and the cart was loaded twice, and Da came out to the end of the reach on foot, which he did perhaps twice a season.

Eighteen days to the thirtieth. One bank to finish. No ramp. No heavy end. Four fingers and an arm at half and a bar in the left hand.

He stood at the top of the cut and looked down at the lines of stone laid out on the flat, nine feet of them, going away in two long rows toward the cart, in the exact order a sledge could take them without a man having to stop and think.

“This is the third time you have been back on it,” he said.

“Fourth.”

“It was the third time I counted.” He looked down the rows again. “I have run stone off a bank for twenty-six years and I have never once had anybody lay a bank out for me.”

He did not say anything else. He turned round and went back up the reach, and he did not say good work and he did not say well done and he did not say thank you, and what he had actually said was that she had laid out nine feet of two-foot bank in two days with one arm, in lines a sledge could take, and that he had never seen it done.

That was the yard’s whole account of her, that day. She carried it the way she carried everything, which was in both hands.

## The Way Down

Under the reach, with the stone still going on:

“I am down,” he said. “And you are on top.”

On the thirteenth she went up the reach at the fourth hour and worked the ordinary end all day, setting early, moving stone by hand, four fingers and no turn and a slowed flat and a bar in the left hand because the right would not go over her shoulder.

It held. The ordinary end held all day, and the bank was off the ground by the middle of the afternoon, and the last of it went on the cart in a load of four that she walked up onto the bed and could not have lifted an inch of it, and she stepped back from it and watched the cart go up the reach with the season’s last stone on it.

The thirteenth closed on a woman with an arm at half, no ramp, no heavy end, sixteen stubs in four marks, a season’s last stone on a cart going up a track, seventeen days left of a season and a date at the far end of her column in another man’s hand.

Something on that reach had stopped owing itself. She did not know yet what would be asked instead.
