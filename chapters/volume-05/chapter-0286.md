# The Far Date

On the fourth she went and read it.

It was at the end of her column, in the small hand of the clerk on the trestle with the slate, four inches from the edge of the page where a column ends because a person has to end it. She had read that date at least twice a month for as long as she had been on this reach, in the half-second it took a glance to travel down a page of dates in order.

She had not read it once. She had read the run and the gaps and the hours and she had stopped her eye four inches short every single time, and the stopping was not a decision. A thing you have looked at a hundred times without seeing is not a thing you decided not to see. It is a thing your eye goes round, the way a tongue goes round a missing tooth.

The Twenty-Seventh. Thirtieth. Same As Last.

It came out of her the way a thing comes out of you that you have been carrying in a closed hand, and she stood at the plank with the date gone in and understood that she had known it perfectly the whole time and had not been spending a copper of her attention on it, and that a body will do that with a fact it is not ready for.

None of it was hers to change.

## The Sum

She did the sum at the store step at midday with a stub of charcoal on the flat of a dead weight ticket, clear of the ration marks, in the ordinary way.

Twenty-six days from the fourth to the thirtieth. Two of them gone already into a course of brick. The days between are workable and she would work them.

And then the thing she had not thought about, because she had never once had to think about it, because for five months the number in her hand had been the same at the start and the end of every day and the day after that.

The pay-day is the seventh. The pay-day is the seventeenth. The pay-day is the twenty-seventh. And the thirtieth is not a pay-day.

She looked at the ticket for a while.

The twenty-eighth, the twenty-ninth and the thirtieth are working days. She would work them. The column would take them in order, the way it took every day, with her name at the top and the hours against them and the season closed at the far end in a hand that was not hers.

And no pay-day would come before the season closed, because the next one is the seventh of the next month and by then the season would be shut and the column finished and she would not be on it.

Fifteen coppers. Three days of a body turning stone at the rate a body turns stone. Gone, and not to anybody, and not to her.

She found that she was not angry about it. What she felt was the flat surprise of a person who has run the same trade for five months and has just been shown the end of the invoice.

## The Office

She went up to the low building at about the fifth hour, in daylight, with nothing in her hands.

The counter was where the counter was. The stove was giving off the same heat it gave off every day of her life on this side. Six men, coats steaming, hands round cups, and the man behind the counter with a book open flat in front of him and a sheet on a spike beside him.

He did not ask her name. He had never asked it.

“My season ends on the thirtieth,” Ning Yan said.

He put his thumb on the page and turned it back a little, and looked at the far date in the other hand’s writing, and did not seem to find anything remarkable in it.

“Then the thirtieth,” he said.

“And the figure stands against my column.”

“It does.”

“And after the thirtieth my column stops.”

He closed the book on his palm, which was what the man at the plank did with a book and which this man also did, because they were both men who kept books on things that were not level.

“Then I have a book,” he said, “with a line in it that begins at a date and ends at a date, and both dates are on the page, and the hours are between them.”

“That is a good thing to have.”

“It is a thing I can answer on.” He said it in the ordinary voice, the way a man says what a thing is for.

“A figure is a figure and it is only paid against something. If the something has a start and an end and is true all the way through, I can write it out and send it. If it goes on and on with no end on it, I cannot write it out at all, because a thing I cannot put a date to is a thing nobody past this post can put a date to either.”

She stood at the counter and understood the whole of the room before the stove had ticked twice, the way she understood things, which was fast and without comfort.

He needed her to stop. He needed the run to be finite and closed and dated on both ends before he could do the one thing the room was built to do. And he could not move the far date, and she could not move it, and neither of them had ever been going to be able to move it, and it had been written in the second of a month by a clerk with a slate who had put his own mark beside it.

Six men at their cups in a room with a stove in it, and one of them wanted something out of the busiest column on the reach, and the busiest column on the reach was hers, and the reason it was hers was that she was the only person within nine days of road who had ever crossed to this side and stood still long enough to be entered.

“Before the thirtieth,” she said.

“Any time before the thirtieth,” he said. “On the day is fine.”

## The End Of The Reach

She walked out to the end of the reach in the afternoon because there was nothing else to do with a day and because she wanted to stand somewhere the length of it.

The reach runs out about two hundred yards short of the post. That is not a fence and it is not a boundary anybody keeps; it is the place where the yard’s ground stops, and past it there is worked ground belonging to a stretch of the course that somebody else is paid for, and then the bad flat, and then nine days of road.

She had walked it perhaps fifty times in five months and had never once looked at it as a thing that ended.

It ended badly.

At the end of it there was a low bank about four feet high with a sledge track running past the top of it and no cart standing at the bottom, and she stood on the track and looked at the bank the way she looked at every bank, which is the way a person looks at a thing that is a question about where a bar goes.

The yard was not short of days. She knew that by then without asking anybody. It was short of stone, and the stone it was short of was at the end of its own reach and could not be carried the length of the reach and back by a cart that was already on its rounds.

Eleven men had taken the season’s stone off four banks in five months and there was one bank left at the end of the reach and it had to be off before the last cart, and if it was not off then the last run of the season did not count.

She stood on the sledge track at the end of the reach with a bar over her shoulder and worked all of that out in about a minute, the way she worked things out, and then she noticed that she already knew what the answer was going to be, because she knew what foremen said and what wages were and what the arithmetic of a book looked like when the book was eleven men.

Nobody is going to hire on that. That was the sentence, and she had not heard anybody say it, and it arrived in her head with the clarity of something a man had said to her.

She walked back up the reach with the bar on her shoulder and the sun going down behind the rise and did not tell anybody anything, because it was not hers to tell and because the far date was in a hand that was not hers and she had four days to find out what she was going to do with the rest of it.

## What It Came To

She walked back down the track with the light going and the bar over her shoulder and the whole of it assembled in her head without being asked for, which was how the important ones always came.

Twenty-six days. One figure. One column. One office that could answer and one county nine days west that would never hear that it had been answered, because a county book has no column for a thing done past its own boundary and never has had.

And under all of it the thing she had been carrying since the second of a month, which was that the only instrument on this side of the line was a column of days written by hand by a woman with four fingers on one hand, and the whole of what that column could do was carry days, and a figure could be paid against days and nothing else in this country.

She wanted, concretely, to know one thing before the thirtieth, and she knew what it was, and she had known it since the twenty-ninth of a month five weeks ago, and she had not said it to him and was not going to.

What she felt walking down that track in the last of the light was dread, plain and physical, and it was not about hunger and had never been about hunger. It was the dread of a person who has spent five months building one true thing in a country where true things are not currency, and has just been told by a man with a stove in his room that the building has a date on it.

The fourth closed on a woman twenty-six days from the end of the only run of hours in the world that a figure could be paid against, three days from it that she would work and never be paid for, and a season she could read the end of at last and could not un-read.

She had wanted to know the date since the second of a month four months back. It had been four inches from her eye the whole time.
