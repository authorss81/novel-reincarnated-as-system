# The Wash House Door

On the thirteenth a rain came in the night and the yard spent the morning on wedges and rails, because sound in the ground loosened fixings the way weather loosened them, and Da moved two men to the ramp boards to check them.

She held the bar for the lifting while a man half her age drove the wedges, and the furnace ran through the middle of the hold, steady and banked, and the rails came true. The work was ordinary and she worked it the way ordinary days were worked, stone after stone, breath in four counts, arm open a third of the time.

A wedge is a piece of iron that has to go in with a hammer and stop when it is in. She had held bars for four months and this was the first work since the second stage where her hands knew they were the ones that mattered. Four fingers on a bar can lift anything. Four fingers on a wedge hold it while a man brings iron down on it, and there is no way to do that wrong if you have decided not to.

At midday the woman with the scarred forearm said her sister washed at a house nine days west and the door stood open for carriers’ women, the way women say things that are not invitations, and Ning Yan heard the wash house in it, the girl of twenty-four at a wash house, leg mended, copying lists, holding a bunk secret years back.

They ate in the lee of the sledge because there was nowhere out of the sun, and the woman with the scarred forearm had a heel of bread and a half onion and cut the onion across in her thumb, which is how a person shares when they have nothing that can be shared whole, and Ning Yan took the smaller piece because she was the one who had asked nothing and got the most, which is the rule on a working reach.

The woman said her sister had been at that wash house since before the leg broke and had not left it, and that the door stood open for anybody off the road and that it was the only room she had ever stood in where nobody wanted to know a woman’s county.

She said nothing with it. The wash house was nine days west, out of her reach, doing the only thing it was built to do, with a door open for women who came in off the road, which was more than most of the things she had left standing in that county could say for themselves.

What she felt was tenderness, sudden and unguarded, for a girl with a lath and cord for a splint who had copied carriers’ lists fair for an estate that was not hers so nobody answered for it twice. She wanted to send word that the door held. She had no instrument and no date for it and she held the wanting anyway, the way she held the furnace, without knowing what it was doing there.

## The Slab

In the afternoon a slab came off the bank wrong, edge under, weight sideways, and it went toward her foot the way slabs go when the bank is wet, without warning and without malice.

She moved the way she had moved since she was sixteen, bar over, weight turned, foot clear, and the slab went past her boot and settled against the sledge with a sound like a door shutting. Nobody saw it happen. There was no version of this where a man came, because the bank was hers and the hands were hers, four fingers and nine holds and a slowed finger and a furnace under them.

It was a wet bank and a bad slab and about two seconds of daylight in which a four-fingered hand came down on a stone instead of on a foot. She had had eleven slabs in two months and had missed this one by the width of a boot. Eleven.

She stood with the bar in her hands breathing in four counts until the breath settled, and felt the risk settle with it, plain and physical. A furnace was not a mending and never offered to be one. A slowed finger was not a mending either. The body was what it was, and it held.

After the slab she spent an hour with the younger man checking the sledge runners, because a slab gone sideways strained fixings, and the runners had shifted a finger’s width on the near side. They righted it with the bar for the lifting and wedges for the seating, her hands on the bar while he drove the wedges, furnace running through the middle of the hold. Da walked past and looked at the seating and nodded once, and that was the whole of what the yard said about a slab missed and made good.

They went back over the near-side runner three times, which is twice more than a runner needs, and the younger man asked her once, without looking at her, whether she had ever had one go at her before, and she said she had had eleven and missed this one, and he said, right, and drove the last wedge.

Afterwards they went back over the working bank together, face to the cut, and made him take the next slab out himself, and she stood behind his shoulder and said nothing at all for eleven minutes while he worked it, which was the whole of what she had to teach and it was not a technique.

She had told him more in five words than she had told the man with the bad shoulder in a month. It was not a confidence. It was that a man who has been half her age and has held a bar for her for a week has seen the four fingers and done the arithmetic, and there is nothing to be gained on a working reach by being the last person to say a number.

What she felt then was fear, clean and late, arriving after the danger the way fear arrives on banks, and under it relief that sat in the legs like water.

## The Evening

At the plank she wrote the date in, longer, permanently, hour gone into the writing, page held flat without being asked, column going down in order under the far date in his hand.

She put her hand flat on the book and felt the paper under the four fingers, and the column was hers, and that was the whole of what she knew.

Under her, with her hand still flat on the book:

“You are whole,” he said. “And you are holding.”

The pay-day stood four days ahead, unmovable, and the days between were workable. Behind her the furnace ticked under the boards and the clay of the ring had gone a colour in the wet that it had not been before, darker where the rain had got into the seam, and she would look at the seam in the morning and put more clay on it if it wanted it.

The thirteenth closed on a woman with a furnace, a slowed finger, thirteen stubs, a slab missed by a boot’s width, a wash house door open nine days west, a father’s paper west, a brother’s decade reachable, a figure on a far-side book, and a season holding.

Nine days of road each way had been the largest deadline she had ever walked into and the largest thing she had ever walked out of. The bank had given her a second one that afternoon, in the space of two seconds, with nobody in it.

She slept with the bar by the door, which she had not done in four months, and did not dream of lines.
