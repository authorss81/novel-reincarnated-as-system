# Told Before Spent

On the fourteenth Da closed the run off at the top of the reach with his thumb on the last line of it, and she stood at the far end with her hand flat on the cart bed where the load had lain, and the bed was still warm, and behind her the bank was a flat scar with nine feet of lines scraped out of it and nothing on the track but the barrows going back.

It was a listed run. Every run the yard put out was a listed run, in the way that every job a place sets down is a job in the world’s own bookkeeping before anybody has said the word for it, and when a listed run is closed something settles out of the ground it was done on.

She felt it as a cold that came up through the base at about the fourth hour, not into her but through her, the way a thing comes up through a floor from a room below, and it went away as fast as it came and she put her hand on the ring behind the hut and found it hot and did not think anything more about it.

She thought about it a good deal more over the next two days.

## The Team

Wei Ran came past the reach at about the sixth hour with a team and an empty cart going out, at the speed a carter goes when she has a load to find, and pulled up without being asked and without getting down.

“Bank’s off,” she said.

“Bank’s off.”

“Nobody would have done it. You did it.” She said it the way she said everything, which was as a fact about ground. “Both of those are going to be true for about a year. After that only one of them is.”

“You are going to be two days late if you stop here.”

“I am going to be two days late somewhere.” She put the reins over her hand. “East of the turn the going is short of carts and a team carried past the turn is paying over the ordinary. Eight a load where it is five, and the mill side empties first.”

Eight a load. Five coppers a day at a trestle, five in and five out and the number the same at the start and the end of every day. Eight a load, past the cart turn, two days east, for a body with four fingers on one hand and an arm that would not go over her shoulder.

“Eight is over,” Wei Ran said, before she was asked. “I have taken a load over at eight before with a sledge I had to drag. Take it below eight and you are being paid what a two-handed woman is paid for a thing a two-handed woman does.”

“And you are telling me this because.”

“I am telling you this because the going is short of carts and I have a team and you are standing at the end of a reach with nothing to carry and an arm.” She flicked the reins. “And because you have been on this ground five months and you have never once taken anything off it that anybody paid for.”

Then she went, at the speed a carter goes, and did not look back, and that was the whole of what Wei Ran had ever been, and it was a trade and a rate and a road and an opinion, and nothing in this book was ever going to be a romance.

Ning Yan watched the cart go up the track and did not take it, and could not have said out loud why, except that the thirtieth was in sixteen days and a column that starts again in the middle of a season is a column with a hole in it in front of everybody, and she had put two holes in that column herself on the first of the month in plain ink.

## The Cold

It came again at the fourth hour of the fifteenth, standing at the ordinary end with the bar in her left hand, and this time she was ready for it, because she had been thinking about it for a day and a half, and it came up through the base and through the cracked aperture and into the four counts of her breath and sat there for the length of about four, and then it was not there any more.

Under her, with the bar under a stone:

“There is something settling,” he said. “It settled at the fourth hour yesterday and it has settled again. It comes off a listed run when a listed run is closed. I am taking a share of it.”

“Take it.”

“I have taken it. I am holding it. It is yours and it was earned with your hands on that bank and I am not spending it in the next minute.” He said it in the ordinary voice he used for things that were about her body. “I will tell you what it is for before I spend it. That is the whole of the arrangement and I have never once broken it.”

## What It Is For

She set the stone down and stood with the bar along the sledge and waited for it, and it took him about eleven seconds, which was longer than she had expected and shorter than she wanted.

“One line,” he said. “In the record the offices read. It says the balance carried forward against you is discharged, and it says the balance is no longer carried, and it says there is nothing standing against your name in it.”

“The furnace account.”

“The furnace account. Your father’s line. Unsettled, balance carried to the nearest unspent thing, credit held, six years carried, and the nearest unspent thing has been you since you were eleven.”

The wind came across the reach and moved the dust along it in a low sheet, and she stood in it and let the sentence arrive, and it arrived the way the true ones always arrived, all at once, and too late to be refused.

“You want to write off my father’s account.”

“I want to discharge what was carried forward on it. That is the same thing and it is not the same thing, and the difference is that writing an account off says the account was wrong and discharging a balance says the balance is gone.” He paused, and the pause was not for effect; it was the pause of a thing being got exactly right by a mind that had nothing else to do.

“I cannot reach a county nine days west. I have no hands and I have no road and I have never once in four years been anywhere she was not. I can put one line in a record that an office reads, and that is the whole of what I can do, and it is not nothing, and it is not very much.”

“Pay for it.”

“It comes out of your foundation.”

She waited for the rest of it and there was no rest of it, and the absence of a rest of it was the thing she had been waiting for, because she had been waiting for it since the fourth of a month four months back and there had never been one.

“How much.”

“I do not know.” He said it without any weight on it at all, the way a man says a thing he has said before and will say again.

“Not a part of it, not an amount of it, not a stage, not a hold, not a finger. Not one person alive has ever been able to tell you how much, and I have been the one taking it for four years and I could not tell you now if you put the whole of it in a line.”

## The Yes She Did Not Say

She stood at the sledge on the ordinary end of a stone yard at the fourth hour of the fifteenth of a month with the bar in her left hand and the wind going along the ground past her, and she had about four seconds in which to say no, and she had said no three times in three rooms in this country already and every one of those had been a refusal of a thing she did not want and this was not that.

This was the account. Six years carried forward, and she had been eleven when it was entered. A furnace column in four counties with a credit held against a child. A word put in a register by three men who wanted her signature, and a brother standing behind a word that said settled. Every stage she had taken for four years and every hour of every one of them, and a plate, and an arm, and a finger, and an hour a line for the rest of her life.

All of it a payment. All of it *on an account*. That was the thing she had never been able to put into a sentence, and it had been sitting under every cost she had ever paid like a floor under a table, and a payment is not the same as being robbed, and she had been telling herself the difference for four years without ever once being asked to.

He was offering to take the account away.

She did not say no.

That was all it was.

She did not say no, and she did not say yes, and she did not thank him, and she went back to the bank and lifted four stones up the line before the light went, and the fifteen closed on a woman holding something in her hands that was hers and was going to be spent, and two days to the pay-day, and fifteen to the thirtieth, and a line in a record four hundred yards behind her that had not been written yet.

Going up the reach at the top of the slope with the last load she could lift alone, where there was nobody at all:

“You are owed,” he said. “And I am paying it.”

She had known since the fourteenth that the cold at the fourth hour was his and not hers. She had known it the way she knew the flat of her own second finger against a page. She had decided not to think about it for a day and a half and had thought about nothing else.
