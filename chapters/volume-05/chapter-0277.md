# A Brother’s Standing Debt

On the fifth she laid the stubs out on her knee at the store step in the order they were torn off, the way she had laid nine on the seventh and eleven on the twenty-seventh, and checked them against each other by touch in the morning light.

Twelve now in the oilcloth in four marks, none checkable by anybody within nine days of road, dates clean, no overlap and no gap. Beside them the dead weight tickets with the ration marks and the folded figures of rates, and the space above where the paper for her brother had been, now empty, because the paper was on a board nine days west where somebody had to look at it.

She folded them back into the oilcloth, above the plate she never described, and felt the weight of the season in the cloth against her ribs. What she felt was not lightness. It was a sharp sort of love, the kind that has no machinery in it at all, for a name that had been open since before she was born.

Stubs were not worth much. That was the thing nobody on that reach knew and she had worked out over two months at a cost of her own. A stub is a promise that a strip of a book said something, and it cannot be checked by anybody within nine days of road, and nine days of road is where every office that matters sits. She had carried eleven and then twelve of them against her ribs every working day since the seventeenth of a month and they had never once been worth anything to anybody, including her.

And she kept them anyway, in four marks, in order, no overlap and no gap, because a person who cannot be checked keeps what is checkable.

What she wanted, sitting on a step with twelve stubs in her lap, was to know what Ning Bo’s account actually held now that the paper was where somebody had to look at it. She had no way to know that. She wanted it anyway, the way a person wants weather.

## The Mouth

At midday a hauler off the coming round stopped at the yard for water, a man she had never seen, with dust in his hair and a carrier’s mark on his sleeve, and drank with his hands cupped, and talked the way haulers talk on a road, without weight.

“Floor west still on?” Da said.

“On,” the hauler said. “Nine men. Same nine. Paper on the board nobody’s taken down.”

Ning Yan stood at the sledge with the bar in her hands and heard it the way she heard everything from nine days west, by consequence, once, without asking a second question, because asking a second question would reveal that it was not news to her.

“Whose paper?” Da said.

“Furnace-man’s,” the hauler said. “Old account. Name on it somebody has to look at now.”

She turned stone the rest of the afternoon with the sentence under the work, lift with the furnace, set early, move by hand, and the sentence held the way sentences hold on that reach, without comfort and without threat.

Nobody’s taken down. That was the four words she turned the stone over on. A paper on a board in a low building is a thing that ages. It gathers dust and a thumbprint and the smell of the stove, and after long enough it is simply part of the board, and the men who came in after it did not notice a sheet under a date because a sheet under a date is not a thing a floor stops work for.

She wanted it to be a thing a floor stopped work for. She wanted a man to take it down, read it, and not put it back. She wanted it to be an event. She was not going to get that, and she understood in the middle of the afternoon that she had never been going to get that, and that a paper waiting to be looked at is exactly what she had put it there to be.

The hauler stayed about twenty minutes and did two sledge-loads of hauling for wedges and was paid for them in kind at the ramp, and went out along the track east without looking back at the bank, which was the correct thing and cost her nothing.

## The Shape Of It

On the sixth she worked beside a man of about sixty from the mill end, eleven years on the reach, who talked while he worked the way old workers talk, without stopping the bar.

“Old accounts never close clean,” he said. “They close worked. Somebody’s boy does a thing and the book moves a line. That’s the shape of it.”

Ning Yan set a stone and listened, because listening was not asking and old workers knew the difference.

“Furnace-man’s boy?” the man said. “Name on a board west? That’s worked. That’s more than most get.”

She said nothing. The paper was on a board nine days west where somebody had to look at it, and a hauler had said so on the yard, and now an old worker said the shape of it without knowing it was hers. Two mouths, neither knowing, both true. That was the way family paper moved on a working reach, by mouths that did not know whose it was.

It also told her the thing she had not let herself think on the road, and the thing was this: she had done the part she could do. She had walked nine days twice and put a sheet of paper under a date on a board, and from here the account moved on its own. She had no second page to place and no second errand to walk. What was left was her brother, and what her brother did with a page that said his name had been owed a place.

At dusk she wrote the date in at the plank, longer, hour gone, page held flat, and the column took it in order. The man at the plank did not look up. The book was kept by people who were not a county, and the column was hers, and that was the whole of what she knew.

She walked back to the hut with the sentence doubled, worked and not settled, and banked the furnace with three handfuls of ash, and sat against the boards feeling what was open sit open without asking to be closed.

## The Fire

At the fire that evening the talk went the way talk goes at fires, wedges and rails and where the water sat, and she sat with the column behind her and the paper west in front of her, and said nothing.

The old worker from the mill end with ash on his coat sat across the fire from her and looked into it a long while and did not say anything, and she understood that he had said his piece about the shape of accounts in the afternoon and had come to the evening finished, and that a man who has said the true thing about a thing in the morning will not say it twice at night in front of the woman it was true about.

What she felt at the fire was grief, plain and physical, for a father taken for a stalled ascension when she was eleven, furnace-man, account open, and under the grief a steadiness she did not expect, the kind that comes when a paper is where somebody has to look at it and cannot be unwatched.

Under the talk, where it was only hers:

“It is open,” he said. “And it is holding.”

The sixth closed on a woman with twelve stubs, a furnace, a slowed finger, a paper west holding a father’s name where somebody had to look at it, account worked and not settled, and a season with weight in it.

She had put the paper where it belonged. The rest was his doing, and not yet.
