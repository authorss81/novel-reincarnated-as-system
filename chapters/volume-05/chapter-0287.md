# The Last Of The Stone

The seventh came clear and cold and she stood in the short queue with the bar over her shoulder and stone dust on her coat, three men before her, none of them talking.

The man at the plank paid each in turn, strips torn at the rule, face down, into coats, and read the run for each from the book.

Her own run read from the twenty-seventh of the month before to the seventh of this one. Days in order. Hours against them. The writing hours gone on top of them and unpriced. Two gaps in it where two days stood, put in by her own hand four days ago, in front of him, with a reason she had given him in four words and he had written nothing under.

He tore one more strip off at the rule and put it face down on the plank and into her coat.

Sixteen stubs now, in the oilcloth, in four marks, none checkable by anybody within nine days of road.

She held the new one a moment before it went in. Tear-edge straight and clean, the contractor’s mark on the back, and the figure on the front that was a run of ten days with two holes in it, entered true, entered by the woman the holes belonged to. She put it with the rest, the sixteenth in order, no overlap and no gap.

Five coppers of the day into her hand and five out at the store for bread. The number the same both times, and it would be the same both times on the seventeenth and on the twenty-seventh, and then it would stop, and she had twenty-three days to find out what stopping was.

What she felt in the queue was a kind of flatness that was not despair, and under it the thing she had been carrying since the fourth of the month, which was a date at the far end of a column in another man’s hand, and the only instrument she owned.

She wanted, concretely, to see the last of the season’s stone come off that reach. That was the want, and it was about ground and not about money, and she had not known until the queue was moving that those were different things.

## The Trestle

Da put out the last of it at the second hour, at the trestle, to the eleven men and to her, in the ordinary voice a foreman uses when he is reading a fact off his own mind.

“Last run of the season is the low bank at the end of the reach. Four days for a crew. It has to be off the ground before the last cart or the season’s last run does not count and the yard is a yard that did not finish.”

Not one of the eleven said a word, because there was nothing in it that a man on a trestle needed to say out loud.

“After that it is stone on a cart and the cart goes and I have no work for anybody until the season turns,” Da said. “Five coppers a day is five coppers a day. Nobody on this reach gets more than that and nobody on this reach gets paid for standing about.”

He looked down the line of them, the way a foreman looks down a line of them before he says the part he does not enjoy.

“I am not hiring on the bank.”

## Why Not

The question went out of her mouth before she had decided to put it there, which had happened three times in five months and each time had cost something.

“Why not.”

Da looked at her. He did not answer for a while, and she understood that he was not deciding whether to answer. He was finding the true thing, because Da did not have a false thing in him to reach for.

“Because it is undercut and it is the worst ground on this reach and a crew would want more than five for it and I cannot pay more than five, and I am not going to sign a man to a bank at the end of a season for four days when the ground decides to go on the fifth,” he said.

He turned the bar over in his hands.

“And if I hire on it, there is a wage against it, and it comes out of the same book every day you have worked comes out of.”

“You put a crew on that bank and you take four days of wages out of eleven men to pay four days of wages to five. The arithmetic does not work. The arithmetic not working is me telling a man to take a pay cut for a bank I am not sure of.”

“Then hire nobody.”

“Then hire nobody,” he said, “and the season’s last run does not count, and I have told you that instead of deciding it for you.”

## Her Own Terms

She stood at the sledge with the bar in her hands and understood that she had just been told the truth by a man who had no reason to tell her anything, and that the truth was that nobody on this reach was going to take that bank off, and that the reason was not cowardice and was not meanness and was the correct decision of a man with a book.

She wanted it the way she had wanted the paper for her brother’s name, and she could not have said out loud which of the two wanted it harder.

She did not ask Da to put her on it, because there was no way to put her on it. A wage comes out of the column. A column carries days.

What she would be doing on that bank was not work, because work is on a column and this would not be on one, and it would not be paid, and it would not be in the book, and if it came down on her on the fourth day there would be no entry against her name anywhere in nine days of road saying that she had gone there.

“I will take it off,” Ning Yan said, “and it will not be in your book, and I will not be paid for it, and I will tell nobody on this reach that you said no.”

Da looked at her for about as long as it takes to count five.

“You will tell me tonight whether the face is holding or not,” he said. “That is the only thing I am asking and I am asking it because I have to write something down about it tomorrow.”

Then he went back up the bank and left it there.

## The Face

She walked out to the end of the reach in the afternoon with the bar and no sledge, because a sledge cannot go where she was going, and there is nothing past the cart turn that a sledge will follow.

The bank was a low cut about four feet high and two days’ work for one man and nine days for a crew, which was the four days he had said and which was four days for a crew because a crew is nine men and a bar and a wedge and the working out of where a thing is going to fall before it falls.

She stood in front of it for a long time without moving.

The face was cut on the low side. She knew what that meant in the ordinary way every woman who has worked a bank for four years knows what it means, and she had spent a fortnight at the age of eleven in a bank like it, and the knowledge came up into her hands before it came into her head.

There was a seam in the clay under the face, and the seam ran up and back into the cut at about the height of her chest, and above the seam the face stood over on nothing at all, holding itself up by friction and by the fact that nobody had ever undercut it.

She put the bar against it, gently, and the face gave about half an inch and settled back.

It was waiting. That was the whole of what it was doing. It was standing there in the cold with a half inch of give in it, and it would stand there as long as she did not dig under it, and she had twenty-one days before the far date and four fingers on one hand and no sledge and nobody within a day’s walk in either direction.

The woman with the scarred forearm came past behind her at the fourth hour going east with her bag over her good shoulder and looked at the bank for about three seconds.

“That is the one they will not take,” she said.

“That is the one they will not take,” Ning Yan said.

The woman went on east without asking anything else, because she had said the true thing and she was not a woman who said things twice.

What she felt standing in front of that face in the last of the light was the plain and physical knowledge that she had twenty-one days, four fingers and nine holds, and a season that ended on a date she had only known for three days, and a face that would hold exactly as long as she did not ask it anything.

She wanted, more than the stone and more than the season and more than the figure in the room four hundred yards back, to get one bar’s width under that face before dark.

She got one bar’s width under it. It was the last hour of light and she was in to the thigh, and she came out of it and banked the face with a board and a wedge and stood over it in the dark with her breath going in four counts, and she had to be back on it at the fourth hour in the morning, and nobody in nine days of road would know whether she had been.

The seventh closed on a woman with sixteen stubs in four marks, a season with a date at the end of it, a figure in a room that needed the date to come, and a face four feet high at the end of a reach that had half an inch of give in it and was waiting to find out what a four-fingered hand would do about it.
