# The Keeper’s Paste

On the seventeenth the pay-day arrived and she stood in the queue with stone dust on her coat, and the man at the plank paid each in turn and read the run for each from the book, days in order, hours against them.

Her own run read from the seventh to the seventeenth, days worked, writing hours gone on top, gaps none. He tore one more strip off at the rule, face down, into her coat. Fourteen stubs now in the oilcloth in four marks, none checkable by anybody within nine days of road.

She put the strip with the rest, the fourteenth in order, and ate bread outside with her back to the wind. Five in and five out. The number the same both times.

What she felt was steadiness, flat and earned, the kind that comes when a column holds through road and stage and slowed writing. She wanted, concretely, to see the keeper’s strip go up and know without reading what it carried. That was the want, and it had a post and paste in it.

## The Strip

In the evening the keeper came up the rise with his cart, a man of about fifty-five who had kept a board nine years and never read a word of it, and put a strip up with paste, wet and pale, among the strips going up every nine or ten days whether or not there was anything in them.

He did not read it out. He stepped back down the ladder about a foot, looked at it from the side the way a man looks at work he has just done, and came down the rest of the way, and picked the paste pot up off the ground where he had set it, and went back up the track.

She saw it from the bank, a dark line on pale cloth, still damp, and she knew what it was without reading, because she had watched him write a thing he could not read twice before, once on the twenty-ninth of a month when the sound had been hers, once on the twenty-eighth when it had come out of the west.

The paste goes pale first and then goes hard and then goes grey, and it takes about four days to go grey in this weather, and she had watched this strip from the moment his hand left the board to the moment he went up the track out of sight. Then she turned back to the bank and finished the load she was on, and set it down true, and the bar did what the bar did.

Under the reading, in no hand anyone living uses, there was a line that was his. She could not see it from forty yards and did not need to.

She knew what was on that strip the way she knew the flat of the second finger against a page, and she stood at the foot of her own sledge with the bar in her hands and understood that from that hour an office past the line could read him without her, and that it had cost him something with a date in it, and that she was the only person alive who knew what.

She did not go up and look at it. She had decided on the road, nine days west, that she would not, and she had not, and she kept to it the way she kept to everything else on this reach, which is to say without help.

Keeping to it cost her something, and she had not expected that either. She had told herself the not-looking was dignity, and it turned out to be arithmetic. If she went up to that post and stood under the strip with her hand near it, she would be a woman standing next to a man who had just been made findable, and everything that meant would be in the standing, and there would be nothing afterwards that could put it back where it had been before.

So she kept four hundred yards between herself and a post with a board on it, in daylight, on purpose, for the rest of the season. She had held the bar with four fingers for four months and got used to that. This was the other thing, and it was not in the hands.

## The Queue

On the eighteenth she stood in the bread queue at the store with three carriers’ women ahead of her, all of them talking about the road east going soft at the valley crossing, planks lifted, teams doubled. She listened without joining, bread bought at five coppers, eaten outside with her back to the wind, and the talk went past her like water past a stone.

One of the women said her husband kept a tally of crossings, marks for sound days and blank days, and the soft days matched the sound days more often than not. Ning Yan heard the drift in it, the residue running along the line and pooling where the ground was soft, and said nothing, because the tenth part was not given and nothing proved the crews wrong. Ninety parts stood. That was the whole of it.

She had crossed that bridge nine days east of here and gone over it with her bag high on her back and her heart going, and she would cross it again before the season turned if she ever walked east, and she would cross it the way she had crossed it, because a bridge is a bridge and a plank is a plank and a woman who will not cross a bad bridge in the rain is a woman who will not cross anything.

She walked back to the bank with the bread in her coat and the sentence kept where she kept everything, plainly and without machinery. The keeper’s cart stood at the post with the paste pot on the ground beside the ladder. The strips stood damp among dry ones. The board kept what it kept because that was what the board was for.

At the bank she took the heavy end for the afternoon, lift with the furnace, set early, move by hand, and the heavy end held through the heat. The slowed finger ached at the writing hour and not at the stone hour. The stone asked nothing of speed.

She worked six stones to the younger man’s four and did not count them, which was new. For four months she had counted everything twice, once with the hands and once with the head, in case the head was wrong, and the counting had been the only part of the work that was still whole about. She left it alone this afternoon. The bar went under and came up and the stone landed where it was supposed to, and that was enough arithmetic for one day.

She walked past the board without slowing and without looking, the way she had walked past it on the tenth and the twenty-eighth and the twenty-first, because she was not going to stand in front of it again.

## The Fire

At the fire that evening eleven men and her sat at the eight feet, the fire let up into the stones, cold steady, and the strip was the keeper’s business and the yard’s business was stone, and the two of them sat an hour apart across the same dark and neither came near the other.

The furnace ticked under her foundation, steady and banked, and the slowed finger rested in her lap, four fingers and a thumb, swollen and tender and whole, and the hand would hold to the end of the season. That was the knowing, plain and physical.

What she felt at the fire was grief for a date lost off a line, his and not hers, an apology kept and a day gone, and under the grief a steadiness she did not expect. She did not put either anywhere. They had no instrument and no date.

Under her, at the fire:

“I am kept,” he said. “And I am keeping.”

The seventeenth closed on a woman with fourteen stubs, a furnace twice lit, a slowed finger, a board holding two lines that were hers and his, a father’s paper west, a brother’s decade reachable, a figure on a far-side book, season holding.

No office nine days west knew either line. One office past the line could now read both, and could read both without her, and would do it the next time the keeper came up with his cart and a pot of paste.
