# The Listed Tending

The seventeenth came with rain on the rise and a pay-day at the plank, and a job nobody on the yard would take.

She worked the morning on the bank in weather that ran off the bar and into her sleeves, and at about the sixth hour Da came down the slope with his coat dark at the shoulders and stood at the easy end looking at a bank of firebrick stacked by the end house.

The brick had come from the mill kiln, seconds and wasters sold cheap to yards for lining, and the kiln-master wanted them stacked and sorted by grade in a shed with no roof on it, in the rain, before the next firing.

Three men had turned it down that week. It paid six coppers for the day on top of the yard wage, kiln money and not yard money, and nobody had taken it because the shed floor flooded at the bottom and the brick cut the hands and the grade marks washed off in weather.

Da said it standing with rain on his face, in the ordinary way, to the yard at large.

“Six for the sorting. Shed past the mill. Done by dark.”

Two men laughed the way men laugh when they are not refusing and not accepting. The rest kept working.

A man at the easy end with a cut on his cheek said what two others were thinking, into the rain, without stopping his stone.

“Floor’s water. Marks wash. Not worth six.”

“Worth six to the kiln,” Da said. “Kiln pays it. Yard doesn’t hold it.”

That was the whole of the discussion. The shed had stood empty of sorters for a week, and the firing would not wait, and kiln money was kiln money, and the yard worked on.

## The Listing

She was halfway up the ramp with a slab under the bar when he spoke, and he had not spoken since the fifteenth, and what he said stopped her for one step.

“That tending is listed,” he said. “It is work the layer holds. I am here and I see it.”

Every sentence in the present tense, the way every sentence of his had been since she was seventeen. Three sentences and none of them bought anything yet.

She set the slab down at the top of the ramp and stood in the rain with her hands on the bar. She worked the offer the way she had worked the blind run two seasons back, before she entered it.

Listed meant the layer had it down as work nobody would take and nobody had finished. Listed meant merit would settle on it if it was cleared.

And merit was the only thing in the world that lowered what he carried, and the price of it came out of her foundation and did not come back.

She wanted the six coppers the way she wanted the seventeenth’s pay, because the shed woman’s chalk strokes stood at twenty-nine short and the seventeenth was today. That was the concrete want, and under it was the older one, the one she did not say out loud even in her own head.

She had not eaten since the bread at the seventh hour and she was not hungry, and there was no version of this day in which she turned that into something she could spend.

## The Pay-Day

She went to the plank at about the seventh hour and the man at the plank read the run and tore one more strip off at the rule, face down on the plank, into her coat.

Ten stubs now in the oilcloth in four marks, none checkable by anybody within nine days of road. Five coppers of the day into her hand and five out for bread at the store, and the number the same both times the way it always was.

The seventeenth’s pay went the way pays went, day money for days worked, and out of it she counted twenty-three coppers for the shed in the rain, owed and promised, and put the rest where bread money went.

Six short now instead of twenty-nine. Eleven days to the twenty-seventh. The arithmetic held.

She ate the bread outside with her back to the wind and went back to Da where he stood at the ramp with rain running off the rail.

“I will take the sorting,” she said.

Da looked at her hands once, the way the seller had, and nodded the way foremen nod when labour answers.

“Done by dark,” he said. “Kiln money at the shed.”

## The Shed

The shed past the mill was a three-sided thing with a sagged roof and a floor an inch deep in water, and the brick stood in wasters’ heaps sorted by nothing, and the grade marks were chalk that the rain had taken half off already.

She worked it the way she worked everything, by hand, one brick at a time, turning each in eleven holds and four fingers, reading the grade off the shape where the chalk had gone, stacking seconds to the left and wasters to the right and whole ones at the back.

The water came over her shoes in the first hour and stayed there. The brick edges found the heel-gone left hand and the four-fingered right within twenty minutes and opened both, shallow and steady, the way stone always opened her.

By the fourth hour she had the measure of it. It was the blind run’s small brother, fourteen days by hand compressed into one day in a flooded shed, no lime marks and no lime, only brick and water and a firing that would not wait.

The kiln-master had said the firing was the second week out. She worked that into the count and did not let the count work into her, and by the sixth hour the two heaps at the back had gone to one long heap she was turning the whole length of.

Her breath stayed at four counts. Her right arm stayed open a third of the time and she worked round it the way she always worked round it. Nobody came to check her and nobody relieved her. There was no version of the shed where a person came.

## The Grades

The grades were three, and she learned them by shape where the chalk had gone, the way she had learned stone by break years ago.

Seconds had the true line with a flaw in it, a warp or a chip at the corner, sound when struck. Wasters had gone wrong in the firing, bloated or cracked through, light in the hand. Whole ones rang when she set them down and stood true against the lamp she had no lamp for, checked by eye in the grey light through the open side.

She stacked seconds to the left and wasters to the right and whole ones at the back, row on row, and the stacks grew the way columns grow, in order, with nothing at the top but the next brick.

The water took the chalk off her own tally marks on the shed post twice, and twice she remarked them, and the third time she kept the count in her head the way she kept everything the rain could take, eleven and nine and four, stone and holds and fingers.

What she felt in the sixth hour was not despair. It was a clean, cold fury at the brick for being graded by shape where the chalk had washed, because shape was honest and chalk was not, and fury carried her through the last two heaps better than patience would have.

## Cleared

At dark the kiln-master came with a lamp, a man of about sixty with ash on his coat, and walked the stacks with one finger on the grades and said nothing for about four minutes.

Then he said, “Cleared,” in the ordinary voice, and paid six coppers into her hand, kiln money, wet and warm from his pocket.

He went back out through the water to the second heap and looked at that too, and came back and nodded, and did not pay for it twice, and the not-paying-twice told her more about the man than the six coppers did.

She stood in the flooded shed with water over her shoes and six coppers in a hand with four fingers on it, and she felt the thing go through the ground the way she had felt it go two seasons back on the far end of bad flat.

Settled. Not by a killing. By a finished job the layer had listed, tended by hand, in the rain, alone, with a bar left at the yard and brick instead.

He spoke before he spent anything, the way he had learned to do on the Lowwater road, with the price stated first.

“It settles now,” he said. “It costs holds out of your hand. It does not come back. I take a share and I spend it out of your foundation. Say yes and I do it.”

Present tense, every word, and the price plain. She stood in water and said it.

“Yes.”

The share went the way shares go, intercepted and spent, out of the only thing he had ever had a way of getting at, and it did not come back. Two holds out of the eleven in her left hand went with it, the two smallest, gone the way order goes when the tax takes sequence and not substance, and no practice would rebuild them.

Nine holds now where eleven had been. She felt them go the way she had felt the heel go, as order leaving and not strength, and she did not cry out, because the kiln-master was standing there with a lamp.

She walked back to the yard in the dark with six coppers and seventeen owed-against in the cloth, and the rain on the rise, and a foundation with two holds less in it than the morning had held.

Ledger she did not order and did not think about ordering. That standing stayed held and unspent, hers to order, untouched.

The seventeenth was paid. The furnace was six short. The layer had been fed once on tended work, and the cost was in her hand and staying there.
