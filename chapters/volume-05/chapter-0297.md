# Somebody Has To Look

She went up the track on the fifth of the month at the second hour and stood at the counter and asked for a line in their book, which is a thing an office does, and which she had never done anywhere in her life.

The man behind the counter had the book open flat and the sheet on the spike and six men at their cups, and he put his thumb down the margin and looked at her.

“What line.”

“A name,” Ning Yan said. “A county. Ten years.”

He did not move for a moment, and then he said, “Say the name,” in a voice that had changed, because the voice an office uses for a figure is not the voice it uses for a thing somebody is asking out loud.

“Ning Wu,” she said. “Of Lowwater. My brother.”

## What She Gave Him

She gave it to him in the order she had worked it out, on a road, over two days, standing on the ordinary end with the bar in her left hand while eleven men worked round her and one of them counted her loads with his eyes.

“Assessed at the Broken Kiln. Reassigned above furnace authority, in a hand not held by this sect. Kept on a carriers’ list ten years, and the list is not crew and not floor, and it is kept by somebody whose name the woman who told me about it did not know, and I have not met him and I have not seen the list.”

“Ten years of what.”

“His own.”

The clerk wrote it down and then stopped, and looked at what he had written, and then at her, and then he put the pen down.

“That is not how a line goes in this book,” he said.

“I know how a line goes in this book. I have been standing at a plank writing lines in a book four times a month for five months. Yours have got dates at the left and hours against them and a rate.”

“Mine have got what a book needs.”

“Your book needs this more than it needs the ninth run,” Ning Yan said. “Put it in. Put it in the ordinary hand, with the date you are putting it in against, and do not put a mark on it, because a mark on it makes it an entry about you and I do not want an entry about you.”

He picked the pen up and wrote it in, in the small hand, at the bottom of the page under a line that was not ruled for it, and he put the date against it in the left-hand margin the way you put a date against anything, and he did not put a mark on it.

Then he read the whole of it back once, under his breath, the way a man reads a thing he has written to be sure it says what he meant, and he did not read it to the room and he did not read it the way he had read the eight hundred jin in front of nine men, and the difference between the two readings was the whole distance between a thing that answers something and a thing that does not, and he knew it.

## What It Is

It is a line in a book four hundred yards behind her in a country her brother has never stood in.

That is the whole of what she has done. It is a name and a county and ten years and the fact that the years are his own and are not carried on a balance against a dead furnace-man, in a hand no person in nine days of road will ever read, in a book that is kept by people who are not a county and are not obliged to keep it, four hundred yards from a reach that eleven men work.

It is not enough. It is nowhere near enough.

The list is nine days of road away, or further, in the hands of somebody whose name the woman who told her did not have. The line is four hundred yards behind her in a book kept by people who are not a county.

She had worked out, standing at the counter, exactly what she was doing, and it was this: she had spent four years being unable to do one single thing about her brother, and now there was a thing, and it was tiny, and it was the only one, and she was going to do it and then she was going to go on being unable to do the rest of it.

She wanted a great deal more than this. She wanted the list. She wanted the county. She wanted to stand in a room with a man in it and hear him say what ten years had done to him.

She got a line. She had asked for a line. It was in a book.

She did not thank the man who wrote it, and he did not expect it, and the reason she did not thank him was not that she was withholding something.

It was that she had been on this reach five months and had learned exactly what a thanks was on a working reach, which was a way of ending a transaction, and this was not a transaction, and she was not going to turn the only page in the world with her brother’s name on it into the end of a bargain.

## Nine Feet

The man with the notebook was at nine feet off the track when she came out of the room.

He had been there some time. He had a notebook shut under his arm and no other paper of any kind, and he stood in the ordinary way a man stands who has come from past the post and has said he will be on that side of it and has been for two years.

He did not ask her a question. He had never asked her a question in any room in any county and he was not going to start on a track four hundred yards past the last working point.

“They have entered you good,” Bai Zhu said, in the ordinary voice. “I will not ask who entered you or on what ground. I do not need it and it is not mine.”

“You knew.”

“I have watched you for two years from a distance that is mine to keep and I have written four lines a day and none of them has your name in it.” He turned the notebook over once in his hand and did not open it.

“You are a good woman in a book four hundred yards from here. If there were a page I was allowed to write on, it is not one I am allowed to write on, and I would not promise you it would agree with that book.”

That was the whole of what he said and he said it without weight and without a threat in it and without anything in it that cost him anything, and it was the truest thing anybody had said to her in five months.

She did not thank him. She had not thanked him in a room in front of twenty people in a county and she was not going to start on a track, and the account between them had been carrying for four years and was going to carry for the rest of the book.

She did not tell him to stop. She had never once told him to stop and she had thought about telling him to stop perhaps nine times in five months and had not done it, and she was not going to do it now, and the reason was the same reason as the thanks and it had nothing to do with him.

He went back past the post at about the speed he always went, and she watched him to the top of the rise, and then she went down the track.

## The Sixth

She worked the ordinary end all day, because there was nothing else there was, and she lifted four stones to the younger man’s four and did not lose once, and at the fourth hour the woman with the scarred forearm came past with her bag and stopped where Ning Yan was standing.

“You are not on the board any more.”

“No.”

“My sister washed at a house nine days west,” the woman said, “and the door stands open for anybody off the road.” She shifted the bag on her shoulder and went on without finishing it, which is the way women say things that are not invitations and are not not-invitations either.

Ning Yan stood at the sledge after she had gone and worked out that she had been told, in the middle of a working afternoon, by a woman who knew nothing about her, that there was a door nine days west that stood open for anybody off the road.

She had nine days of road and a bar and a furnace and no wages and no season and eighteen stubs in a fold of cloth.

She went back to the bank.

The sixth closed on a woman with a line in a book four hundred yards behind her, her brother’s name on it and ten years of his own against it. A price taken out of her that nobody would ever be able to name.

A claim answered. A date off a page. A furnace-man’s account discharged in the only place in the world it could be discharged.

And the seventh of the month on the morrow, which was a pay-day, and a season that had closed on the thirtieth.

She banked the ring with three handfuls of ash and sat against the boards in the dark and put the oilcloth against her ribs — eighteen stubs in four marks, the dead weight tickets, the folded figures of rates, the plate above them that she never described — and did not open it.

In the hut that night, with the dark against the boards and the oilcloth where she had left it:

“You have put him where somebody has to look,” he said. “And you have not asked for anything else.”

She had not. That was the thing she had not done, and she had gone up to a counter four times in five months and had asked for four things, and the fourth one had been a line with no mark on it, and she had not asked him for anything, and she was going to go on not asking him for anything, and it had been going on for four years and was going to go on for a very long time.

The seventh of the month was tomorrow, and it was a pay-day, and there was no column under her hand for it.
