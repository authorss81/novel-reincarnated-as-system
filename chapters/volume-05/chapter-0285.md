# The Course She Cut

On the first of the month she told Da at the trestle and he asked her one question and it was the only question he asked.

“Fever?”

“No fever.”

“Then take the two days and put them in the column and I will not put a mark against them.”

That was the whole of it. A foreman does not care why a body is not on a bank. A foreman cares that the column is honest, because the column is what the wages come out of, and a day entered by a woman who was not on the bank is a day that somebody at the counter has to be able to defend.

She went out behind the hut at the fourth hour with the bar and took the ring apart.

The top course came off in eleven pieces over about an hour and a half, and every one of them had to come off with four fingers on one hand and nine holds in the other, and there was no way to lift a brick flat off a course without getting underneath it, and getting underneath a brick is the one thing a person with no index finger does badly and does badly in a way that is slow rather than dangerous.

It took the morning. The ring stood open in the middle of the day with the crucible in it and the ground beneath her bare and cold, and the sun came and went over the top of it and did nothing whatever.

What she felt through that morning was a kind of anger so clean it was almost rest. She had paid for this ring with days.

She had paid for it once out of twenty-nine coppers she did not have and once out of two holds out of eleven, and she had paid for it since with an hour a line for the rest of her life, and now she was standing in the cold with it in pieces in front of her because she could not afford four coppers for a course of brick.

## The Clay

The clay was under the yard’s own low bank, which was the whole of the reason there was a yard on that ground at all, and it came out of the cut in blue-grey lumps with stone in it that had to be knocked off with the bar before anything else could be done with it.

She took it out by hand because there was nothing on that reach that would dig for nothing, and because a sledge is for stone and this was not stone, and because the one man who might have swung a pick for free was the man at the ramp with the bad shoulder and he was working.

Six hours at the bank face with the bar, and the lumps broken and knocked and carried back to the hut in a fold of cloth, four lumps at a time, four fingers and a thumb, and her shoulders by the fourth trip.

The clay needed working before it would hold anything. She kneaded it on the flat stone the way she had kneaded it at the start, with the heel of the hand and the flat of the second finger, and it came out of her hands in the evening smooth and cold and exactly as it had come out the first time, and there was no part of that which had changed in fifty days.

That was the thing that got her. Every stage she had taken since the nineteenth of a month four months back had gone somewhere she could not follow. The clay was the same clay. The bar was the same bar. The ring was the same brick laid back in the same place in a different order, and the only thing that was different about any of it was a body with less of itself than the last time.

## The Course

On the second day she laid it, and the laying was the whole of the job.

The old bricks went back in on edge instead of flat, which is what a person does with a course that has to hold heat instead of hold weight, and a course on edge takes twice the width of the same bricks laid flat, and twice the width is the difference between a seam that carries and a seam that opens in a night.

It took from the fourth hour to the dark. Nine holds, four fingers, no turn at the end of a stroke, lift with the furnace, set early, move by hand. Each brick went in on its edge, tight, with the broken face turned out, and each one had to be bedded in clay and then be scraped back, and the scraping back is done with the flat of a second finger on a wet surface and takes eleven minutes a stroke at the old measure and longer now.

She laid sixty-one bricks.

At the dark she clayed the join and banked a fire in it and sat back against the boards with her hands shaking in a way that had nothing to do with cold, and the ring ticked for the first time in two days, and the tick was thinner than the one she had woken without.

## The Column

On the third day she was at the plank at the seventh hour with the bar over her shoulder and she wrote the date in, longer than eleven minutes, the hour gone into the writing, the page held flat by a palm that did not have to be asked.

The column went down in order. Two gaps in it where two days stood, present as gaps and nothing else, and the number in her hand exactly what it had been the day before the first of the month.

She put her hand flat on the book for a line’s length. The paper under the four fingers was the same paper it had been since the second of a month, and the column on the near side was hers, and the two gaps were in it now in front of every man at that plank and every man who ever came to that plank, for as long as the book was a thing.

That was the price of a course of brick and it was the only price there was.

What she felt at the plank was not defeat and not the grief she had gone looking for in the morning.

It was something flatter and harder than either, and it was this: she had been able to pay for the ring three times in five months and every one of the three had cost her the column, and the column was the only thing in the world she had that a person four hundred yards away could check, and she had just put two holes in it with her own hand and put them in honestly, and she would have done it again in the morning.

That was the shape of the last four months. She did not like the shape and she had no other one.

The man at the plank turned a page, which he did about once in ten days, and closed the book on his palm and did not look up.

“Season runs to the thirtieth,” he said. “Same as last.”

She had not asked him. She had wanted to ask him since the second of a month four months back and she had wanted it every day since and had never once opened her mouth, and he had known that for as long as she had known it, and a man who keeps a book on a not-level plank for eleven men and one woman learns what people do with their mouths.

She had no thanks for him and she gave none, and she gave him the one question she had wanted, and she gave it in the ordinary voice because that is the voice a question goes in on a working reach.

“What date is in the far hand,” she said, “at the end of my column.”

He looked up then. It was the first time he had looked up since the day she had walked onto this reach, and he did it for about a second, and then he went back to the book.

“Thirtieth,” he said. “Same as last.”

The third closed on a woman with a ring laid on edge and hot, two days out of her column entered in plain ink against her own name, sixty-one bricks laid with four fingers in two days, and twenty-seven days left in a season she had never been able to read the end of until a man who did not look up told her anyway.
