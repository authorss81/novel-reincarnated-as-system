# What The Ground Keeps

On the twenty-sixth the weather turned on the bank, and the ground showed her what it kept.

The wind came off the rise at about the fourth hour with rain behind it, and by the sixth the bank ran water at the bottom and the sledge slipped on the ramp boards, and Da shut the heavy end before a load went wrong on it.

She worked the middle bank in weather that found every opening in her coat and the one in her right arm, open a third of the time, and the furnace under her ran hotter against the cold the way banked fire runs hotter under ash.

At about the second hour a slab she had set early slid on the sledge boards with the rain under it, and came down across the hand with four fingers on it, and nobody saw it happen.

## The Slab

It was the same hand the load had come down across on the twenty-first of a month thirty-five days back, the hand with four fingers’ worth of grip that had taken the heavy end anyway.

The slab pinned the four fingers against the sledge rail for about as long as it took to count eleven, and the pain came up white and complete, and she got her shoulder under the edge and lifted with the furnace through the middle of the stroke and moved the hand clear by hand at the end, because the turn was gone and there was no other way.

Nobody came. That was the shape of the bank in weather, every person holding their own load, rain on every face, and there was no version of the slab where a man saw it.

The yard went on around her for eleven counts. A bar went in, a stone came up, a cart wheel found the wet and made a noise about it, and eleven counts of that is a long time when the four fingers are pinned against a rail and the rain is going into the sleeve that is over them.

She stood in the rain with the hand held against her coat and counted breath in four, and worked the fingers one at a time, and all four moved, and the blood stayed where it belonged, under the skin, and that was the whole of the mercy.

## The Binding

She bound the hand with the spare strip of cloth the keeper had given her two seasons back, winding it round the four fingers and the palm, tying it off with the heel-gone hand and her teeth.

A man at the easy end saw the binding and looked away, the way men look away from bindings, and Da saw it from the ramp and said nothing, and the yard worked on, because a bound hand in weather was weather, and weather was not discussed.

She worked the rest of the day left-dominant, nine holds doing what eleven had done, the bound hand guiding and steadying, setting stone early, moving by hand, and the stones went where she put them, placed and solid.

At about the sixth hour a stone shifted on the sledge as she set it, and the bound hand caught the edge wrong, and pain went up to the elbow, white and complete, and she stood with her forehead against the sledge rail until it passed, counting breath in four.

A man at the easy end saw and looked away, the way men look away, and Da saw from the ramp and pointed at the water barrel without a word, and she drank and went back to the stone, because light stone waited for nobody.

She felt fear go through her then, plain and physical, not for the hand but for the furnace, because the lift had come out of the middle of the stroke where the furnace lived, and the furnace had answered, and she did not know what the answer had cost.

## The Ground

She finished the day on light stone, set early, moved by hand, the four fingers swelling inside the grip, and at dusk she went behind the hut and put her hands flat on the earth where the furnace had gone in on the nineteenth.

The ground was warm.

Not fire-warm. Ground-warm, the way earth holds what is put into it past the line, steady and without witness. Seven days since the stage, and the earth still held it, and no county book had taken any of it, because no county had a column for a thing taken past its own boundary.

She put the heel-gone hand down beside the warm place and it was cold beside it, the same earth the same inch apart, and she lay the two hands flat and left them there until the cold in the left one had gone up to meet the right, which took about the time of four breaths and gave her nothing except the shape of the difference.

Then she took them up and put them in her coat, because a woman who sits with her hands in the earth behind a hut in the dark on a working reach is a woman somebody will have an opinion about, and there was nobody she wanted an opinion from.

## The Lamp

She had no lamp in the hut and never asked for one, because a booked woman with a furnace banked under boards needed no light to count by, and the dark was honest.

She sat in it with the swollen hand in her lap and the warm earth under her other palm, and counted the days to the twenty-seventh on her fingers, and the count came out in ones, the way counts come out past the line.

One hand bound. One furnace banked. One column waiting. One season holding. That was the arithmetic of the twenty-sixth, and it asked nothing of her but the next day.

## The Re-Tying

Before sleep she re-tied the binding with her teeth and the heel-gone hand, winding the strip tighter round the four fingers, testing each one after, and all four moved, stiff and aching and true.

Da had kept her on light stone without a word after the slab, the way foremen keep bound hands on light stone, and the middle bank had taken her the way it had taken her on the twentieth, lift with the furnace, set early, move by hand.

A woman with a scarred forearm had worked the next stretch and nodded once at the binding, the way workers nod at bindings, and said nothing, and the afternoon had gone the way afternoons go on banks, stone after stone, breath in four counts.

She wanted the binding off before the twenty-seventh. That was the want, concrete and small, to stand at the pay-day plank with a hand that held, and she carried it into sleep the way she carried everything, with both hands.

## The Warmth

In the night she woke once with the cold in her the way a stone is cold, and put her palm down through the boards’ gap to the earth where the furnace had gone in, and the earth was warm under her hand.

Seven days since the nineteenth. The ground held it without cooling, steady and without witness, and she lay with her palm on the warmth and counted breath in four, and slept again.

It was the first time the furnace had woken her for anything but ticking, and she did not know whether warmth in the ground at night was ordinary for furnaces or a thing peculiar to hers, and there was nobody within nine days of road to ask, and she asked nobody.

## The Night Count

She lay awake a while with her palm on the warmth through the boards’ gap, and counted what the furnace had given against what it had taken, the way she counted everything, on her fingers, in the dark.

Given: strength through the middle of every stroke, stone rising for half hands the way it rose for whole ones, the heavy end held and regained. Taken: the turn at the end, two holds, twenty-nine coppers, the road west at ninety standing behind all of it like a wall.

The count came out the way counts come out past the line, given and taken both true, neither touching the other.

She got up out of the dark at about the third hour, which was earlier than the yard and earlier than she needed, and went up the four hundred yards to the trestle in the wet, because a swelling hand is a thing that gets worse if you lie on it, and stood there without a lamp with her left hand flat on the open page.

Her own line ran nineteen days long, the eighth of a month to the twenty-sixth, and there was nothing on it she had not put there herself, and it had eleven minutes a line in it and no other meaning whatever, and it would still be there on the seventh with the next date going in under the far date in his hand.

Da came up the track at about the fourth hour with his hood down and the rain running off the end of his nose, looked at the trestle, looked at her standing at it with a bound hand, and said, “Reading it,” not as a question, and went on up the ramp.

She walked back down the four hundred yards and lay on the boards with her palm through the gap on the warm earth, and slept until the fourth hour, and woke with the binding damp and the fingers aching, and worked each one before she stood.

Rain came off the roof edge onto the ground by the door. She worked out what she wanted, concretely, with the swollen hand in her lap.

She wanted the hand to hold to the twenty-seventh. One day of bank work with a pay-day at the end of it, and the column waiting, and the furnace banked that night, and nothing else.

He said one thing that evening and it was present tense, and it bought nothing, and it was only reporting what the ground was doing.

“It is in the ground,” he said. “And it is staying there.”

She banked the furnace with three handfuls of ash and bound the four fingers with the spare strip, and the twenty-sixth closed on a woman with a hurt hand and a warm patch of ground behind a hut, both of them hers, neither of them in any book.

The ground kept what the counties could not take. That was the whole of it, and she had felt it with her own hands.
