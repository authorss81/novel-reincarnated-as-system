# What A Name Is Owed

On the tenth she worked the heavy end again for the first time since the second stage, nine holds and four bound fingers and no turn, lift with the furnace, set early, move by hand, and the heavy end held.

Da had moved her back without a word in the morning, pointing at the ramp and walking away, the way foremen move labour that has found its shape again. She took it with the furnace new under her and the slowed finger in her hands, and turned stone the rest of the day, and the heavy end said nothing, which was the way the yard said everything that mattered.

A man of about forty with a bad shoulder came past her on the way to the water and stopped, and looked at the four on the sledge in front of her, and counted them with his eyes.

“Nine now,” he said.

“Nine now,” Ning Yan said.

That was the whole of what the heavy end said about being got back the second time.

What she felt at midday was pride, plain and physical, the kind that sits in the shoulders after work. Pride, and under it the thing the pride was standing on, which was that a hand with four fingers on it had gone back to the heaviest work on that yard eleven days after a stage had come up under her and taken an hour a line out of her writing.

She wanted, concretely, to finish the season on the heavy end. That was the want, small and hers, and it sat beside the furnace in her like a second weight on a bar.

## The Store

At the store at the seventh hour she bought bread at five coppers, eaten outside with her back to the wind, and the seller with the board said the furnace brick was holding, the way sellers say things that are not questions, and she said it was holding, and that was the entire account the reach ever took of a furnace no county licensed and no county could hear.

The oilcloth against her ribs held thirteen stubs in four marks, the tickets, the folded figures of rates, and the weight of a season carried in a fold of cloth. The paper still said Foundation the first stage and she had still never opened it. The claim stood on a far-side book against hours she worked past the line. Hou unpaid, patient, never met. The Lowwater date untouched. Luo Ping a direction.

She folded the bread paper and put it in the coat, because paper on that reach was never thrown away, and felt what she owed sit beside what she owned, plainly and without machinery.

What she owed was getting to be a strange shape. Nine coppers a day of bread gone from a book that had been whole hands and two holds and a turn. Twenty-nine coppers of brick and wire and clay gone to a ring of firebrick on beaten earth behind a hut. Ten days of the column gone to a road and back, nine days of it a day at a time and two of them not paid at all. An hour a line, going forward, for the rest of her life.

None of it had bought her a thing anybody could see. Every part of it had bought her the reach.

Standing in the store doorway with a day’s bread in her coat she worked out, for the first time since the twenty-ninth, what leaving would now cost, and it came out different from what it had cost a week before.

It would cost the far side. Not the bound, which had held her hours against a figure it could not pay and had said so three times in three rooms.

A book in a low building four hundred yards past the post, with an eight hundred jin entry written in it against the hours she worked past the last working point, would begin to stand up and be questioned the day she stopped standing there to feed it. Somebody in that room would have to say, out loud, to somebody: we took this onto our book against a woman’s column and the woman is not on the column.

That was what her yes had bought. It had not made her safe and it had not made the family safe and it had not put a single coin in her hand. It had made her departure expensive, which is the only kind of safety this country has ever offered anybody who works with their hands.

She went back to the bank with the bread wrapped in her coat and the arithmetic done and the whole of it, she understood, worth exactly what a piece of paper is worth when the person who wrote it is nine days of road away and cannot be asked anything about it.

## The Lines

On the eleventh a pay clerk from the low building came to the trestle with a tally book under his arm, checking columns against tallies the way clerks check things that must come out clean, and the man at the plank stood aside while he ran the lines.

Ning Yan stood at the sledge with the bar in her hands and watched without watching, the way workers watch paper that is theirs without seeming to claim it.

“Column true?” the clerk said.

“True,” the man at the plank said. “Dates in order. Gaps where gaps are. Season at each end.”

He ran four columns while she turned stone, and hers was the last, and he put a thumb down the side of it and said nothing about it, and she understood from the nothing that he had counted her gaps. Ten of them, in a run that went in order and came out in order with the holes in it, and a county clerk with a tally book had put his thumb on a furnace-man’s holes and said nothing about them, because his job that morning was the columns and the columns were true.

The clerk nodded, closed the tally book, and went back up the track. That was the entire check the far side ever gave her column. No county within nine days of road had ever heard of either book, and the figure on the second one was eight hundred jin, and the only hours anybody on this side of the line could put against a figure were the hours in the first one.

She worked the afternoon with the check under the work, lift with the furnace, set early, move by hand.

A name in a book, in ink, where somebody has to look at it. Her brother’s, and her father’s, opened and worked. The register still read reconciled and was not corrected, and it would go on reading reconciled, and no hand of hers would ever be near it.

## The Fire

She sat at the eight feet that evening with the binding still on, inside the warmth while eleven men held it, and felt the day come down on her like a load settling into a sledge.

Da talked about the ramp boards with a man from the mill end, and the talk went the way talk goes at fires, and she sat with the pay-day hours behind her and the heavy end regained under her, and said nothing.

The man with the bad shoulder came and stood at the edge of the fire and looked at her hands where they lay in her lap, four fingers and a thumb, swollen at the ends from the day, and then at the bar leaning against the sledge behind her, and then at nothing in particular, and went and sat down.

Under her, where nobody at a fire could hear it:

“You are owed,” he said. “And you are owing.”

The tenth closed on a woman booked to the end of a season, paid to the day, furnace lit twice, debt to the shed cleared, draft west with no date, fair copy in a far-side book, figure on a far-side book against her hours, slowed finger, heavy end regained, father’s paper west, brother’s decade reachable.

The season had weight now. She carried it the way she carried the bar, with both hands.
