# What A Name Is Owed

On the tenth she worked the heavy end again for the first time since the second stage, nine holds and four bound fingers and no turn, lift with the furnace, set early, move by hand, and the heavy end held.

Da had moved her back without a word in the morning, pointing at the ramp and walking away, the way foremen move labour that has found its shape again. She took it with the furnace new under her and the slowed finger in her hands, and turned stone the rest of the day, and the heavy end said nothing, which was the way the yard said everything that mattered.

A man of about forty with a bad shoulder came past her on the way to the water and stopped, and looked at the four on the sledge in front of her, and counted them with his eyes.

“Nine now,” he said.

“Nine now,” Ning Yan said.

That was the whole of what the heavy end said about being got back the second time.

What she felt at midday was pride, plain and physical, the kind that sits in the shoulders after work. She wanted, concretely, to finish the season on the heavy end. That was the want, small and hers, and it sat beside the furnace want the way stone sits beside stone.

## The Store

At the store at the seventh hour she bought bread at five coppers, eaten outside with her back to the wind, and the seller with the board said the furnace brick was holding, the way sellers say things that are not questions, and she said it was holding, and that was the entire account the reach ever took of a furnace no county licensed and no county could hear.

The oilcloth against her ribs held thirteen stubs in four marks, the tickets, the folded figures of rates, and the weight of a season carried in a fold of cloth. The paper still said Foundation the first stage and she had still never opened it. The claim stood on a far-side book against hours she worked past the line. Hou unpaid, patient, never met. The Lowwater date untouched. Luo Ping a direction.

She folded the bread paper and put it in the coat, because paper on that reach was never thrown away, and felt what she owed sit beside what she owned, plainly and without machinery.

## The Ledger Talk

On the eleventh a pay clerk from the low building came to the trestle with a tally book under his arm, checking columns against tallies the way clerks check things that must come out clean, and the man at the plank stood aside while he ran the lines.

Ning Yan stood at the sledge with the bar in her hands and watched without watching, the way workers watch paper that is theirs without seeming to claim it.

“Column true?” the clerk said.

“True,” the man at the plank said. “Dates in order. Gaps where gaps are. Season at each end.”

The clerk nodded and closed the tally book, and went back up the track, and that was the entire audit the far side ever gave her column. No county within nine days of road had ever heard of either book, and the figure on the second one was eight hundred jin, and the only hours anybody on this side of the line could put against a figure were the hours in the first one.

She worked the afternoon with the audit under the work, lift with the furnace, set early, move by hand. A name in a book, in ink, where somebody has to look at it. That was what a name was owed.

A name in a book, in ink, where somebody has to look at it. That was what a name was owed. Her brother’s. Her father’s opened and worked. The register still read reconciled and was not corrected. That stood for another batch to read once at the close for what it was, if it could carry it, and never to correct.

## The Fire

She sat at the eight feet that evening with the binding still on, inside the warmth while eleven men held it, and felt the day settle the way days settle at fires, stone by stone, into something carried.

Da talked about the ramp boards with a man from the mill end, and the talk went the way talk goes at fires, and she sat with the pay-day hours behind her and the heavy end regained under her, and said nothing.

What she felt at the fire was not lightness. It was the heaviness of a thing carried straight, furnace fed, column clean, season holding, family paper placed where paper had to be placed.

He said one thing that night and it was present tense and it bought nothing.

“You are owed,” he said. “And you are owing.”

The tenth closed on a woman booked to the end of a season, paid to the day, furnace lit twice, debt to the shed cleared, draft west with no date, fair copy in a far-side book, figure on a far-side book against her hours, slowed finger, heavy end regained, father’s paper west, brother’s decade reachable.

The season had weight now. She carried it the way she carried the bar, with both hands.
