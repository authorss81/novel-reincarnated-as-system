# Nine Holds

The sixteenth was an ordinary day and she worked it the way ordinary days were worked, which she had learned to do without resenting it somewhere around the fourth month of being on this reach.

Da put her on the ordinary end in the morning without a word, which was the fourth time in five months he had put her on work she could do with what she had left, and it was never a kindness and it was always correct, and she had never once been able to find the seam between the two.

She set early. She moved stone by hand.

She worked the bar in the left hand the whole day because the right would not go over her shoulder, and she found in about the first hour that a bar can be worked one-handed at the ordinary end quite well if the ordinary end is where the bar’s weight is not, and she worked four to the younger man’s four for most of the afternoon, and at the fourth hour she stopped counting, which she had not done for a long time.

## The List

She went through it at the store step at the seventh hour, standing in the sun with the day’s bread in her coat, because there was a thing on her that she had been carrying since the fourth of a month four months back and it had got heavier rather than lighter as the months went on, and a body with a thing coming in it ought to be asked what it is going to do about it.

Four counts of breath, everywhere, and three is gone and does not come back.

Four fingers and a thumb on the right hand. The index went at the far end of a bad flat at about the sixth hour of a day she could not put a number to, and nothing in the world has offered to give it back and nothing has pretended to.

The order gone out of the left hand with the heel of it, gone in the winter of the year before the first of them, and every grip on that hand built from nothing, one at a time, in the four months since.

Nine holds that hold to the end of a stroke, out of about seventy she had tried since a stage she could count to.

No turn at the end of a bar stroke. It went on the nineteenth of a month three months back and the middle bank came back afterward and has been coming back.

The flat of the second finger longer than eleven minutes a line, permanently, and every hour she writes after that is an hour gone into the writing and staying there.

The right arm open half the time, since a bank at the end of a reach four days ago.

The aperture cracked through and permanent, and every stage she has taken since the first has gone through that crack and left it exactly the width it found it.

And underneath all of it, carried the way a floor is carried under a table, the thing that has no name and no figure and that she has never once been able to get at: there was a stretch of her own life her body had and she did not, and she had gone after it once, hard, in the first year, and had come back out of the going with nothing in her hands, and had not gone after it since.

She stood in the sun with the list and found that it did not get heavier when she said it all out in order, which she had expected, and it did not get lighter either, which she had also expected, and which was the true answer both times.

What she felt was a kind of anger with no object in it, which is what a stage feels like the day before.

## What It Would Take

She had three Golden Core stages in front of her and two of them were taken and one of them was not, and the third was forty-four days out from the second, which was forty-four days after the first, which was fifty days after the ninth of Foundation, and there was no pattern in it anywhere and she had stopped looking for one.

What each of them took was a thing she would not have again, and she knew that the way she knew the number of her breath, and she had gone into two of them with her eyes open.

What she did not know, and had never once been able to find out, was how much the third would take, or in what place, or whether it would be something she could name. Two had been a turn and an hour.

Both of those had been things she could point at, and she had pointed at them, and there had been a satisfaction in pointing that she had not noticed at the time and that now, standing in the sun, she understood had been a kind of pride.

The third might not give her anything she can name. She stood there with the bread in her coat and understood that she was going to do it anyway, at her own order, on a working day, in the open, where eleven men and a foreman and a board could see a woman stand still for a while and then go back to work.

That was the decision, and she made it in the sun at a store step on the sixteenth, the day before the pay-day and fourteen days before the thirtieth, and it did not need a witness and she gave it none.

## The Fire

The eleven men had the fire at the eight feet and she was inside the warmth with her bar across her knees, and the woman with the scarred forearm was one place along the stones from her and had been one place along for about a month now, and neither of them had ever said a word about the distance.

Da talked about the season turning and about what eleven men do with three weeks in which there is nothing on the reach to lift. The man from the mill end said he had a brother-in-law with a winter job two days east and got the same answer every year.

Somebody said something about a cart that had gone down the soft at the valley and the talk went the way talk goes at fires and she sat in it and did not join, and that was the whole of what she wanted from an evening.

At some point the talk stopped for a moment, the way talk stops, and in the gap the younger man on the ramp said across the fire:

“Tomorrow you are on the ordinary again.”

“Tomorrow I am on the ordinary again,” she said.

He nodded and the talk came back. He had held a bar for her for eleven days in the middle of the season and he had seen the four fingers and done the arithmetic and had never once said a number out loud, and now he was telling her what she was doing tomorrow in the tone of a man telling a woman the weather, and he was not being kind and he was not asking anything.

She wanted, at that fire, with the bar across her knees, to say one true sentence to one person in her own life. She had a moment in which she could have said it, and it went past while she was standing there, and there was no sentence, and that was correct, because there was nothing in her that she could say out loud to a man at a fire that would have been anything but a lie dressed as a confession.

She wanted, concretely, to get tomorrow right. To stand at the fourth hour in the open, in daylight, where the yard could see it, and give the order, and take what it took, and be standing at the bar by the sixth hour with four fingers and four counts and whatever else, because the sixth hour was a working hour and the sixth hour did not care and had never once cared.

The sixteenth closed on a woman with a list of nine items and no name for the tenth, sixteen stubs in four marks, fourteen days of a season, a far date in another man’s hand, and an order given to herself in the sun at a store step and not to anybody else.

She banked the ring with three handfuls of ash and sat against the boards with the oilcloth against her ribs and felt the heat come up through the base the way it came up every night, and it was thinner than it had been before the course came out of it, and it would come back over the days, and tonight it was enough.

At the boards that night, with the ash still ticking under her and the oilcloth against her ribs:

“You are going to take it,” he said. “And I am going to be here.”

She slept four hours and was up before the light, and the reach was empty the way a reach is at the fourth hour, and nobody was going to see her at all, and she had known that when she decided, and had decided it anyway, and went out with the bar over her shoulder.
