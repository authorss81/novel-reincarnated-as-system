# The Attendance She Had Lost

On the ninth she came down the last rise and saw the floor nine days west spread below her in the morning light, nine men on it, a wash house to one side with smoke from its chimney, a tower with a light in it that burned even in daylight, and beyond the floor the roofs of Lowwater and the counting room with a date still in it.

She had been walking nine days. The bag strap had worn a groove in the good shoulder. The bread was gone since yesterday. The oilcloth against her ribs held eleven stubs in four marks, the dead weight tickets, and a paper for her brother’s name folded clear of every mark in the space above, written in her own hand with the flat of the second finger, eleven minutes a line, the way she wrote everything.

She stood on the rise and looked at it, and what she felt was grief, plain and unadorned, the kind that has no machinery in it at all. Nine days of road and a floor and nine men and a tower with a light in it. She would not walk that reach again in this book, and she knew it the way she knew her own breath.

What she wanted was narrow and physical and she could have said it out loud without embarrassment. She wanted the hour on that floor to be an hour she had walked nine days for, in her own hand, with her name in the air where a man could hear it, and then she wanted the road east and the rest of her life.

## The Floor

She walked down to the floor in daylight, with her name ready in her mouth the way she had said it at every trestle, and the nine men stopped work the way men stop work when a stranger walks onto a floor, bars down, hands empty.

The foreman came forward, a man she did not know, and looked at her bag and her coat and her hands, four fingers on one, nine holds on the other, and waited.

“Ning Yan,” Ning Yan said. “Of the Qinghe furnace crew. Servant-born. I am here to stand one attendance.”

The referral was still pending. The dismantling was executable past the line as well as inside it, and had been executable for years. The far-side board held a figure no county could answer for, and the ground past the post held her hours.

She stood inside the whole of that in daylight, saying her name out loud, and not one man on the floor put down his bar to ask her a second thing about any of it, because none of it was his to ask about, and because a woman standing on a floor in daylight wanting an hour written is not a question a floor needs to answer.

The foreman nodded once and showed her where to stand, at the end of the floor where the attendance column was kept, and she stood there through the hour with her hands down, in person, in her own hand, the way she had stood at every trestle. The nine men worked round her. The tower light burned. The wash house smoked.

A girl of twenty-four came out of the wash house with a bundle and stopped when she saw her, and looked, and went on. The leg that had been broken on the cold end years back held her weight. The lath and cord were gone. That was the whole of what the wash house said about being carried, and it said it without words.

## The Paper

When the hour ended she took the paper from the oilcloth and put it where somebody had to look at it, on the attendance board, under the date, in ink, in her own hand.

It carried her brother’s name. Ning Bo’s account stood open since before she was born, and this paper opened its working, not its settling, and it asked nothing. It said the hours were hers, the furnace was hers, the column was hers, and the name on it was owed a place where somebody had to look at it.

She said nothing with it. Daylight, her name said out loud, asked for, entered. That was the whole of it.

The foreman read it the way men read paper on floors, without comment, and left it where it was, because a paper put where somebody has to look at it stays where it is until somebody takes it down, and taking it down was a job, and the job was not his, and a man who took down a stranger’s paper on a floor like this one would be the kind of man a floor talks about.

Two of the nine men had read it upside down while they worked near the board, and neither said anything, and both of them worked the rest of the hour a little better than they had worked the hour before, which was the whole of what the paper had been asked to do.

She stood a moment longer with her hand flat on the board for a line’s length, feeling the wood under the four fingers, and then she took the hand away.

## The Counting Room

She walked to Lowwater in the afternoon and stood outside Hou Sanliang’s counting room without going in, the way she had stood nine minutes from its door years back and not gone in.

The door was shut. The step under it had been worn pale in a narrow band, and the rest of the stone was grey, and she had stood in the narrow band once, as a girl, with a refusal ready and a whole life to spend on the keeping of it.

## The Street

The street outside the counting room was narrow with overhanging eaves, and carriers’ boys ran past with tallies, and a woman sold hot water from a cart with a brass tap. Ning Yan stood in the lee of a doorway with the bag over her shoulder and watched the counting room’s door the way she watched everything she would not enter, plainly and without lingering.

A clerk came out with a bundle under his arm and went up the stair opposite, and another went in with his sleeves tied back against the ink. Nobody looked at her. She was a walker with dust on her coat, one of a dozen on the street, and the room held its date whether or not she stood near it.

She had stood in a lane nine minutes from that door once and not gone in, and the refusal at the handover years back had paid Hou nothing and kept her whole, and now the far side held the figure on its own book against hours she worked past the line. Hou unpaid, patient, never met. That stood.

The date in the counting room had been entered against eight hundred jin by a hand that was not the counting room’s own. She had never seen the hand write it. She knew the hand the way she knew the flat of the second finger against a page.

She stood in the lee of a stranger’s doorway and understood, with the whole weight of the walk behind it, that the hand which had put it in would be the hand to take it out, and that it was not hers and would not be done in a season she could walk to.

What she wanted there was to leave that street without knocking on that door and without a single soul in Lowwater seeing her leave it, and she wanted it badly enough to stand in the cold until the wanting had been got out of her body, and then she left it the way she had come, on foot, bag light, hands empty.

She walked back out past the floor, past the wash house, past the tower with the light in it, because there was nothing left in there that was hers to carry either, knowing the dismantling was executable past the line as well as inside it, the referral pending, the far-side board holding what no county could answer for.

She did not knock. There was nothing in that room that was hers to carry.

He said nothing through any of it. There was nothing to buy and nothing to advise, and the standing was hers and the ground’s and nobody else’s.

The ninth closed on a woman who had stood where her hours no longer reached, put a paper for her brother’s name where somebody had to look at it, and walked back out with the oilcloth lighter by one sheet and heavier by the standing.

She had asked for one hour and got it. She had been entered. That was enough.
