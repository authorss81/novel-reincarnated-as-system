# The Second Refusal

On the twenty-fourth the room tried a different voice, and she gave it the same answer in a different shape.

She went to the store at about the seventh hour out of habit, the way she had gone every day since the crossing, and stood at the counter with empty hands, because the bread money had run out the day before and the twenty-seventh was three days off.

She was not hungry. She had not been hungry since the first Foundation, and the habit had been cover and company and not hunger, and standing without buying showed her which half she missed.

A man she had never seen stood at the counter beside her, about sixty, with a clerk’s stoop and a mild face, and spoke to her the way strangers on that reach never spoke to her, directly and without preamble.

“You work the stone bank,” he said. “At the trestle with the book.”

She faced him with her back to the wind, the way she faced everything on that reach.

“I do.”

“The room has a sheet with a figure on it,” he said. “It stands and it can be acted on. The room sent a boy to the trestle and the boy came back with no. The room respects a no.”

He said it mildly, without pressure, the way men of his age say things they have said to many workers. Behind him the storekeeper counted coppers and said nothing.

The store was the far-side counter and five coppers, bread at the seventh hour, the only place on the ground where anything was sold. It had no shelves she would describe and no pot under any shelf, because that store was between the last stake and the cart turn inside the line’s country and nothing of it had crossed the flat.

“What does the room want with me,” she said.

“Nothing but the asking,” he said. “A figure is only paid against something. The only things on this side a figure can be paid against are hours worked past the last working point. There is one run with dates and it is yours. If you ever say yes the room takes them. Until you say it the figure stands.”

“And if I said yes and then came to want to have not said it.”

“Then you have said it,” the man said. “That is the whole of what saying is. I have been sixty for a good while and I have watched a good many people say a thing to make a room stop, and the room does stop, and the thing is said.”

He said it without weight, the way a man says a thing about a road, and he was not warning her and he was not persuading her. He was telling her what the shape of the door was so that she would not walk into it thinking she could walk back out.

She looked at his hands on the counter. They were clerk’s hands gone soft at the joints, stained at the second finger the way her own were stained at the second finger, and she thought that he had been a person on a book once and had ended up in a store and that this was not an accident that had happened to him this month.

The price a third time, in a third room, with a third voice in it. At the counter at speed. At the trestle by daylight. At a store at the seventh hour, mild and direct, from a man who did not know the figure was hers and had never asked.

## The Shape

She felt the anger come up, and under it something colder, which was the knowledge that the room would keep sending voices until she said yes or the season ended, and the season had no date on the page and no length, only a far date in a different hand.

What frightened her about the third voice was not that it was a voice. It was that it was the last one available to the room without a paper, and a room that has run out of voices sends somebody with a reason, and a reason is harder to refuse than a price.

She gave the refusal the shape the room had earned, plain and out loud, with her hands down and nothing in her coat.

“No,” she said. “Not my hours. Not on that sheet. Not this month and not on the seventh or the seventeenth or the twenty-seventh.”

He nodded the way men nod when a thing is settled, and did not argue, and did not press, because he was doing the only thing he was built to do and she had done the only thing she could do.

“The room will be there,” he said. “Four hundred yards up the track.”

Then he bought lamp oil and went out with it, and the storekeeper counted the coppers into the box, and the morning went on the way mornings go on a working reach.

## The Queue

She stood outside with empty hands and watched the store work the way stores work at the seventh hour, carriers and yard men, coppers counted, goods handed, no names taken.

A boy of about nineteen bought nails by count and argued the count the way boys argue counts, and the storekeeper counted again without hurry, and the boy paid and went out satisfied.

It was ordinary and settled and lit, the way the whole reach was ordinary, and she stood in it with three refusals standing behind her and a furnace banked four hundred yards east, and felt the distance between the store’s morning and her own the way she felt the cold, as a thing that did not stop.

She stood there past the point of needing to stand there, which was new, and she worked out what it was: that she had nowhere else to be that hour and nowhere to be it that was worse. That was a freedom and it had the shape of a room four hundred yards up the track.

She wanted the morning to stay ordinary. That was the want, sudden and small, to stand at a counter where figures were nails and oil and bread, counted and paid and done.

Then she put the bar over her shoulder and went to the bank, because ordinary mornings were for people whose columns nobody had priced, and hers had been priced three times.

## What It Moved

It moved nothing on paper and everything in standing.

Three refusals now, in three rooms, with three voices in them. Once in her own head at a counter. Once out loud at a trestle. Once out loud at a store to a mild man with a clerk’s stoop.

A refusal given three times is a wall the way a figure is a wall, and it costs the person standing it the knowledge that every voice after the first is further from the room and closer to her, until the room runs out of voices and sends the one voice she cannot refuse.

She worked the day in the middle of the bank, setting down early, moving stone by hand, the furnace steady under her, nine holds and four fingers doing the work of eleven and five, and she wanted, concretely, to finish the day without the mild man’s face coming back into it.

It came back anyway, at the ramp, at the sledge, at the plank, mild and direct, saying the figure the way men say a figure that is not theirs.

## The Ramp

At about the second hour a cart came wrong on the ramp boards with a wheel at the edge, and Da caught the rail with one hand and the load with his shoulder, and shouted once, short and without words.

She was under the other side before the shout ended, bar across, furnace through the middle, and the two of them held the cart true while the driver led the team forward off the edge.

It took eleven seconds. Nobody was hurt. Da looked at her across the rail for about two seconds and nodded once, and went back to the lading, and the yard worked on.

She stood with the bar in her hands and felt the furnace tick under her the way it ticked at night under the boards, steady and banked, and knew it had carried part of the eleven seconds, and did not know what part, and worked anyway.

At the plank she wrote the date in, eleven minutes, and put her hand flat on the book for a line’s length, and felt the column under her palm, the only hours on the reach a figure could be set against, and the only person who could give them standing at the plank with stone dust on her coat.

The man at the plank finished the line he was on and looked up for about two seconds, the way he looked up when the book told him something the yard had not, and then looked down again, because the book told him nothing but dates and hours, and dates and hours were the whole of what he kept.

She stood a moment longer with her palm on the page, feeling the season run under her hand like water under ice, held and moving at once, and then she took up the bar and went back to the bank.

He said one thing that evening and it was present tense and it bought nothing.

“You have said it three times,” he said. “And it still stands.”

She banked the furnace and sat against the boards, and the twenty-fourth closed on a debt with three refusals standing against it, and a draft nine days west with no date on it, and a fair copy in a far-side book nobody could read but her.

The debt was coming due the way weather comes due, not all at once and not to be refused, only to be stood under.
