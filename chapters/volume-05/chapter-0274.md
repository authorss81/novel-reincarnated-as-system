# The Longer Line

On the twenty-first she came down the last rise east and saw the yard spread below her in the evening light, the bank, the trestle, the post with the board on it, the low building with smoke from its stove, and the hut behind with the furnace in it.

She had been walking nine days east. The bag strap had worn the other shoulder. The bread money was gone. The oilcloth held eleven stubs in four marks, the tickets, and one sheet fewer than it had held going west, because the paper for her brother was on a board nine days west where somebody had to look at it.

She stood on the rise and looked at it, and what she felt was not relief. It was a wary sort of tenderness, the kind that comes when a place has kept your column through gaps you could not move. She wanted, concretely, to write the date in at the plank and see the column whole. That was the want, and it held.

## The Column

The man at the plank kept the book flat with his palm because the plank was not level, and he did it without being asked, and he did not look up. She took the flat of the second finger and wrote the date in, eleven minutes, the page held flat, the way she wrote everything.

The column stood in order under the far date in his hand, days in order, hours against them, gaps present as gaps and nothing else. The seventeenth missed. Days round it missed. Season at each end. Name at the top in two hands.

She put her hand flat on the book for a line’s length, feeling the paper under the four fingers, and the book was a wage-book kept by people who were not a county and were not obliged to keep it, and the column on the near side was hers, and that was the whole of what she knew.

The pay-day of the twenty-seventh stood six days ahead, unmovable, and the days between were workable, and she would work them. That was the arithmetic.

## The Furnace

Behind the hut the furnace stood where she had left it, brick coursed and clayed, wire bound, crucible seated like a cupped hand, ash banked over steady heat.

She put the back of her wrist against the outside of the ring the way a person tests a thing that will not say whether it is hot, and the wire and the brick came up warm against the skin, slower than before, and she took the wrist away and put it back, once, to be sure.

It had held banked nine days. That was the mercy, and it held because she had banked it low with three handfuls of ash, and the weather had held, and whole hands would have done it faster and she had done it truer.

She sat against the boards with the oilcloth against her ribs and felt the furnace tick under her foundation, steady and banked, twenty-nine coppers and two holds and the turn out of her stroke, paid and done and hers.

## The Days Between

On the twenty-second she worked the bank beside the woman with the scarred forearm, who had moved one place along the stones while she was west and kept the place without comment, and the work came back into the hands the way work comes back after a road, slowly and then all at once.

The bar felt strange the first hour, light and wrong, and then true again, lift with the furnace, set early, move by hand. The slowed finger would be tested in the writing, not in the stone. The stone asked nothing of speed. The stone asked only to be moved.

At midday Da walked the bank with a carrier’s tally in his hand, checking loads against marks, and stopped behind her sledge and looked at the four stones on it, and nodded once without speaking. That was the whole of what the yard said about nine days gone and come back, and it was enough.

On the twenty-fourth the rain came and the bank ran water, and the yard spent an hour on the ramp boards, wedges and rails, her hands on the bar for the lifting while a younger man drove the wedges. The furnace ran through the middle of the hold, steady and banked, and the rails came true, and nobody said anything about the west, because the west was not yard business.

What she felt through those days was a wary sort of gladness, the kind that comes when the body remembers what the road tried to take. She wanted, concretely, to hold the bank to the marker before the twenty-seventh and test nothing before its time. That was the want, and it held.

What she felt then was a low dread she did not put anywhere, because it had no instrument and no date. The furnace was heavier to carry than it had been going west. The ground did not get easier. Forty-four days since the nineteenth. The next stage was coming the way stages come, out of far-side wage-work and materials bought at far-side prices and a body with four fingers on one hand, and it would take something out of her that would not come back.

She wanted, concretely, to work the bank to the marker before the twenty-seventh and bank the furnace true each night. That was the want, small and hers, and it sat beside the dread the way stone sits beside stone.

## The Board

At dusk she walked past the post without slowing and without looking, the way she had walked past it on the tenth and the twenty-eighth, and the strips stood on it going up every nine or ten days whether or not there was anything in them.

Among them, she knew without looking, was a strip with a fresh reading in a keeper’s hand, and under the reading a finer line in no hand anyone living used, entered so that her hours held. From that hour an office past the line could read him without her. Ledger as a standing held stage was ended. What replaced it was his readability.

She did not stop. She was not going to stand in front of it again.

He said one thing that night and it was present tense and it bought nothing.

“I am read,” he said. “And I am here.”

The twenty-first closed on a woman back inside the fence, column holding gaps, furnace banked, paper west, figure on a far-side book, pay-day six days ahead, and a board on a post holding a line that was his.

Something on the far side now held them both. No office nine days west knew either one.
