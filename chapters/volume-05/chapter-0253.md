# What The Round Carries

She heard the road before any person told her about it, on the fourteenth of the month at about the sixth hour, standing at the heavy end of the bank with a bar under a slab.

A cart went up the track past the yard with an empty bed and a team pulling steady, and the sound it made on the wet ground was the sound of a road that had been shut for a season and was not shut now.

She had been on that bank thirty-six days of work and ten days of booking, and she knew the difference between a mill cart and a road cart the way she knew the difference between stone that would break and stone that would hold.

This one was a road cart. It had a cover rolled at the back and a rail for bags and a driver sitting forward the way drivers sit when they have nine days in front of them.

She wanted a date. That was the whole of what she wanted before the fourth hour was out, and it sat in her the way hunger used to sit before the first Foundation, low and plain and not to be argued with.

## The Yard

The yard worked the way it had worked since the second of the month, eleven men and a foreman at the ramp called Da, stone out of a low bank with about two feet of soil over it, bars and a timber sledge and carts at the ramp.

She was on the heavy end because she had put herself there in the third week and nobody had moved her off it, and the bar sat in eleven holds in the left hand with the heel gone and four fingers and a thumb on the right.

The man at the plank had the book open on the trestle about sixty yards off, and the column on the near side of it had her name at the top in her own flat-of-a-second-finger hand and dates going down it in order and a far date at the far end in his hand and not hers.

Nine stubs in the oilcloth against her ribs in four marks. A plate under all of them she did not describe to herself at all.

She worked the slab up and walked it to the sledge and came back for the next, and the road cart went on up the track toward the post, and she counted her breath in four and did not look after it.

## The Day’s Work

The bank gave her thirty-odd stones before midday, soil first with the mattock end and then stone with the bar, lift with eleven holds, set early, move by hand where the turn would not serve.

The furnace was not yet lit and the body was what it had been since the crossing, nine holds gone to eleven and back to nine, four fingers on the right, the arm open a third of the time, breath in four counts everywhere.

A man of about forty with a bad shoulder worked the next stretch with a steady rhythm that never hurried and never stopped, and she measured herself against him the way she measured everything, by the stone and not by the strength.

He turned one for her two in the first hour. She turned three for his two by the sixth. Neither of them said one word about either figure, because the yard paid the same five coppers to all of them and counting was for the book, not for men.

At midday she sat on the sledge with bread from the store, five coppers at the seventh hour, eaten outside with her back to the wind, and watched two more road carts go up the track with covers rolled and rails for bags.

The drivers sat forward. That was the road seat, and she knew it from the cart turn a month and a half back, and from four years of watching carts go where she could not follow.

She felt envy go through her like weather, plain and unadorned, and put it down beside the bread. A booked woman does not envy a road seat. She writes the date in and works the bank.

## Wei Ran

The team came back down at about the second hour with Wei Ran walking at the near end of the cart with her hand on the upright, and this time the cart was not hers and the team was not hers and she was walking beside a driver of about forty with a road face on him.

Ning Yan had the bar across her knees, sitting on the step of the store with bread in her hand that had cost five coppers at the seventh hour, and Wei Ran slowed for about the length of a cart going past a yard and did not stop.

“You are still on that bank,” Wei Ran said.

“Yes.”

“The road is open,” Wei Ran said, walking. “It opened at first light. Carts through to the line and past it.”

That was the rate-and-road register and nothing else, a working woman with a trade and an opinion saying a thing once at the same time as something else.

“What does the going cost now,” Ning Yan said.

“Ninety for a person and one bag off the far end of the reach,” Wei Ran said. “Nine days hard going. Bad road. Not a favour.”

The driver did not look at either of them. The cart went on three paces.

“And the coming,” Ning Yan said.

Wei Ran did not break stride. “Different,” she said. “Nobody has quoted it and I am not quoting it.”

Then the cart was past and Wei Ran’s hand came off the upright for about two seconds and went back on, the way a carter marks a yard she has worked before, and that was the whole of what passed between them.

## What Open Means

She finished the bread outside with her back to the wind and went back to the bank, and she worked out what open meant the way she worked out everything on that reach, with a bar in her hands and stone under it.

Open meant carts stopping at the shut end of the season and starting again. It meant the track down to the post carrying wheels that had somewhere to be. It meant nine days of hard road west to a county that could no longer hear her, and nine days back for whoever came the other way.

She was eighteen years old and booked to the end of a season with a date at each end of her column, and she could not be un-entered without a fact going round that reach about a woman who took a term and walked off it.

She felt the hook of that the way she had felt it on the sixth of the month, two days late, on the heavy end. A hold keeps where it is put. A hook does not come off.

What she felt about the open road was not want. It was anger, low and clean, that the road had opened without a date any person could give her for anything on it.

## The Carrier’s Round

At about the sixth hour she took a stone to the ramp and Da checked the load the way he checked everything, with one hand on the rail and his eyes on the lading and not on her, and he said it without looking up.

“Carrier’s round west at first light tomorrow. Papers and bags and persons. No date on it.”

She set the stone down and straightened with the bar in her hand.

“No date,” she said.

“No date on the round,” Da said. “Goes when it is full and comes when it comes. Nine days hard going. That is the round.”

He was a man on a job saying a thing about a job, and there was nothing behind it but the ramp and the carts and the stone.

She wanted to ask when it would be full. She wanted to ask what a paper cost on it and whether a paper with a figure on it could go on it and who read papers at the other end.

She asked none of it. A woman booked to the end of a season does not ask a foreman about a road she cannot be un-entered from, not on the first day it opens.

## The Book

At the end of the day she went to the plank and wrote the date in herself, eleven minutes with the flat of the second finger on the left hand, the man at the plank holding the page flat with his palm because the plank was not level, without being asked and without saying her name.

The column went down in order. The far date stood at the far end in his hand, written by him on the fourth, eleven inches from her own end and forty minutes apart in the day.

She stood with her hand flat on the book for about as long as it took to read a line, and she worked out the one new thing the day had put into it.

Every hour she worked past the line went into a column no county within nine days of road had ever heard of. Every paper that went west went on a round with no date on it. The two kept different time, and neither of them waited for her.

## The Hut

She went to the hut with three boards in it four hundred yards east and laid the bar by the door and put the bread money for tomorrow where she always put it, in the fold of the oilcloth beside the nine stubs in four marks.

She laid the stubs out on her knee the way she had laid them on the seventh, in the order they were torn off, and checked them against each other by touch in the dark.

No overlap. No contradiction. No gap. Three off the far-side plank on the seventeenth and the twenty-seventh and the seventh, one man and one mark. Six from inside the bound over two months, four men who did not know each other and had never met her.

For the first stretch of time in the book she could prove she was somewhere, and the proof was paper nobody could check, and that was the whole of what proof meant past the line.

She put the stubs back above the plate she did not describe to herself at all, and sat against the boards with the cold in her the way a stone is cold, and listened to carts going up the track in the dark toward the open road.

He said one thing that night and it was present tense and it bought nothing.

“You are booked through,” he said. “And the road is open under it.”

She did not answer him. She sat with the bread money for tomorrow in the fold of the oilcloth and the nine stubs above the plate, and worked out the one new thing the day had put into the book.

The road west was open and it cost ninety coppers for a person and one bag and the coming was a different number nobody had quoted. The carrier went west at first light with no date on the round.

Something had to be written and it had not been written.
