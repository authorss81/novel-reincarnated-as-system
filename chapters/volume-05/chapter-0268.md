# The Far Side Takes The Figure

On the twenty-ninth she worked the middle of the bank with the furnace steady under her and the bar true in hands that had learned the new shape of the stroke.

The morning came up pale over the rise and the stone came out of the low bank in the ordinary way, lift with the legs, set early, move by hand, and the turn at the end of the stroke stayed gone the way it had stayed gone since the nineteenth. She had stopped testing it at dusk. Testing was a kind of asking and the ground had answered.

Da moved two men to the ramp before the second hour and left her where she was. The heavy end was hers again since the twenty-seventh and no man said anything about it, which was the way the yard said everything that mattered. A man of about forty with a bad shoulder worked the next stretch and counted her loads with his eyes and did not count them out loud.

She ate bread outside at midday with her back to the wind. Five coppers in and five out. The number the same both times.

What she felt at midday was a flat sort of steadiness, the kind that comes when debt is done and the next pay is eleven days off and the body holds. She wanted, concretely, to finish the bank to the marker before the light went. That was the want, small and hers, and it held through the afternoon.

## The Summons

At about the fifth hour a boy from the mill end came down the bank with a message said the way boys say messages on a working reach, without weight and without waiting for an answer.

“The office wants the column woman,” he said. “At the low building. Before dark.”

Ning Yan set the bar down against the sledge and wiped her hands on her coat. The low building was four hundred yards past the post, the room with the counter and the stove and six men in it, the room where the seventh condition had been read out loud once on the twenty-ninth of a month and confirmed actionable on the tenth. She had not been inside it since the twenty-fourth, when she had refused the third asking in the ordinary voice and walked back to the yard.

She went up the track with stone dust on her sleeves and the oilcloth against her ribs, eleven stubs in four marks above the plate she never described, and the furnace banked under her foundation, steady and without witness.

The room was the same room. The counter stood where it stood. The stove gave off the same heat. Six men sat where six men sat, coats steaming, hands round cups, and the man who had read the condition stood behind the counter with a sheet on a spike beside him and a book open flat in front of him.

He did not ask her name. He had never asked it.

## The Book

He laid his hand flat on the open book the way the foreman at the trestle laid his hand on ground he was about to describe, and looked at her across the counter with the patience of a man who has a full book and a short day.

“Your column is here,” he said. “Dates in order. Hours against them. Season at each end.”

Ning Yan said nothing. The book was kept by people who were not a county and were not obliged to keep it, and the column on the near side was hers, and that was the whole of what she knew.

“The figure stands,” he said. “Eight hundred jin. Entered in good order. Travelling on your paper. Actionable on this side.”

She had heard those sentences before, in that voice, in front of those men, and they had cost her nothing to hear until now because hearing was not holding.

“What the room can do now is this,” he said. “The room can take the figure onto its own book. Against hours worked past the last working point. Against your column.”

The stove ticked. One of the six men shifted on his bench and the bench creaked. Nobody looked at her the way men look at a woman. They looked at her the way men look at a column that has just been named.

She understood the rule perfectly. It was plain and complete and it had no machinery in it at all. A figure was only paid against something, and the only things on this side of the line a figure could be paid against were hours worked past the last working point, and there was one run of those with dates in it on this reach and it was hers.

She also understood that it would move the weight off every back she had left nine days west and put it onto ground she stood on.

That was the whole of it, and it sat in her the way stone sits in a sledge, plain and unmovable.

“What does it cost the room?” Ning Yan said.

“Nothing until it is answered,” he said. “A figure on our book stands the way a figure on any book stands. It costs the room the keeping of it. It costs you the hours if you ever stop working them.”

“Until I say it,” she said.

“Until you say it,” he said. “Nobody in this room takes a person’s hours off a piece of paper somebody else read out loud in a room. If you ever say yes we take them. Until you say it the claim stands and it costs us nothing to stand.”

## The Yes

She stood at the counter with stone dust on her sleeves and felt the season in the oilcloth against her ribs, paper and stubs and marks, all of it hers and none of it checkable, and the furnace steady under her, twenty-nine coppers and two holds and the turn out of her stroke, paid and done.

What she felt then was not steadiness. It was anger, clean and sudden, at every book that had ever held her family by a corner and never once been made to carry anything itself. The bound’s books could not close without her and never would, and the people she had left standing carried it, and the room in front of her was offering to carry it instead, on paper, in ink, where somebody had to look at it.

She wanted, concretely, for the nine men on a floor nine days west to finish a season without her name in the margin of it. That was the want, and it was not small, and it stood beside the anger the way stone stands beside stone.

“Yes,” Ning Yan said. “Take it onto your book. Against my hours.”

He wrote it down. The pen moved in the ordinary way, without ceremony, and the figure went onto the far-side office’s own book against hours worked past the line, and the bound’s bill closed by opening one the bound could not read.

From that hour leaving would cost the far side instead of the bound. That was what finishing meant, and she had said it in daylight, with her name at the top of the column in two hands, asking nothing.

He read the entry out once, the way offices read entries out, without looking up, and the six men heard it the way men hear paper, without comment.

She walked back to the hut in the dark with the bar over her shoulder and banked the furnace with three handfuls of ash, and sat against the boards with the oilcloth against her ribs and felt what she had done settle the way days settle at fires, stone by stone, into something carried.

He said one thing that night and it was present tense and it bought nothing.

“You are held,” he said. “And you are holding.”

The twenty-ninth closed on a woman whose hours now carried a figure no county could answer for, booked to the end of a season, furnace lit, eleven stubs, three refusals spent into a yes, draft west with no date, and a season with weight in it that had just got heavier.

There was no instrument she could think of and no office within nine days of road that could undo what the pen had done.
