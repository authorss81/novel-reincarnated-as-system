# A Hand She Knows

On the twenty-third the keeper put a strip up on the post, and among the papers on his cart was a bundle she had not seen before, and a hand she had.

She was coming down the track from the yard at about the sixth hour with stone dust on her coat when the keeper’s cart stood at the post, and the keeper of about fifty-five was up the post with strips and paste, doing the work he had done for nine years.

The cart held the usual things, strips and paste and a book with a board cover, and beside them a bundle of fair sheets tied with cord, the kind of bundle that goes down a stair and up four flights in other hands.

She stopped the way workers stop when a cart blocks a track, to the side and out of the way, with her hands down.

The keeper worked the way he had worked for nine years, strips and paste, measured and unhurried, a man doing the only thing he was built to do. He asked her nothing. He had asked her one thing once, on the twenty-sixth of a month, and that was the whole of what she got, and the rule held.

She watched his hands the way she watched all hands on the reach, cut and stained and steady, and felt something close to kinship, which she put down beside the stone dust, because kinship with a keeper was not hers to take.

## The Taking

The keeper came down the post and took the fair bundle in his hands and untied the cord, and sorted through it the way a man sorts what he cannot read, by size and by shape, keeping the strips separate from the sheets.

The sheets were fair work. Even lines. A column of figures in order, one through nine with one hole in it, the hole the width of an absence, the order the order she had stood in over four years.

Her breath caught at four counts and stayed there. She knew that hand the way she knew the flat of her own second finger, from tower volumes read by lamplight years ago, from eleven volumes given without knowing they were a method, from four entries written upward in a column nobody read.

She did not say the name. There was no name on the track to say, and no man within nine days of road knew it, and she had not thanked him and was not going to start.

The keeper took one fair sheet and pasted it into the book with the board cover, into a book the far side keeps, past the line, in a hand nobody within nine days of road could read but her.

He did it without knowing what it was. That was plain in every movement, the paste, the placing, the pressing flat with his palm. A keeper keeps what comes on the cart. That is what the board is for. That is what the book is for.

He worked the paste down the whole length of the sheet with the edge of his thumb and left his own handprint in the middle of the ninth line without looking at it, and that handprint was the only part of that page made by a person standing on the far side of the line, and he had made it by accident, on a day he had not counted and did not keep.

## What She Did

She did nothing.

She stood to the side of the track with stone dust on her coat and watched a fair hand’s copy of her Foundation column go past the line into a book the far side keeps, up a stair that belonged now to a district clerk, carried in arms that were not the writer’s, taken by hands that did not know it.

She wanted to touch the sheet. That was the want, sudden and physical, to put her finger on the fair lines and hold them still. She kept her hands down.

She stood there until the keeper came down the ladder and got past her on the track, and when he did she turned and took up her own bar from the post where she had leaned it, which was not a thing she remembered doing.

The keeper tied the rest of the bundle and put it back on the cart, and went up the post again with a fresh strip, and the track was clear, and she walked on down to the yard with the bar on her shoulder and the line still going in her head.

## The Morning On The Bank

At the bank she worked the morning with the furnace under her and the turn gone out of her stroke, setting down early, moving stone by hand, and she worked out what had been given a home.

The beat promised from the twenty-fourth of a month seasons back, the fair copy going up a stair and past the line, had landed on the twenty-third without a sound, through a clerk’s arms and a keeper’s paste, and nobody on the reach knew what had gone into the book.

She turned twenty-seven stones before midday and set two of them down twice, the first on the ramp boards and the second by the water barrel, and neither time did she lose her place in the run.

The odd thing, and she turned it over on the second one, was that she was not angry. She had come down that track ready to be stopped by something, and what had happened was a man she had never spoken to had taken her column off a cart with a thumb and put it somewhere past the reach entirely, and it had cost him nothing and had not been refused.

## The Column Beside The Column

She worked it beside the trestle book all morning, the way she worked everything beside the book, two columns in her head that no county held together.

One column at the plank, in her own flat-of-a-second-finger hand since the second of a month, dates in order, hours against them, her name at the top, continuous.

One column in a fair hand in a far-side book, one through nine with one hole, in the order she had stood in, pasted in by hands that did not know it.

Both past the line. Both out of any county’s hearing. One she wrote herself at eleven minutes a line. One a writer wrote for her, up a stair that belonged to a district clerk now, carried in arms not his, taken small and off the page.

She wanted to stand between the two and hold them both still. That was the want, sudden and plain and physical, and she kept her hands on the bar.

## The Plank

At the end of the day she went to the plank and wrote the date in, eleven minutes with the flat of the second finger, the page held flat without being asked, and the man at the plank went down the column in the ordinary way.

She stood with her hand flat on the book for a line’s length and thought of the other book, the one with the board cover on the keeper’s cart, with a fair sheet pasted into it that morning by hands that did not know it.

Two books on one reach held her now, one in her own hand at eleven minutes a line, one in a fair hand she had known for years, one through nine with one hole.

She wanted to ask the keeper what book it was. That was the want, sudden and sharp, to stand at the post and put the question the way she had put one question on the twenty-sixth of a month.

She did not. He had asked her one thing and that was what she had got, and she walked down to the yard with the question still whole in her mouth and never put it down.

## The Evening

At the hut she banked the furnace with three handfuls of ash over the mouth, the way the seller had told her, and sat against the boards with the day behind her, and held the two columns beside each other once more before sleep, hers at eleven minutes a line and the fair hand’s pasted into a book she would never open.

Ten stubs in the oilcloth. The chalk strip gone with the paying. The dead weight tickets with the ration marks. The plate under all of them, undescribed, unremoved.

She felt the strangeness of being a person whose stages stood in order in a fair hand in a book she would never be allowed to open, kept by people who were not a county.

A thing kept by people who were not a county could be lost by people who were not a county. She worked that out in the dark with her palm flat on the boards, and it did not make the sheet worth less. It made it worth exactly what it had cost, which was nothing, and she sat with that for a long while and let it be true.

He said one thing that night and it was present tense and it bought nothing.

“It is kept,” he said. “And it is not yours to keep.”

She had wanted, since the crossing, to know whether anything she had been inside the bound followed her. Now a column in a fair hand had followed her past the line, one through nine with one hole, and it sat in a far-side book in a keeper’s paste.

She did not thank anybody. The word was available in no form, and the work stood, and the arrangement held.
