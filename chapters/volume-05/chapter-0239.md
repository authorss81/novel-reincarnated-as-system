# The Stone Yard

The eighth of a month she was at the bank at about the fourth hour with the bar in her left hand, and it was the first working day she had done on that side of the post.

The bank is low. There is about two feet of soil on it and about nine feet of stone under that, and it comes out with a bar at the face and goes up a ramp onto carts, and eleven men work it and she works it, and not one of them has put her anywhere on it.

The stone is a soft grey and it comes out in slabs that want a bar rather than a lever, and it goes on the carts in pieces you can lift with one hand or pieces you cannot lift with two. That is the whole of what a stone yard is.

## What The Book Is

The book is open on a plank at the trestle and there are two things in it that matter and the rest of it is paper.

There is a price column. It takes the rate that is said out loud, which is what it is for, and the man at the plank wrote the five in it on the seventh of the month before and did not look up.

And there is a column nearest the near side of the plank, and that one is hers.

Her name is at the top of it in a hand that is hers and has been there since the second of a month. Under it there are dates, in order, and hours against the dates, and the run is continuous, and that is the only instrument the world has ever handed her, and she has it again three-quarters of a mile past the post.

She wrote today's date in herself, standing at the plank at about the sixth hour with the wind coming off the rise behind her.

It took her about eleven minutes. The writing hand is the left one and it has the heel of it gone and it goes with the flat of the second finger, and that is how every county hour of her life has been written since she was fifteen.

It took her longer than eleven minutes, because the plank is not level. The man at the plank put his palm flat on the page above her hand and held it there for the whole of it without being asked and without saying anything, and she held the near corner down with four fingers and a thumb and the corner went twice, and the second time it went she did not ask him to hold it again.

He did not look up while she wrote and he did not look up after, and he did not say her name, and the name is already on the page and does not need saying again.

## The Counter On This Side

There is a store on this side of the flat. It is a room with a counter in it and a board over the counter with nothing written on the board, and bread at five coppers, and it is the only place on this ground where anything is sold.

It is not the store she has been buying bread in for two months. That one is between the last stake and the cart turn, on the line's own side, and it has shelves, and a woman behind the counter who has been there every day of her life and who read her name off a board about nine times and never once used it.

This one is on the far side of that flat and she has never stood in it before the seventh hour this morning.

Same bread. Same price. Same hour.

There is a difference between them and the difference is only that she had to cross the flat to reach this one, and she did that on the second of a month with her hands cut and nobody within a day's walk, and everything on the other side of it is the same as everything on this side of it.

She put five coppers on the counter and went out and ate it on the step with her back to the wind.

She did not do the sum standing there. The sum is the same sum it was at the other counter and the price is the same price and she has done it in front of herself enough times in two months that doing it again would be a thing she was doing instead of eating.

## The Strip On The Post

The keeper came up the rise with his cart at about the sixth hour, the way he does, and he took a strip off the back of the cart against a rule and squared the end of it with his thumb and wrote a date at the left and a figure at the right and pushed it up behind the plank and went down the rise.

That is every nine or ten days. She has seen it three times now and she is not going to ask about it, because she asked about it once and the answer was nine years long and she is not going to get a shorter one by asking a second time.

Nine days of road behind her, and a floor, and nine men, and a tower with a light in it. She has not been to the post and she is not going today, and the board is four hundred yards away and she could not read a word of it if she stood in front of it.

## The Whole Of The Day

She worked the whole of the day. She took the heavy end of the bank, because the heavy end is there and because not one man at that yard is going to walk over and give it to her, and she is not going to stand at the face of a bank working the easy end for two days so that somebody will offer her the other one.

And she did more of the day than any of them, and it was visible from the ramp, and it is not visible anywhere else, and no man at that yard looked up from what he was doing to see it happen.

That is the whole of the eighth. She worked a day on the far side of a line of work at five coppers and she bought a loaf and she stood at a plank for eleven minutes with her hand in a book and nothing happened to her.

## The Yard At The End Of A Day

There are eleven men on it and it is eleven and not ten because one of them is the man at the ramp and the man at the ramp does not go at the face on an ordinary day.

They got there before her and they were on the face when she came at the fourth hour, and they went down on the ramp and up it all day with the sledge, and the sledge is a timber runner about nine feet long that two of them take between them because it weighs more than a person.

They have a rhythm. She is not in it and she was never going to be in it, and after about two days she stopped being slowed down by it and started being slowed down by something else, which is that she can do about four times what any of them can do at any moment and has to choose to do exactly what they are doing, because the bank is worked by the yard and not by the strongest person on it.

She chose it every day she has been here. That took her about a day to work out and she did not find it remarkable and then found it remarkable and then stopped finding it remarkable again.

At about the seventh hour they put the tools in the end house and one of them put a pot on and there was bread in the yard, and she ate in it, and not one man at that yard looked up to work out whether she had paid for it or not, because it was not a thing that went in a column.

At about the eighth hour they went up the track to the mill in ones and twos and the yard was empty and the fire under the trestle was let down, and that is how a day ends on this reach and there is no bell on it and no one calls it.

## The Feeling Of It

She expected to be conspicuous.

There is not one thing on this reach that can see what she is. The strongest man at that yard has a bad shoulder and a bar and he is not the strongest man here, and the strongest body within nine days of road is paid five coppers a day and is measured entirely by what she turns over with a bar in a day, and nothing else about her exists in the ordinary way that people see each other.

Not one man at that trestle said her name. Not one of them asked her where she came from, or how she learned the stone, or what she was doing at the end of the bank, and eleven men worked a yard about nine feet from her all day and not one of them put her in the middle of a thing.

She had spent four years being looked at, written down, priced, licensed, referred, closed against and read out loud in rooms.

On the far side of a line of work she was a woman on a bank at five coppers, and being that was a relief for about two hours.

Then it stopped being a relief and became the thing itself, and it has no name in it, and she sat on the step at the seventh hour with her back to the wind and a crust in her hand and worked out that the most ordinary person on nine days of road is the strongest one, and that nobody here has any way of knowing that, and that she is going to be on this bank tomorrow at the fourth hour anyway.

There is a book four hundred yards from here with her name at the top of a column in it, and it is the only paper in the world that says where she was, and it was opened once at the seventh of a month by a man who wrote a five in it and did not look up.

That is what she found out on the eighth. The far side has its own paper and it keeps it in the ordinary way, and it has her in it, and she has to go out at the fourth hour to keep it there.
