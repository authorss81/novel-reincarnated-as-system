# The Cuts Go On


She went at the register the wrong way.
It was the fourteenth of the month beginning two thousand nine hundred and fifty-four and she had been reading it in the same direction for nine days, always from the near end of the table toward the far side, because the five mercies are five mercies in an order and you do not read them backwards.

She had put her left thumb on the first cut and gone along to the fifth, twice, once by lamp and once by her own fingers in the dark, and both readings had found five clauses and no conditions in any of them.

What she had never done was stand at the fifth cut and keep going.

## What Was Past the Fifth

She took the lamp off the stone and stood it on the table at the edge of the register, which put two paces of light on the stone and almost none on the cuts, and she put her left hand flat over the fifth cut with her three fingers and a thumb and moved it a breadth past, the way she had moved it a breadth at a time for nine days, and she found more.

There was more.

Her hand went on along the register past the place where the fifth mercy ended and kept finding cuts, even and deep and unhurried, made by the same kind of hand, and she stood in the middle of a dark room at the top of a tower at about the fourth hour with a lamp nearly out on a table with no wear in it and read the shape of what was there without being able to read a word of it.

She had been reading mercies. That was the whole of why she had stopped at the fifth. Five mercies is an offer and she had come up nine hundred and four steps to find the price of one, and a woman who has found the price of an offer does not stand at a table at the fourth hour running her fingers past it because she is curious.

She ran her fingers past it anyway.

It went a long way. Longer than the five. She could not see how far, because the lamp gave her two paces and the register was not two paces deep, but she could feel where the cuts stopped and started again, and there were breaks in them, and she had read breaks before.

## The Sixth Cut

There were numbers in the breaks.

She stood up straight at the near end of the table because readings were standing, with nothing in either hand, three fingers and a thumb on each, her right arm hanging open at the elbow for six hundred and seventy-three days, and she put both hands flat on the register where the sixth thing began.

The lamp gave her an eyelash of light and she bent into it and read.

“A thing is said in the voice of the person it is about, standing, before it is a line anywhere.”

She read it standing, in the ordinary voice, first in her own voice before it was a line anywhere, and the place behind her fourth rib pulled, and she read it again because she had been reading things twice since the twenty-fourth and a reading read once is only a finding.

“A thing is said in the voice of the person it is about, standing, before it is a line anywhere.”

Same words. Same cuts.

## The Rule She Had Been Living By

She put her hand flat on it and did not lift it.

She stood at the table in the dark and let the six days she had been here settle around that sentence and she found every one of them standing inside it.
The binding at the peak, said out loud standing in her own voice before it was a line anywhere, by her and by no other mouth. The count at the ninth hour, said out loud standing where the keeper reads, first in her own voice, ungettable back. The six terms on the stairs, said standing.

Every finding she had made in nineteen years that mattered had been said out loud by her before anything wrote it, and she had done it so long and so automatically that she had stopped thinking of it as a rule and had started thinking of it as the way she was built.

It was not the way she was built. It was written down. It was cut into the stone of the table at the top of a tower by something that had never once lied to her, in a register that already carried five mercies and a second reading that found no conditions in any of them, and it was not one of the five and it was not a condition and it asked nothing of anybody.

It was the rule the whole thing ran on.


“Nineteen years,” she said out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice, into the dark past the last landing. “I have been saying everything in my own voice before it is a line anywhere, and I thought that was mine. It is written on this table and it is older than the bargain and there is no name at the head of it and no name at the foot of it, which is the whole of the Scribe’s manner, and it has never given one word back out of itself.”

## What It Does Not Let Her Do


She stood at the register with her hands on it and worked out what a rule like that one costs, which was nothing at all, and that was the difficulty.

It asks nothing. It is not a condition and it does not narrow the offer and it does not take anything. It says only that a thing goes into the world in the mouth of the person it belongs to, first, and not afterward, and everything after that is written.


She put her thumb on the sixth cut and thought about the reading she had done on the sixth day, when she read five clauses with her fingers in the dark and found no turn and no hook and no narrowed cut where a keeping becomes a price. She had been reading under this rule the whole time.

Her own voice had gone in front of five mercies before anything wrote them, and the layer had written after, and the writing had not moved, and she had taken that as the proof that nothing was behind them.

## What He Had Not Known

Nothing was behind them. But there was this under them, and she had run her hand the wrong way for nine days to find it.

“You have been able to hear the five mercies through the wire since the first day I stood on this table,” she said. “You read them with me. You corrected me on the second clause. Have you got the sixth.”

He was quiet for a count of forty, which was his way of listening past her, into the traffic, the way she listened past her light into the dark.

“No,” he said.

“You have had nine days of me reading this register out loud and you did not know there was one more.”

“No,” he said. “What I have had is you, and the five, and the going past the five where the light stopped. I could not hear the stone. You are the hands and I am not, and I have said so before and I am saying it again because it is the whole of what I can and cannot do.”

She stood with that. Nine years of a voice that could read every living cultivator inside the bound as a set of numbers, that had answered figures to the hour for a month, that could hear a sentence go out into the traffic before it landed, and he could not feel a cut in a table four feet from her hand.

“I want you to read all of it,” he said. “Not the five. All of it, as far as it goes, and I want to be in the room for every word of it, and I have wanted that for six hundred years and this is the first morning I have had a reason to ask.”

“Ask properly,” she said, “and hear the answer.”

“I want you to read all of it,” he said. “I want every word of what is past the sixth, in your voice, standing, before it is a line anywhere. That is what I want this morning and it is the only thing I have said this morning.”

She stood at the table and let a want sit in her that she had not expected to have been asked for, and it was one she would have granted in a second and that no one, in nineteen years of record, had ever thought to ask her for.

“Why here,” she said out loud, standing, with both hands flat on the register. “You write terms and you answer figures and you do not explain. You have had five mercies cut into a table for four hundred years and you have put this past them and not in front of them, so that a woman who came up to find a price would have to go past the answer to get to the rule.”

## What the Sixth Cut Holds Down

The dark did not answer, and it never had, and she was not expecting it to, and she said the rest of it anyway because saying it out loud was the method and the method was the only instrument she had ever had.

“The rule goes first,” she said. “Before the offer and before the walls and before whatever is past this. The one that makes a person answerable for what is written is the one you put where I would not look.”

She lifted her hands off the register and stood at the table with the lamp an eyelash in front of her and the whole of what she had read in nine days rearranged behind her eyes into a different shape, which was: a table at the top of a tower with five mercies at the near end, and a sixth thing past them, and more past that, and she had read to the end of what her light reached and had thought that was the end of it.

It was not the end of it.

She put her hand back on the stone at the near end of the register and found the sixth cut by thumb and held it there, and she said out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice, first in her own voice, the thing she had brought nine hundred and four steps up to say and had not said on the way:

“This is mine,” she said. “I came up here to find out what a bargain costs and what it costs is that the person it is about says it first. That is what I have been doing for nineteen years for free and there is somebody who noticed.”


The lamp stood nearly out on the table. The register lay under her hands with five mercies in it and one thing past them and more past that, and there were numbers in the breaks she had not read yet, and inside her head a man who had wanted one thing since he was nineteen was very quiet, and she could not tell whether the quiet was surprise or something worse.
