# The Count In Other Mouths

She lost the number in the seventh county and she did not find it, and the losing of it took about a day and a half, which was longer than she expected and less than she had braced for.

She was sitting at the side of a made road at about the seventh hour on the second morning with her right arm across her knees, and she was trying to do the thing she had done every morning since she was ten years old, which was to say a figure out loud and stand still while it went past her.

She got as far as the fourth hundred and stopped.

Four hundred was the number that was on the record. It had been said standing, in the ordinary voice, at a gap in a low wall, in front of about ten people, by a woman of about fifty called He Yu, and a boy of about sixteen had been bound the day before that out of a queue of two, and one of them was her, and it could not be un-bound by any person in the world including her.

That was the number and she had it.

What she did not have was how many of them she had said it standing in front of.

## The Arithmetic Of The Fourth County

She put the figures out loud at the ninth hour on the first morning, standing, in the ordinary voice, at the side of the road, because the gap between the last place she had stood in and this one was seven hundred and fifty days and there is no way of crossing that in a head without saying it out loud.

“One thousand nine hundred and forty days since the seventh of the month beginning two thousand nine hundred and fifty-four. That is five years and a hundred and forty days and I am twenty-four.

“One thousand seven hundred and thirty-seven days since the thirtieth of a month beginning three thousand and one hundred and thirty-four.

“One thousand eight hundred and sixteen days out of a room. Two thousand five hundred and ninety-nine days in that room before it. Two thousand six hundred and six days with this arm open at the elbow, and I am going to be forty before it closes if it ever does, and it will not.”

Then she had said the run and there was a sixth figure in it and there was nothing behind the sixth one, and she did not go looking for it, because she had been through that on a rim in the second year of a life she was not having any more of.

## Four Answers Out Of One Afternoon

She did the arithmetic, out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice, on the side of a road in the seventh county, which is what she does, and she got four answers before the light went.

She did it by counties. Nine counties, and she had walked all nine, and she had stood in about two hundred and thirty villages in them over four years and nine months, and if the four hundred were spread evenly across nine counties that was about forty-four to a county, and forty-four to a county across twenty-five villages was under two to a village, and she had stood in front of people who were not hers every single day of it.

She did it by villages. Two hundred and thirty villages and about seventeen hundred days of walking, and about four people to a village in the villages where she had found the ones that were hers, and that gave a number she was not willing to say out loud twice.

She did it by days, which is the instrument she has always trusted, and it came out with a remainder.

And she did it by what the boy of about fourteen with a slate had told her, which is below.

Four answers and not two of them the same, and there is not one instrument in nine hundred years that can tell her which of the four is true.

## The Slate

The boy had found her on the fourth day and he had been finding her at intervals ever since, and he was about fourteen now and he had not asked her for anything in a year and a half and she had not been able to get rid of him.

He had a slate.

Not the same slate. She did not know where the first one had gone and he did not either, and there were numbers on this one in a hand that was his own and had got steady.

“Villages,” he said.

“How many.”

“Two hundred and forty-one.”

“Villages or stops.”

“Villages. I stopped at four hundred and ninety-one places. I counted the ones that had people in them and I did not count a village twice, and I did most of the counting myself after about a year, and I know the two places I have got wrong.”

“Who counted you.”

“My aunt counted me for about a year and then stopped and my mother started and then she stopped,” said the boy, “and I have been doing it myself since and I have got it wrong twice and I did not find out for about six months.”

She looked at the slate for a long time.

“That is a number of places,” she said.

“Yes.”

“That is not a number of people. I have got a figure for a figure and it is the wrong figure, and there is not one thing in the world that will turn a place into a person.”

The boy said, “I thought you wanted a number.”

“I wanted one number and there are about nine in the world,” said Ning Yan, “and I have been carrying one of them for nineteen years and it is the only one I am allowed to say out loud, and I cannot get it to sit still beside the other eight.”

## What She Said Standing Up

She said it out loud on the second evening, standing, in the ordinary voice, in a village in the seventh county, in front of about fourteen people who had come to be told something and did not know yet that they were going to be given a problem instead.

“Four hundred,” she said. “That is the number on the record and it has been there since the nineteenth of a month beginning three thousand and one hundred and four, and not one of you can be un-bound from it, including me, and I have not got any way of finding out how many of the four hundred I have said the three sentences to in the last four years and nine months.

“And I have got the number in my mouth this evening and it is the first time in nineteen years that I have said it without knowing what came after it.”

A man of about fifty at the back said it after her. Just said it, the way a man says a thing he is being told at a gate.

Four hundred.

A woman beside him said it too, and two children said it, and there were about fourteen people in a village in the seventh county saying a number that had belonged to one woman until about a minute before.

She stood in front of them and let it happen, and that was the decision, and she made it standing up, with her hands where they could be seen, and she said the rest of it out loud because she was not going to have it come out of her mouth and go into the air without the cost going with it.

“It cannot come back into my mouth,” she said. “That is the whole of it and I am saying it now and not in a week. Once a number is in other people it is not mine, and I cannot correct it, and I cannot check it, and there is not one instrument in the world that would tell me on any morning of the rest of my life whether the number going round nine counties is the number that is on the record.”

The man of about fifty said, “It is four hundred. I have just heard you say it.”

“You have heard me say it once,” said Ning Yan.

## A Woman Beside Him

She did not sleep that night and she got up on the third morning and went back down the lane to the same place because she had to find out whether the number was still the number, and she found nine people there before the seventh hour and six of them said it before she had said anything.

That was the part she had not braced for.

A boy of about nine said it. A woman who had been in the village for about nine years said it. Two men she had never seen said it, and one of them said it in the voice of a man reciting something he had been told twice.

They said four hundred and it was correct, and she could not be certain of that, and she stood in a lane at the seventh hour and worked out standing, in the ordinary voice, what she had done.

She had given away the only number she had. Not the four hundred itself, which could not be taken off anybody. The keeping of it. The place in her head where it had sat since she was nine years old, where it had been a thing she could hold still and look at, and where it had been the one part of nineteen years that had not needed anybody else in it.

## Four Hundred And One

The woman beside the man of about fifty was standing at the back with her arms folded and she said it again when Ning Yan got there.

“Four hundred and one.”

Nine people said four hundred and one.

She said, “That is not the number on the record.”

“Is it not,” said the woman. “Then somebody has added somebody, or somebody has made one up, or somebody has heard it from a person who heard it from you, and you are the only one who can say which of those it is and you have told us on the second evening that you cannot check it.”

She was about thirty and she had a basket on her arm and she had been in that village before the second of the month beginning four thousand eight hundred and seventy-four.

“Yes,” said Ning Yan.

“Then we will go on saying four hundred and one,” the woman said, “until you can tell us, and if you cannot tell us then that is what the number is and you have not got a word about it.”

And she said it a fourth time, and the nine people said it with her, and Ning Yan stood in a lane in the seventh county with the lamp in her left hand and one arm that had been open at the elbow for two thousand six hundred and six days, and did not correct them, because there was nothing in her mouth that she could put there that would be better than what was already in it.
