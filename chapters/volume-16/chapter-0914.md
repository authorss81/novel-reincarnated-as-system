# Nothing Worn


She left it on the thirteenth of the month beginning two thousand nine hundred and eighty-four, at about the ninth hour, and she had known since the seventh that she was going to and had not let herself do it any sooner, because there was a thing she wanted done first and the thing she wanted done first was on the seventh and the seventh was six days gone.

She stood at the near end of the table with her hands on the register and read the whole of it once more, standing, in the ordinary voice, first in her own voice, from the first cut to the end of what her fingers could find.

Then she picked it up.

It was heavier than it looked. It was a slab of stone a hand’s breadth thick and a foot and a half wide and it had been lying on that table through four hundred years of a keeping and it came off the stone with a sound like a plate coming off a shelf, and she held it under her left arm, the arm with three fingers and a thumb on it, and walked it to the door.

## What She Put Down

She stood in the doorway of a room at the top of a tower with a register in her arm and a lamp standing on the table behind her.

“Take it with you,” he said.

“No.”

“It is yours. You read all of it. You put four lines of it into the record in your own voice. There is not one word on that stone that has not gone through your mouth standing.”

“There is not one word on it that went through my mouth standing before it went through the layer’s,” she said, “and that is a different thing, and it is the thing that makes it theirs and not mine.”

She turned round and walked back to the table and set the register down on it, in the middle, square, the way it had lain when she found it, with the first cut toward the near end where her hands knew how to find it.

“The offer is not mine to carry,” she said, “and I am not taking it to a room where I cannot be met. If you need it held to what is on it, then it is on the table and the table is where it was written, and that is the only proof either of us gets.”

“I want you to have it,” he said. “I want you to be the one holding what I said yes to.”

“Why.”

He was quiet for a count of forty.
“Because I would rather be held to a thing by somebody who has read it twice,” he said, “and you are the only person alive who has read it twice. And because I have never once in six hundred years been able to check my own word against anything.”

She kept her hand flat on the register where it had been all morning.

“That is the whole of what is wrong with me and it is not the offer and it is not the walls. I say a thing and it stands in me and nothing in the world can come and set it against what I am.”


She put her hand flat on the register one last time and felt the cuts go past under her palm, deep and even, made by a hand that never wore anything.

## What She Took

“Then hold yourself to it,” she said. “I read it. That is what I did for you.”

She took the lamp.

That was the whole of what she took off that table, and she stood in the doorway for a count of a hundred with the lamp in her right hand and the open arm hanging against her side and looked at the room behind her.

No lamp over the table. No wear in it. A place at the far side with nothing worn at all, not a mark, not a hollow, not a place where a person had ever put a forearm down or shifted in a chair or waited. Four hundred years of a table at the top of a tower with nothing worn in it anywhere on it, and thirteen days of her standing at the near end of it, and not one mark.

She had not put one there either. That was the finding and she had expected it to be something else.

“I am going down,” she said.

“I know,” he said. “I have known since you picked the stone up. I have not said anything because you have had it for six days and it was yours to have.”

“I am not going to come back up here.”

“All right.”

“Not because of you.”

“No,” he said. “Because there is nothing left on that table for you to do. You have read it and you have said it and it has been answered and it has not begun and there is not one more breadth of stone under your fingers that you have not been over. You are going down because the work is done and staying would be the only thing left to do and you have never in nineteen years stayed anywhere for that.”

## Down Nine Hundred and Four Steps

She went out into the stair.

The stair was dark. It had been dark for nine years and she had gone up it on wick as much as oil and she went down it in the same way, one tread at a time, with her left hand on the wall and the lamp caged in the crook of her left arm where it had been on the fourth of the month beginning two thousand nine hundred and twenty-four.

Nine landings. She counted them, because she counted everything, and she went past the fifteenth she had named without counting and past the fourteenth and past the thirteenth where she had fallen and not fallen, and then she was on the twelfth, and the boy was not on it, and the landing was empty and dark the way landings are.

She went down off the twelfth.

The tread past the twelfth was polished. It had been polished on the twenty-fourth of the month beginning two thousand nine hundred and twenty-four by the feet of a man with oil on his hands who had gone up past her without surprise and told her to mind it, and it took her foot exactly the way it had always taken it and she went down.

The lamp went out. She went down about four treads and put her right forearm across the step above and her left hand flat on the stone and lay there.
Her arm went. Not the open one.

The other one, which had carried the lamp nine hundred and four steps up a stair in the dark a month ago and had not been asked to carry anything since, and it shook hard and then went out of her shoulder altogether the way it went when she had been standing too long, and she lay on a cold stair in the dark in a tower with her back against a step and listened to her own breath going nine and then four and then four.

“You went over,” he said, “and I felt it go through you and I could not put a hand on anything. Say something. Say anything. You have been still for a count of ninety and there is nothing I can do.”

“I am here,” she said, to the stair. “I went over on the tread past the twelfth. You told me it took the foot.”

“I told a stranger,” he said. “That was not about you. I did not think you were coming down in the dark.”

“It is about me now,” she said. “Nine hundred and four steps is nine hundred and four steps whoever is on them.”

## The Room She Went Down To

She got her arm back in by counting four and then four again, and she got up off the step, and she went down the rest of it with the lamp relit off her own sleeve, and she did not fall again.

She went past the ninth, the eighth, the seventh, the sixth, the fifth and the fourth, and then she was in her own room.

One hundred and seventy-one days since she had walked out of it with six terms said out loud standing and had not come back. The table was under her hand and it had wear in it, and she knew the wear without putting her hand on it, because it was the table she had done every figure of nineteen years at and the near end was rubbed into a hollow where her forearms had gone.
The boards were face up a foot in from the near end. The terms board was face up a foot further along. A strip of pale wood lay face up under the table at the near end of the leg, in the same place it had lain on the fourth of the month beginning two thousand seven hundred and seventy-four, and a hundred and seventy-one days had not moved it.

A bundle was corded twice under the far end of the table and it was not knotted and it had not been pulled. A bar stood nine feet nine against the left-hand wall.

She put her hand flat on the table she had done every figure of nineteen years at and the hollow took her forearms the way it always had, and she stood in the middle of her own room at about the tenth hour with a lamp in her hand that she did not put anywhere.

“Do not put it on the table,” he said. “That table has a cage lamp on a shelf and it has been out since the first of the month beginning two thousand five hundred and thirty-four, and you have not put a light on it in six hundred and ninety-five days.”

“Why is that.”

“Because the moment you put a light in this room the room stops being the room you were kept in and starts being a room you are standing in,” he said. “You have never once been allowed that and you have never once wanted it and I have never told you, and I am telling you now because in about an hour you are going to want to be able to see this place.”
She stood in the dark of her own room and put the lamp on the stone floor, on the near end, where she had put it ten thousand times, and the light lay along the boards and the terms board and the bar, and the second place at the far side was where it had been, kept four hundred years and empty, and she did not look at it for long.
