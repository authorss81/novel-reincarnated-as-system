# A Thing In A Wall

Three hundred and eleven days since the ninth stage. One hundred and four since it was shut. Seven hundred and forty-nine days with this arm open at the elbow. Seven hundred and forty-two days in this room. One thousand four hundred and eighty-four days gone at this morning, taken out of her at a rate she derived out of her own life, not one of them coming back.

She said it standing at the near end of her own table at about the sixth hour on the thirtieth of the month beginning three thousand and fourteen, with the lamp on the stone a little to the left of where it had stood since the second hour on the twenty-seventh, and then she stood there with her hands flat on the wood and waited for a man to do the sum back at her, which he had done every morning for nine years.

Nothing did the sum back at her.

She gave it until the seventh hour.

She read the near board by and put it back face up a foot in from the near end, and read the terms board and put that back a foot further along.

She went the length of the table with her hand along the wood, past the bundle under the far end where it was corded twice and not knotted, and she came back to the near end and put her hand in the hollow her own forearms had worn there over nineteen years.

Then she said his name.

She said it the ordinary way, not loudly, into her own room, the way a person says a name in a room in which a person used to be.

Nothing came.

She waited about a count of two hundred and she said it again, and this time she listened the way she had learned to listen at ten years old, with the whole of the inside of her head, the way you listen for a man thinking against your own thinking.

There was nothing in her head to listen with. That was the part that undid her knees for about a count of four. She had spent nine years learning the difference between a man being quiet and a man being gone, and the difference was the loudest thing she had ever been inside of, and she had gone looking for it at the sixth hour and at the seventh hour and it was not there in either place.

Then it came.

It did not come the way it had come for nine years. It came up through the floor of her head the way the going of a bundle of boards comes up nine hundred and four steps, felt in the stone rather than heard in a room, and it was further off than it had ever been, and it was a voice.

“I am not gone.”

She put her hand flat on the table and had to put her weight on it.

## What She Could Do About It

“You are further,” she said.

“Yes.”

“Further than a room.”

“Further than a wall,” he said. “I am in the layer. Not in your head. I want to be exact about that, because you have spent nine years hearing me in a place you could put a hand on, and the place you could put a hand on is gone and what is left of me is in the wall of the thing that keeps this building.”

She stood at the near end of the table with her palm on the worn hollow and let that go through her one piece at a time.

“Can I address you.”

“No.”

“Say that again.”

“You cannot address me,” he said. “There is no mouth in me and there has not been a mouth in me since the second hour on the twenty-seventh, and there is not one thing in this building a person can say that will land on me because I am addressed by nothing. I come when I come.”

She tried it anyway, because she had been a person who tried things for nine years.

“Yuan.”

Nothing came. She gave it a count of a hundred and a hundred, and she put her other hand on the table beside the first one, and the lamp burned on the stone beside her at her feet and the boards lay face up and the far end of the room was dark, and nothing came.

Then he spoke, and what he said had nothing to do with her saying his name.

“I have been trying to say something to you since the second hour on the twenty-seventh and I have got as far as the first sentence four times and I have not said any of them.”

## The Test

She stood up straight off the table.

“Answer me one thing so I know the difference.”

“I will.”

“I am going to ask you something and you are going to answer it, and if it comes straight away then I have a door and I will use it, and if it does not then I know what I am working with.”

“Say it.”

“How many days is it since the ninth was shut.”

“One hundred and four.”

She waited. It was already in her, so it told her nothing about the wall.

“Now say something you have not worked out,” she said.

He was quiet for about a count of a hundred, and she understood in the middle of that quiet that he was not being slow. He was somewhere else and there was a distance between wherever he was and the room she was standing in, and the distance was not a number she could put her thumb in, and it was the thing the dissolving had cost him.

“I cannot hold a shape,” he said. “That is what I can tell you about the wall. When I was a person I had a way of arriving in a room and everybody in the room knew where I was, and I had a face and a pair of hands and I could put one of them on a table.

I have none of that now. I am audible and I have no shape and there is nothing in this room that knows where I am, and if you go round that table with your hand out you will not find me at the end of it.”

“That is what you are saying about your body.”

“That is what I am saying about being anywhere,” he said. “I am not in a wall the way a stone is in a wall. I am in it the way a voice is in a stair, and you can hear me from every step of it and you cannot put your hand on the step I am on.”

She stood there with that and felt it go all the way down.

There had been nine years in which a man had been in her head, and he had read her figures back at her and argued with her and been angry with her and said her name and asked her things at the ninth hour, and every single part of that had been done from about the length of a table away.

She had never once been able to put her hand on him, and she had never once known it, because a voice in a head does not feel like something in another country.

He was in another country. She could hear him from here.

## The Quiet In The Middle Of The Day

He did not speak again for the better part of a count of two hundred and two hundred.

She did the whole of the middle of the day standing up, because sitting down had started to feel like something she was doing instead of the day. She went up the room to the second place at the far side and stood four feet off it and did not look at it, because it was kept four hundred years and empty and nothing had ever been sat in it and nothing was going to be, not on this morning.

Then she went to the bar against the left-hand wall and put her hand flat on it and took no weight on it, because a bar nine feet nine that is dated nowhere is not a thing to lean on.

Then she put both hands on the stone wall on either side of the near end of the table and pushed, not hard, the way you push a wall to find out whether anything is behind it.

There was nothing behind it. There was four feet of building and then the inside of the mountain, and somewhere on the other side of all of that was a room with no wear in it, and further still was the whole of a layer that had kept the walls of the world for nine hundred years without once being asked what it was doing it for.

She had spent nine years with a man reading her at the length of a table. She had never once gone looking for where he actually was.

She had not wanted to know. That was the finding, and she stood there with her palms flat on cold stone and let it stand. She had a voice in her head and it was a voice and it was enough, and she had never once in nineteen years asked where it lived, and it turned out that not asking was a decision she had made about him for all nine years without knowing she was making it.

## What He Wants Today

She took a breath of four and let it out and put her hands flat on the wood again, because the wood was there and it had been there for nineteen years and it was the only thing in the room that would take her weight.

“Say what it is you want.”

“I want to start,” he said. “Not to finish. I have had four first sentences since the twenty-seventh and none of them survives being said, and I have gone back over every one of them, and I want to say the first one of the whole thing and have it be the first one and not a copy of a figure I have read off a wall.”

“What is in it at the moment.”

“A number,” he said.

She put her thumb into the hollow.

“Then that is the first thing wrong with it.”

“Yes,” he said. “That is the first thing wrong with it and I have known that since the twenty-eighth and I have not been able to do anything about it, and that is what I have been putting in front of you for three days.

I cannot get to the beginning of it. I have been a reader for six hundred years and the whole of what I know how to do is stand in front of a person and read them what they have already got, and an apology is not that.”

She stood at the near end of her own table in a room that no one could come into and listened to a man she had carried for nine years tell her that he was in a wall and could hear her and could not answer her and could not get to the first line of the one thing he had come back for.

And she understood, standing there with the lamp burning at her feet, that she could not reach him.

Not today. Not by saying a word at it, and not by doing the arithmetic, and not by standing the way she stood. She had a room with a table and a hollow in the wood and a woman in it who said everything out loud before it was a line anywhere, and the thing she was sitting in was four miles off and one wall thick and it came when it came.
