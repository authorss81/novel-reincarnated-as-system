# In Front Of Everybody

They were watching her before she opened her mouth.

She came over the gap in the low wall at about the sixth hour on the last day of the month and there were people all down the length of the made road and two thirds of them turned round when a girl of nineteen came out of the gap carrying a lamp that was not lit.

That was the first thing and it was the whole of the difficulty.

She had said sentences into a room for nineteen years and the room had never watched her. She had said six figures at the ninth hour for six thousand mornings and the only thing on the other side of them had been a wall. She had said a clause once, standing, in the ordinary voice, about a boy of sixteen carrying oil up nine hundred and four steps, and it had gone into a keeping and had never been opened.

There were about forty people on this road and every one of them could see her face.

## Finding Her

She found Dai Yu at about the seventh hour with a pole over one shoulder and her back to a wall, doing what everybody else at the edge of that road was doing, which was standing about and deciding whether to go on.

Ning Yan stopped four paces off and did not come closer.

Dai Yu saw her.

“You came.”

“I came. I am asking you here and now, standing, in the open, with these people in it, the way you said you would want it asked. Say no if you want to say no.”

“No,” Dai Yu said. “I am saying yes. I have been standing here for an hour deciding whether to say yes and I have said it out loud so that everybody here has heard me say it, so it is done and neither of us can do it quietly.”

“Right,” Ning Yan said. “All right.”

## Said First, In The Open

She stood up straight in the middle of a road with people looking at her and said it.

“Dai Yu. I bind you into the Sisterhood, and you are the third of it, and the price of it is on the record. Nothing has been done to you and I am the only one who binds you. I told you what was on the record before you answered and you have answered out loud where people can hear you.”

There was no answer from anywhere. That was what a line going past into a keeping sounded like in a road with forty people on it: nothing at all, and everybody still standing there.

Dai Yu put her pole down against the wall.

“That is done then,” she said. “Say it again to that lot and see what happens.”

## What Happened

A woman near the front said, “Are you doing a thing or asking a question.”

“I am asking you a question.”

“Then ask it.”

“I am asking whether you will let me put you into an arithmetic of mine.”

“What is that.”

“It is a thing that I keep a count of, and you would go in it and stay in it, and the price of it is on the record of a keeping nine hundred years old that runs this world, and I do not know what it costs me, and no one sent you here and nothing is done to you and I am the only one who binds you.”

The woman looked at her for a while.

“No,” she said. “You are talking about a machine again. You told me you would not do that.”

“I am not. That is the whole of what there is.”

“That is a machine.”

A man further back said, “Say it louder.”

She said it louder.

She put her voice up out of her in the middle of a road in front of about forty people and she said the three sentences again, all of them, in the ordinary voice, and it went across the road and stopped at the far side.

Nobody moved.

And she felt her own face go hot in the middle of a road, which she had not done since she was ten years old, and she stood there with the feeling going all the way down through her the way heat goes through a hand.

## Getting It Wrong

She got it wrong for about two hours.

She kept starting at the beginning. She kept starting with the mountain and the nine hundred years and the walls of the world, and every single time she got about a sentence into it somebody would tell her that they had not understood and she would have to go back to the start again.

A man asked her whether this was about the ninth stage, because everybody in that part of the world had heard about a chosen one at some point in their lives in the way you have heard of a thing that is not true.

A boy of about fifteen asked her whether she was going to make them all go somewhere, and she said no, and he said that was what everybody said, and she could not answer that and she had to stand there.

Kan Shou was at the market with her four jars and her tray of wick, and she came over at about the eighth hour with her arms inside her sleeves, and she did not get in the way.

She stood four paces off and listened to the whole of a man saying no to Ning Yan and then she said, to the man and not to Ning Yan, “She has not had a thing to eat since the first of the month and she will not take anything off you, and she is telling you the truth about that as well, so you can decide for yourself.”

The man said no again and went off, and Kan Shou said, “He would have said yes if I had not spoken.”

“Why did you speak.”

“Because it is not a queue if the people in it are being starved into it,” Kan Shou said, and she went back to her four jars.

A woman of about sixty asked her what happened to the ones who said yes if she died, and she said she did not know, and the woman said that was the first true thing she had heard all morning, and that was the worst of it, because the woman said it kindly.

## The Anger

She snapped at somebody about the ninth hour.

She did not mean to and she heard herself do it. A man had asked her twice for the price and she had told him twice that she did not know, and on the third time she said, “If you cannot hear a sentence the first two times that is a thing about you and not about me,” and it came out of her mouth in the ordinary voice and it went across that road and a woman two places along put a hand on her arm.

She said sorry.

She had not said it out loud since she was ten years old. She said it standing, in the ordinary voice, in front of forty people, and it went past her into a keeping like anything else she said, and two people near the front of that road looked at each other.

Then she went and found the man and said it again properly, that she was sorry, and that the man was owed an answer she did not have, and that being asked three times by a stranger on a bad road is not a thing to be short with anybody about.

## What She Did With The Rest Of It

She stopped asking at about the tenth hour.

Not because the road ran out. There were still people standing at the edges of it who had not heard and who came over when she stopped.

She stood in the middle of the road with her dead lamp in her hand and said the true thing.

“I have been asking people since the twentieth and I have got three of them. One of them said yes to me in the wind on her own. The second one said she would only say it in front of people, and she has, this morning. That is three.

“I have been doing this for ten days and I am bad at it and I have told at least four of you about a machine when I should have told you about myself, and one of the four was me being short with him, and I said sorry out loud and I meant it.

“I have not got the words yet. I have got the price being on the record and me not knowing what it costs, and everything else I say takes the wrong shape.

“I will be on this road tomorrow and the day after that and the day after that, and I will keep asking, and I will keep saying sorry in the open when I get it wrong.”

## The Queue Builds Itself

A woman about half way back said, “I will come back tomorrow.”

And then nine of them said it at once, raggedly, over each other, not because she had asked them to and not because anybody told them to, and one more said it after the nine had stopped.

Ning Yan stood in the middle of that road with the rain going into her collar and did not move for about a count of two hundred.

She had been in a room for eight hundred and two days. She had said six figures a morning into a wall for nineteen years and had been answered by nothing, and she had got to the end of that road and said one sentence to a crowd of strangers in the wrong way and been watched while she said it.

And then a road full of people had built itself a queue.

She said the line to every one of the eleven, standing, in the ordinary voice, in the middle of the road, one at a time, and it took her about a count of four hundred, and not one of the eleven went away afterwards.

She put her hand up under her own ribs on the left side, high, behind the fourth one, because that is where she had put it every morning for nineteen years, and it was the size of her thumbnail and it was not different.

Then she said the count out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice, in the middle of the road, where anybody who wanted to hear it could hear it, and it was fourteen.

There were about sixty people at the end of that road in the rain who had not heard one word of any of it.

She had not slept under a roof since the eleventh and she was going to have to do all of it again in about twenty hours.
