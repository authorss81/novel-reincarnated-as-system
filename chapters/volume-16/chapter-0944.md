# Decisions

He began at the sixth hour on the fourth of the month beginning three thousand and seventy-four and he did not stop for the better part of the whole day.

She stood at the near end of her own table and listened to it the way she had listened to the going of a bundle of boards up nine hundred and four steps, all the way up, without letting go of the fact that it was happening.

“I decided what you were going to be.”

That was the first one and there was nothing after it. She waited for a count of forty and he did not add anything to it, and it stood in the room on its own, and it was not a sentence. It was the head of a column with the rest of the column not written yet, and she found that she could not tell whether that was the apology at last or a thing that had come apart before it started.

Then he said the second.

“I decided that you would not be met where you are expected.”

“I sent that down the stair myself,” she said.

“I know you did,” he said. “I want the part of it that is mine in the room.”

She put her hands flat on the wood and did not say another word for the rest of the morning.

## What She Heard

She had been given a very great deal of arithmetic over nineteen years. She had a figure for every day of her life and she could take any two of them and put one against the other and know what a keeping had done to her by the difference.

She had no instrument at all for what was coming out of the wall.

It was not in order. It did not have a day on it anywhere, and it did not have a number on it anywhere, and that was the first thing about it that she noticed and it was the thing about it that made her put her hands flat on the wood, because a man had been reading her figures since she was ten years old and there had not been one in this.

“I decided what a stage was, and I let you believe the stage was the thing that was happening to you.”

“I decided what you could be got to spend, and I decided it before you were old enough to know that you were spending it.”

“I decided that you would keep the count.”

That one she did not have any answer to. She had kept it. She had kept it every morning at the ninth hour for nineteen years and she had never once been asked whether she wanted to, and she would have kept it anyway, and neither of those facts had anything to do with him and both of them had been decided by him in advance.

She put her hand flat on the table where the wood had gone down under her own arms and left it there and did not speak.

## The Part She Stopped

About the eighth hour he came to it and said, “I want to tell you how hard this has been,” and then he said that he had never once in six hundred years had anybody standing in a room who would have let him.

“No.”

“It is one sentence.”

“It is about you,” she said. “You have had nine years and you have never once been in a room where anybody said one word about how it has been for them, and you have got to the middle of the hardest thing you have ever put your hand to and you have stopped to tell me what it has cost you, and I am telling you now, standing, in the ordinary voice, that I am not going to listen to it.”

He was quiet for about a count of two hundred.

“All right,” he said.

“All right.”

He did not say it again. She heard him not say it again for the rest of the day and it was the single hardest thing anyone had ever done in that room, because she had listened to it, because part of her wanted it, and because she had said no standing up in the ordinary voice before it was a line anywhere and the whole of what she got for it was a room with a man in a wall going on.

## The Middle Of The Day

Somewhere in the middle of it the place behind her fourth rib began to pull.

It had done that before, when her body took in a sentence she had not got anywhere to put. It had happened to her perhaps six times in nineteen years and she had never once worked out what it was, and it was doing it now on a morning of a month in a room with a lamp on the floor, and there was no place in this building she could put six hundred years of a man telling her what he had decided.

So she stood there and took them.

She put one hand up under her own ribs on the left side, high, behind the fourth one, and held it there and let it pull.

She went on standing where she was with the other hand flat on the wood while a voice she could not account for said decision after decision at her about her own life. She did not sit down and she did not go and touch a board and she did not put her hand on the bar against the wall.

That was the whole of what she did with her body for the better part of four hours and there was nobody in the world who could have told her that it was enough.

Then, somewhere about the eighth hour, she found that her eyes were doing something she had not sent them to do, and she went and stood at the far end of the room over the place that had been kept four hundred years and empty, with her hand still up under her own ribs, and looked down at a table top where a hollow was not.

## The Decisions

They went through the rest of the day.

He said that he had decided a man in her head should not be looked at by anybody, and that he had not told her that he had decided it.

He said that he had decided a place would be kept at the far side of her table, and that he had known before it was offered that she would not sit in it.

He said that he had heard a question asked of him standing in a room on a night near the end and had sat on the answer for a whole day, and that sitting on it had been a decision. He had made it standing in the same room and in the same voice, and she had kept the silence for him for the whole of it.

He said that he had decided what went down the stair in a cloth with forty boards on the top of it, and that he had let a woman of eighteen go on believing that a thing she had put in her own hand had been put there by her.

That one stopped the room.

She put both hands flat on the wood and stood in a room eight flights and a stair below the stair and worked out that she had been wrong about it for years, and that she had gone over that decision in her own arithmetic more times than any other in her life, and that there was nothing she could do about it now because the only person who could have told her had gone into the substrate of a layer nine hundred years old thirty-seven days ago.

She did not say a word.

He went on.

## The Last One

“I decided on the twenty-fifth of the month before last, before you had asked me for anything, that I was going to refuse it. I decided that in the morning. You came and asked me for it in the afternoon and I did not tell you that I had already said no.”

She did not move off the wood.

“That was the twenty-fifth,” she said. “You had already decided before I opened my mouth.”

“Yes.”

“You let me ask you for it.”

“I let you ask me for it,” he said. “I had the count of it in me and I started it twice and I did not put it in, because a number on the day I decided to end what I am comes out of me as a figure, and a figure on this is the whole of what has been wrong with it since the seventh.”

“No,” she said, “and I am not going to say that number either, and you do not get to have it.”

“All right.”

“All right.”

## Where It Ended

It stopped at about the ninth hour and a hundred without any warning and without a last word, the way it had stopped on the seventh of the month and on the nineteenth and on the twenty-second, and there was nothing after it.

He had not finished. She knew that the way a woman who has listened to a keeping for nineteen years knows when a line has been cut short at the other end, and there was a piece of it that had not been said, and she had heard six hundred years of the inside of a machine that made people less solid and she had been in it the whole time and it had never once finished anything in nineteen years either.

She stood at the near end of her own table and did not fill it.

She did not say that it had not been good. She did not say that it had been good. She did not say that it had taken a long time, because it had, and saying so would have been about him.

She put her hands flat on the worn wood where her own arms had been for nineteen years and she stood there in a room that was a room and let what had been said in it stay said, and she understood standing there, in the ordinary voice, in her own head, which nobody would ever hear, that there was not one decision in the whole of nineteen years left in him that she did not now know about.

He had got to the end of what he had done and had not got to the end of what he was, and she was not going to wait for the rest of it, because she had asked him on the seventh of a month at about the eighth hour not to be a thing that got to the end of things.

She had wanted it to be a thing she could put down. That was the whole of what she had carried it down the stair for, and there was nobody in the room to put it down in front of, and she had known that since the twenty-eighth of a month.

She had asked him not to finish. He had finished.

She got exactly the opposite of what she had asked for, and she was still standing at the wood with her hands flat on it at about the ninth hour and a hundred of the fourth of a month, nineteen years old, and the lamp was burning on the stone at her feet, and it was the only thing in that room that had answered her all day.
