# Nowhere to Put It


“Two hundred and thirty-two days since the ninth stage. Six hundred and sixty-three days in that room. The cage out four hundred and thirty. This arm open six hundred and seventy days. One thousand four hundred and five gone at this morning.”

She said it sitting on the stone at the near end of the table in the making-place, on the eleventh of the month beginning two thousand nine hundred and fifty-four, with the lamp on the stone beside her and the oil nearly out of it, and the register under her other hand.

The bar stood six hundred and twenty days against its wall, dated nowhere.

## The Check She Ran

Three days since he said it. She had not asked him once in three days, and she had spent every one of them wanting to, and the not asking had been a discipline and not an accident, because she had heard one sentence and she wanted to know whether it would still be there on the fourth morning the way everything she had ever been told by anybody or anything had still been there.

“Say it again,” she said.

“I want it,” he said. “I have wanted it since I was nineteen.”

Same. The same weight on the word *it*. The same plainness. Not the voice of a man repeating a thing to be kind to somebody who did not like hearing it, which she had heard from other mouths in nineteen years and knew exactly what it sounded like, and this was not it.

“That is the third time,” she said. “Give me a figure.”

He did not go quiet. That was the thing she was listening for, and he did not go quiet, which meant the silence was not thought and was only him deciding how much of the truth to hand her.

“There is no figure,” he said.

“There is always a figure.”

“There is always a figure in what I say to you,” he said. “Nine years. Days since. Days in. Days lost. Days at a stage. Four counts of breath and seven on a rise. The number of boards on a table. I have handed you a figure every single time anything has come out of my mouth, and you have never once had to take a word of mine on trust.”

“Nine years,” she said, “and not one sentence I could not have put in my own arithmetic if I had wanted to.”

“Not one,” he said.

## What an Absence Proves

She put her hands flat on the stone and turned it over the way she turned everything, once, twice, three times, and it came out the same every time, and the same was this: a man who has counted everything he has ever said for nine years has just said the largest thing he has ever said and there is no number in it.

“A man lying about a want puts a number on it,” she said, “because a number is what makes a want checkable, and if he cannot check it he does not want you to look at it too long. That is not a rule I made up. That is every bargain anybody has ever put in front of me.”

“That is true,” he said.

“And you have put nothing in front of me.”

“I have put nothing in front of you,” he said, “because there is nothing to put. I did not choose to leave it off. I went looking for it yesterday morning the way you go looking for a landing with your foot, and it was not there.”

She sat with that for a while and her breath went four counts and the lamp gave its eyelash across the stone and the room at the top of the tower did what it had done for four hundred years, which was nothing.

“Here is the check,” she said. “Nine years, and every number you have ever handed me came out the same when I turned it. If this one came out the same I would take it and put it in a line and go on. It does not come out the same. It comes out unchanged, and unchanged is not the same as checked, and I have never in nineteen years accepted a thing that came out unchanged.”

“No,” he said. “You have not.”

“So I am accepting it on a different finding.”

“What finding.”

She thought about it for long enough that he waited, which he had never once failed to do, in nine years, when she needed a count of forty and got it.
“That a thing you can put a figure on can be argued with,” she said. “Days since. Days at a stage. Every cost in this has a day on it and I can set it against another day and it moves. This has no day on it. There is nothing to set it against.”

She turned her thumb over on the stone without lifting it.

“And a man who is telling me the truest thing he has does not need me to check it, because if I check it I will find nothing, and there being nothing to find is the whole of what he is telling me.”


He was quiet for a count of forty, which was his way of keeping a thing where she had put it instead of moving it.

“That is the first time in nine years,” he said, “that anyone has taken a thing I said and made it stronger by not checking it.”

## The Thing With No Number

“I did not make it stronger,” she said. “I made it arithmetic. There is a difference and you would be the first to know it.”

“Give me something to hold it by,” she said. “That is all I am asking. I have held every cost in this for nineteen years by the day it happened on and I would hold this the same way. Tell me when it started and I will put it in a line and it will be in my arithmetic for the rest of my life and it will be no worse for that.”

“No,” he said.

“You cannot work it out.”

“I can work it out exactly,” he said. “I know what year I died and I know what was offered and I know it was offered then and refused then. I could put a figure on this morning that would be correct to the hour and you would have no way of knowing it was wrong.”

“Then do it.”

“No,” he said. “Because if I give you a figure, then it is a figure, and then it is a claim, and then you owe me the argument you give every claim, and I would have spent the one thing I have on making you do arithmetic about the one thing I do not want arithmetic about.”

She sat on the stone in the dark at the top of a tower with a lamp nearly out beside her and had nothing at all to do with what he had given her.


That was the part that was new. Not the answer. The lack of anywhere to put it. Six hundred and sixty-three days in a room. Six hundred and seventy days of an arm open at the elbow. One thousand four hundred and five days gone at a rate she could derive from her own life. Every single one of them held by a date and a column and a sum she did in her mouth before her hand ever moved.

Nineteen years of a life built out of the certainty that everything that happens to a person has a figure on it somewhere, and a man had just handed her the largest thing in it and the figure was not there.

She put her hand flat on the register and found the fifth cut with her thumb and stopped.

“I have nowhere to put it,” she said.

“No,” he said. “You do not.”

“Nineteen years,” she said. “Not one thing. Not one cost, not one day, not one refusal. You have given me a want with nothing in it and I have no arithmetic for it and I have never in my life had to hold a thing I could not count.”

## The Thing She Wanted From Him

“No,” he said, “and I am not sorry about that, and I want you to hear that I am not sorry about that.”

She sat with that for longer than she meant to and the wanting came up in her out of the place where her own wants had been kept, which was not a place she had looked at in nineteen years, because there had never been anything in it to look at.

She wanted him to want something from her.

Not the offer. Not the walls. Not the four minutes or the word *work* or anything that was on a register with cuts in it. Something from her, from her specifically, that she could give and that would cost her something and that he would not have gotten otherwise, and she had been waiting nine years for it and had not once admitted that she was waiting.

She was nearly certain that he heard her think it, because there was no room in her head that was his alone, and she waited for him to say something and he did not.

That was the part that undid her.

“Say it,” she said.

“Say what.”

“That you want something from me. I have been sitting here wanting you to and you have not said it once in nine years and I have just found out that I wanted it, and if you say it now I will give you something, and if you do not say it I will have to go on being a woman who hands things down a stair and gets nothing at the other end for it.”

He was quiet for a count of a hundred.

“Yes,” he said. “I want something from you.”

She waited.

“I am not going to tell you what it is,” he said. “Not because you would use it against me or because you would say no. Because you would say yes, and you would say yes before you knew what it was, and that is the thing I have spent nineteen years refusing to do to you.”


The lamp gave its eyelash across the stone. The register lay under her hand with five cuts in it and nothing worn in the table.


Eight flights and a stair below her a boy was carrying oil he made and could not buy, and the room was running on firsts without her. Inside her head a man who had wanted one thing since before she was born had just told her that he wanted one more thing and had put a wall around it.
She sat on the floor of a room with no wear in it holding an unmeasured want in both hands and no place in her at all to set it down.
