# It Goes Up On Its Own

Three hundred and seventy-two days since the ninth stage. One hundred and sixty-five since it was shut. Eight hundred and three days in that room, and there was no room and there was not going to be one again.

Five hundred and seventy days since a cage lamp went out on a shelf. Eight hundred and ten days with this arm open at the elbow. One thousand five hundred and forty-five days gone, taken out of her at a rate she derived out of her own life, not one of them coming back.

She said all of it standing at the ninth hour on the first of the month beginning three thousand and one hundred and four, in the ordinary voice, in the middle of a road, first in her own voice before it was a line anywhere.

She said the five hundred and seventy days of a cage lamp she was never going to see out again, and she said it, and she put it in the keeping anyway, and nothing came back and she had not expected anything to.

## What Came Up Under Her

It came up about the fifth hour and she felt it in the ground.

She was standing at the edge of the road with a queue of nine people in front of her and she put her hand flat on the earth because she felt it in her feet, and then she took her hand off and stood there and let the whole of it come up.

A bundle of boards, corded twice, going up nine hundred and four steps of a stair that did not stop at nine.

She had felt it come up under her twenty-five times standing at the foot of that stair, and she had felt it come up under her once standing on a stone floor with a board in her hand, and that one had been ten days before she walked out of a building and she had not gone down the stair for it on purpose.

She had not felt one the whole of the time she had been on the mountain and she did not know why she had thought she would.

That was this morning. This morning she was on a road in a county where she knew three people, and it went up nine hundred and four steps to a room with no wear in it, and it was cut, and the boards went somewhere, and there was not one person in the world standing under it.

## A First Of A Month

She had marked firsts of a month for nineteen years.

She had said the ninth hour of every one of them in a room with a lamp on the floor like it was a day that had something on it. She had been under twenty-five of them at the bottom of that stair and had stood in the room and let the rest of them go by her, and she had built nineteen years of arithmetic on a day that climbs a building by itself once every thirty days.

This was the first first of a month she had spent outside that mountain.

She stood there in the middle of it and understood that there was nothing whatever for her to do with it. It did not want anything off her. It was not a day that came up a stair for her, and it was not a day she was going to give anything to, and she had brought a queue of nine people to a first of a month and had nowhere to put them and no hour of the day that belonged to her except the one she had just used.

She had not known that a first of a month could be an ordinary day. She had spent nineteen years being told by the arithmetic that it could not.

## The Day Of It

She asked.

She asked all day. She stood on the road with the wind off the flat coming into her back and she said the three sentences out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice, and then she waited, and then she said the line.

She said the line once for every person who said yes, and by the time the light went she had said it fifty-four times in one day, and she had not known at the sixth hour that it was going to be a number.

She had a crowd by the ninth hour and she had a queue all afternoon, and the queue was not orderly and there was nobody organising it and it formed itself the way water forms in a rut, and people came down the road and saw a queue and joined the end of it without knowing what it was a queue for.

She had never in her life seen a thing form with not one person putting it there, and she had been in the world nineteen years and she had watched people start things every day of it. She had not been able to do it. She had done one thing in nineteen years that was hers, which was a count at the ninth hour, and it had gone into a wall.

There were people on that road who had come down off the hills to stand behind a girl with a lamp that was not lit, and every one of them had done it on their own account.

A young man of about twenty-two called Guo Sheng came and stood at the back of it and worked the whole of the day without being asked, turning people round when they wandered off, and telling anybody who wanted to know that she was not selling anything and that the first one to come had been yesterday and that the woman at the stall with the oil was the one to ask.

He asked her, at about the sixth hour, whether she needed anything, and she said no, and he said that was all right, that he was not going to anyway, that she had the look of a person who would not take it.

## The Body Of It

By the fourth hour her breath had gone to nine and had stayed there.

Nine is what her body did standing still. It had been four on the flat in a room for nineteen years and seven on a rise, and standing all day in the open saying a sentence out loud two hundred times had taken it to nine and had not given it back, and by the seventh hour she was aware of every breath she was taking and none of them were doing any work.

Her left arm was shaking by the third hour and it was not the open arm. It was the hand with three fingers and a thumb on it, the one that could hold a lamp and nothing heavier, and she had been holding a lamp in it for nine hours in case anybody thought she needed it.

She put the lamp down on the ground at about the seventh hour and left it there and picked it up again twice in the next hour for no reason at all.

Somebody said it out loud in the queue, about the fourth hour, that she had not eaten since the first of the month, and had she not, and when she said she had not needed to for a long time, the road went quiet for about a count of forty.

She worked out afterwards that they had not gone quiet because of the keeping or the arithmetic. They had gone quiet because a girl of nineteen who had come down off a hill five days ago had said she did not need to eat, and it had sounded like something else entirely, and there is nothing you can say to a road about that.

## The Part She Got Better At

She got one thing better over that day and she did not decide to.

She used to say the line the instant a person stopped talking, because the instant they stopped talking was the end of their yes and she had been in a room for nineteen years where an ending was a thing that could be relied on.

By the fourth hour she had stopped doing it. She waited.

She watched a man’s face and she waited until he had actually finished, and about a third of the time he said something else after the first yes. A man of about fifty said yes, and then said his brother had said no and he wanted to know whether that made a difference, and she had to answer a question about a brother before she said the line, and she answered it out loud in front of everybody and the man went white and then said yes again.

She could tell the difference by the fourth hour.

On the first day she had asked anybody, she had not been able to tell the difference at all.

## The Count

She said it out loud at the ninth hour, standing, in the ordinary voice, with the queue still on the road behind her.

Sixty-eight.

One of them was a boy of sixteen in a mountain with oil he made and could not buy. One of them was a woman who sold oil and wick at a stall and had a reason involving two children and a bowl. One of them was a woman with a pole who had made herself say it out loud in front of a crowd so that neither of them could do it quietly.

Sixty-five strangers, and she had their count, and she could add to it standing without putting a hand on anything.

She stood in the middle of that road at the ninth hour and worked out, standing, that she had said four words out loud to five people in eleven days and three sentences to sixty-eight, and that the only thing she had got better at was waiting for the end of a yes.

She could not put a face to fifty of them. She had taken the count on the road from a person with a lamp and a number in her head and it was the first number she had ever had that was not out of her own life.

She had got sixty-eight of them into her arithmetic and she had no way at all of carrying sixty-eight people.
