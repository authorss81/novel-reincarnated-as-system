# Nowhere To Look In

She walked the whole length of that road twice before the light came properly, and the second time she got to the far end of it and turned round, and there was a boy of about fifteen driving pigs across the place where the end of it used to be, and the pigs went round her and carried on.

Three days since the four hundredth. The bundle He Yu had brought was still sitting on the wall where Ning Yan had told her to leave it, and it had taken the wet through on one side, and it was still exactly where she had put it.

The queue was gone. That was not a surprise to anybody. A queue exists where people have heard, and everybody in that county who had heard was already in her arithmetic.

## The Question She Had Not Said Yet

She had got up on the twenty-first of the month with a question in her mouth and she had not said it out loud, which was not like her.

The question was not about the four hundred. The four hundred existed and could not be un-bound and she had said that standing in the rain to a woman of about fifty and she was not going to say it again.

The question was this. Nine hundred years of a keeping, nine hundred years of walls, and one sentence in the whole of that time that could take it.

Somebody had to know what that sentence looked like. Somebody had to have a copy of it, or a draft, or a rejected one with a mark against it, or a precedent of some kind, and she had gone down a mountain with a lamp she could not refill and had not once asked herself where she was going to look.

She found that out on the twenty-first of the month, at about the seventh hour, and the finding took her about a count of four hundred.

She had never in nineteen years wanted a thing she could not produce out of her own head.

## Where She Went First

Kan Shou’s stall was the first thing on the road, and it was the first place she asked.

She asked it badly, standing, in the ordinary voice, in the open, with about six people within hearing, and Kan Shou looked at her for a while before she answered.

“What sentence.”

“The kind that gets written down.”

“That is not a sentence,” Kan Shou said. “That is a line in a book, and I do not have a book, and I have not had one since my husband died, and what he had in it was what people owed for oil.”

She said that kindly and she went back to her jars.

Ning Yan stood on the road and understood that she had asked the wrong question in a way she had not known she could ask it.

She had said “the kind that gets written down” as though there were a kind, and there were nine hundred years of a grammar in her head. She had just heard a woman who had been on that road every day since the twentieth of a month tell her, in the plainest words anybody had used with her in three days, that she had gone to a stall and asked for the sort of thing that lives in a different country.

Then Kan Shou said, “What are you doing on this road.”

“I am standing on it.”

“That is not what I asked.”

“I am asking people where something gets written down,” Ning Yan said, “and I have asked you, and you have told me.”

Kan Shou wiped her hands down her apron and looked down the road and then back at her.

“You are asking the wrong woman. And so is everybody else on this road, and so has everybody been for three days. There is not one person in this county you could ask that would give you the right answer, because if it is written down anywhere it is not written down here.”

“Why do you say that as though you know.”

Kan Shou thought about it with her hand on a jar.

“Because I have had a husband buried and had him put on a thing by a man in a county town with a wet thumb, and that man did not know his name either. He wrote it down because it was his day to write it down.” She turned a jar over. “Everything that has ever been written about me in nineteen years, somebody wrote it because it was the day for it.”

## The Board Under The Eave

She walked back up the row of roofs to the place where she had sat with a flat board off a wall and a stub of charcoal on the twenty-second of the month beginning three thousand and seventy-four and had written out three sentences twice and had cut them down twice.

The board was still there. It had been put back under the eave by somebody, not by her, and it had gone grey along the top with rain, and the charcoal had run in three places and the first line had gone off into a grey cloud you could not read at all.

The second line was still there and it was the one that mattered. The price of it is on the record. I do not know what it costs me.

She got down on the wet stones and looked at it for about a count of a hundred and twenty.

It was the only writing in nine counties that she was answerable for, and not one living person had ever come and read it, and she had put it under an eave herself so that the rain would be the thing keeping it.

## The Woman Whose Floor It Was

She went back to the house where she had taken a floor on the ninth of the month, after walking out of a building for the first time, and where a woman had brought her a bowl of something hot and had not stayed in the room.

The woman was in the yard with a hoe over her shoulder. She looked at Ning Yan and did not put the hoe down.

“I want to ask you a thing.”

“Ask.”

“Is there anywhere in this county that keeps writing.”

The woman thought about it for longer than Ning Yan had expected, and then she put the hoe against the wall, which was the difference between not wanting to answer and being willing to.

“My mother kept a book,” she said. “What people owed a hoe to each other. It was not even good. It was burnt in a year I am not going to tell you about.”

“What was in it.”

“Hovels,” the woman said. “And a pig, twice. And a name written wrong twice, and neither time did anybody say a word about it.”

Nine hundred years of a layer that kept everything, and the longest record of anything at all in that county was a list of hoes that had gone in a fire.

Then the woman said, “Is it somebody’s job.”

“No.”

“Then what is it for.”

That stopped her. She stood in the yard in the rain with her hand against the wall and found that she did not have an answer that was any use, because the honest answer was that a keeping of nine hundred years was not anybody’s job and had never been anybody’s job, and the whole of it ran on the same thing every time, without a person in it, and she was the only person in the world who had ever been near it.

“I am looking for a sentence,” she said, “and it is not in any of your books, and it is not at the stall, and it is not on a board under an eave.”

“Then it is nowhere,” the woman said, “and that is the first sensible thing anybody has said on that road since you came off the hill.”

## The Whole Of It

She went back to the crossing where the two roads met and stood at the gap in the low wall with the lamp in her left hand and the wet coming down off the stones behind her. She did the whole of the working out in one place, because she had tried it three times on the road and had got the same answer three times.

The register was on a table at the top of a mountain.

It was the only place in the world that had ever held a sentence of this layer in its hand, and it had five mercies cut into it and a set of terms and not one line about how a person takes one of them off. She was never going back up that stair to look, and there was nothing else anywhere.

There was no book. There was no board. There was no wall with a mark on it and no woman at a stall with a book of oil debts and no house where anybody had ever written a refusal down, in nine hundred years, because not one had ever happened.

She said it out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice, at the gap in the wall, in the rain, on the twenty-second of the month beginning three thousand and one hundred and four, with a boy driving pigs past at the far end of it.

“There is not one place in the world where anybody has ever written down a refusal.”

Then she stood with her hand flat on the stone of the gap and said the other half, which was the half she had come out here to say.

“So there is no copy, and there is no example, and nothing in nine hundred years has ever been written down that sounds like what I have to say.”

She looked at the road, which was empty, and at the wall, which had a gap in it because somebody a long time ago had taken a stone out and had not put it back.

“It is going to have to be built by me, out of my own head, standing up, in bad weather, with nothing to copy. And I have not got the first part of it yet. I have got four hundred people and an arithmetic and I have not got nine words.”
