# Left On The Steps

The rain came in off the west at about the fourth hour and settled in over the quarry for the rest of the day, and by the sixth hour the whole of the flat knew there were people still on the old terrace above it.

Knowing and doing something about it were two different jobs, and she had the second one and not the first.

## Forty Times Up And Down A Track

She went up at the fourth hour and she came down about forty times.

That is not a figure she could have put anywhere. It is what she did.

She went to the top of the track and she came down the track and she went up it again. Each time she carried whoever was on the terrace wanted to come off and could not manage it alone, and each time she came back up to stand in the gap at the top with the rain in her face and the rest of them looking at her to find out what she knew.

She knew one thing and she told them that one thing about forty times.

That is what a day was. Not the counting, which she has done since she was seven. The standing in the gap at the top of a stone staircase in the rain being looked at.

## What People Wanted Off It

A man of about fifty wanted his bench.

That was the one she could not do anything clever with. His bench was on the fourth course and it was eight feet of ash under a stone weight, and he had made it, and there was one of them.

She said no.

He said, “It is eight feet of ash and there is one of it.”

“It was,” she said. “And there were forty-one of us on the thing yesterday and a boy got his foot shut in the gap, and I was not able to tell you why and I was not going to pretend I could.”

“I am not asking you why. I am asking you to hold one end of it.”

“I have one arm that works,” Ning Yan said, “and it was carrying your neighbour’s boy down it at about the seventh hour. If I put my hands on a bench I was not carrying anybody, and there was a woman on the second course who had not come off yet and who could not do the tread in front of mine.”

He stood in the rain and looked at her for a while.

Then he said, “You are nineteen.”

“Yes.”

“My sister said nineteen, about a year back, and she was not wrong.”

And he went down the track and left the bench, and she did not see him again until the light went, and by then the bench was not on the terrace any more.

## The Cart

A carter came up from the flat at about the ninth hour with a cart and one boy and a rope, and he wanted to take his load down off the fourth course and go away with it.

He had salt in four sacks and a grindstone and a churn and somebody else’s churn as well.

She let him take the salt. She told him to take one sack at a time and to leave the rest, and he left the rest, and the grindstone went down with him in pieces because he would not leave it, and the churns stayed.

He was not angry about it and that was worse. He said, “That is four hundred and sixteen households and the stone is under most of them,” and then he went away down the flat without saying one word to her about any part of it, which she noted, and did not put anywhere.

## The Man Who Came Back Up

He came back up the track at about the eighth hour to get his bench.

She was at the bottom of the track and she watched him come, and she could not go up it in front of him, because there were nine people still on the terrace above and she had been going up and down it about forty times since the fourth hour.

He got to the second course and there was no bench on it.

There was a place on the course where the ash had been, in the shape of a bench, with the edges of it still sharp against the stone, and the stone either side of the shape had closed on it about a hand’s width.

He stood there for about a count of four hundred.

Then he came back down and stopped at the bottom of the track and looked at her, and she braced, because she had been waiting for this since the fifth hour.

He said, “It is not the terrace.”

“No.”

“It is the bench going into the stone.” He said it the way a man says a thing he has just worked out and does not like. “Nine years of the weather getting into it. The terrace has not moved since I was a boy and the bench is what let go.”

She stood in the mud at the bottom of a track and had not one figure for that, and could not have had one, and found that she did not need one for what she had to say.

“Yes,” she said. “That is what happened, and I have been standing here all day being certain it was the terrace, and you have worked it out in about a count of four hundred from the bottom of a track.”

“Do not do that.”

“Do not do what.”

“Do not put a thing I said into that voice.” He wiped his face with the back of his wrist, which did not need doing. “I am not a thing on your list. I am a man who has lost eight feet of ash.”

“No,” Ning Yan said. “You are not.”

## The Question With No Answer

A boy of about thirteen came up the track at the second hour of the afternoon and stood at the bottom of it for a long time before he came the rest of the way.

“Is it somebody doing it.”

She had been waiting for that since the fourth hour of the day before and she had nothing to put under it.

She said, standing, in the ordinary voice, at the foot of a track in the rain, to one boy.

“No. I was not able to find anything that made it look like somebody was doing it. I asked myself it four times today and I got the same answer four times, and the answer is that I do not know.”

“That was not the same as no.”

“No,” she said. “It was not. I was telling you the two things separately because putting them together would have been the easier thing for me and I was not going to do it.”

He stood there and let that go past him.

“My mother is on the second course,” he said.

“I know. She was the last one I would have got off. She will be down before the light.”

## The Figures At The Foot Of The Track

She said the figures at the ninth hour standing at the foot of the track with about nine people within hearing, and there was only the one worth saying.

Eighty-one days out of a room.

Eight hundred and sixty-four days in that room before it, which is a number about her own life and the only kind she has left, and she said both of them out loud in the ordinary voice in the rain and then she did not say anything else, and two people who had never heard her do it before asked her what the second one was.

She told them. It is a room and it is over and that is all of it, and neither of them understood a word of what she said and one of them wrote it down anyway on the back of his hand, which she found out about on the second day of a month and never mentioned to anybody.

## The First Ground

It came at about the ninth hour and a bit, when the rain had gone slack and the light had gone the colour of a wet slate.

There was no sound.

She had been braced for a sound for a day and a half, standing at the bottom of that track with her weight forward, because stone that lets go makes a noise and nine hundred years of a terrace going over four courses at once makes a noise you can hear in the next county.

There was not one.

What came instead was a sort of lean. The whole of the terrace went forward about a hand’s width and stopped, and the four courses above the gap went down into the gap and the gap closed, and a wall of water came off the top course and went out across the flat in one sheet, and the rain came back into it after that.

And then it stopped, entirely.

The whole of the flat was standing clear of it.

Forty-one people and about thirty others had come off that terrace over the two days because one woman stood in a gap at the top of it for a day and a half and would not be moved off her own two feet, and every one of them was on the flat or on the track when the lean came, and not one of them was under a single stone.

She worked that out standing at the bottom of the track with her own two hands, by name, the way she does, and she got every one of the thirty that she had a name for and the rest of them were on the flat because the flat had forty people on it at the ninth hour and she had looked at them and counted them at the fourth hour.

Not one.

## What She Could Not Put Anywhere

The terrace did not go over.

It sat there in the rain, four courses lower than it had been and a hand’s width forward, with the top two filled in and the dust washed off it, and it was a terrace again by the time the light went, and it looked like a thing that had been there nine hundred years, which it had.

Not one person in the flat said anything about that, and there was nothing to say about it, and she stood at the foot of the track and could not work out how a thing that had been sitting still for nine hundred years had picked up and leaned forward about a hand’s width and then stopped and been all right.

She could not derive it and so it did not go into anything she said out loud.

What she did do, at about the eleventh hour, in the rain, at the foot of a track, with about twenty people standing about who had spent the whole day being moved off a stone staircase by one person they had never seen before, was this.

She said, standing, in the ordinary voice, out loud, so that the whole of it was on the record before anybody said anything to anybody else:

“Not one of them. That was what I was putting out and it was the first thing I had had in two days.

“There was a bench on that terrace and a carter’s grindstone and one of his churns and about four hundred pots and seven people’s tools, and not one person. And I was not going to soften any part of that by telling you what I found instead.”

The flat was quiet for about a count of two hundred.

Then the kiln-man’s wife said, “Then the firing is still on.”

“The firing was still on,” Ning Yan said, “and there was nothing under it to come down on, and she had four hundred pots to rebuild out of, and I was the one who carried three people off that terrace and took their work off with them.”

She said that last part out loud because it was true and because she had worked out, standing in the rain at the foot of a track, that if she did not say it out loud she would go on and on doing this for nine more days with it sitting in her where the figures used to go.
