# His Part of It

“Two hundred and forty-four days since the ninth stage. Six hundred and seventy-five days in that room. The cage out four hundred and forty-two. This arm open six hundred and eighty-two days. One thousand four hundred and seventeen gone at this morning.”

She said it standing in the dark at the near end of the table in the making-place, on the twenty-third of the month beginning two thousand nine hundred and fifty-four, with her hands on the register and no light anywhere in the room and the oil gone since the seventeenth.

## Nine Days of Reading

The bar stood six hundred and thirty-two days against its wall, dated nowhere.

She had been at it for six days the way a person is at a thing they have decided to finish. Thumb a breadth. Three fingers and a thumb finding the start of each cut in the stone. The line out loud in the ordinary voice, standing, before it was a line anywhere. Then the next breadth. Then the next.

There is no faster way to read by hand and she did not look for one. She read the way she had walked up the stair, one tread at a time, and the days went past the same way they had gone past all the way to the top.

She had found seven more blocks past the first new one. Some were a line and some were four and one was nine lines long and had taken a whole night because her hand shook on the eighth and she would not let a line go past her mouth that she had not found with her fingers first.

## The Second of What Is Past

On the sixth night she had said out loud, to the room, that the Scribe does not sign and had never signed and had been writing terms and answering figures for as long as there had been a keeping, and that she had established that on the eleventh of the month beginning two thousand nine hundred and fifty-four, and that it had held for a month.

The second new term was on the seventh day of reading and it was four lines long.

“He is archived where he is, and addressed wherever he is, and remembered in the keeping of what he did and not of what he cost. He is given a name that is not Sheng, and he is given work that matters, and neither of the two is a rank.”

She read it out loud standing in the ordinary voice in a room with no light in it at about the fourth hour on the twenty-third of the month beginning two thousand nine hundred and fifty-four.

## Read to Him

Then she said it again, the whole of it, with the room listening.

“You did not stop me,” she said.

“No,” he said.

“I have been reading this aloud for six days and you have never once asked me to stop, and I have just read out loud the whole of what there is for you on this table, standing, in my own voice, before it is a line anywhere, and you sat there and let me.”

“I did,” he said.

“Why.”

He was quiet for a count of forty.

“Because you have never read anything to me before,” he said. “Nineteen years. You have counted at me and refused at me and asked at me and told me what I cost you. You have never once held up a thing and read it out so that I could hear what I sounded like being offered it. And I want that, and I am not going to be the one who takes it away from you by flinching.”

She stood in the dark with her hands on the register and felt her face do something she had no control of and had not felt since she was a child in a room she did not want to be in.

“Read it again,” he said.

“No.”

“Read it again,” he said, “and I will let you hear it a third time.”

## The Name It Does Not Give

She stood there for a count of a hundred with her mouth shut and then she read the four lines again, out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice, first in her own voice, and it was worse the second time and she could not have told anybody how.

“The name,” she said, when she had finished. “It does not give it.”

“No,” he said. “It says a name that is not the one I died under and it does not say which.”

“So whoever is doing this hands you to yourself with a name chosen for you.”

“Yes,” he said.

She put her hands flat on the stone on either side of the register and worked out what that meant, standing, in the dark, the way she worked out everything.

“It puts the whole of the naming in one place and it is not yours,” she said. “Six hundred years and the one thing you have not had is a name that somebody chose for you while you were in a room and able to hear it, and this puts it on a table and does not say it.”

“That is what it does,” he said.

“You have thought about that for six days.”

“I have thought about nothing else for six days,” he said. “I have turned the four lines over the way you turn figures and they come out the same every time, and what they come out to is that I am to be handed to somebody and I do not get a word in the handing.”

She stood very still.

“Say the rest of it.”

## What She Would Not Do

“There is one way I get a word,” he said, “and I am not going to take it before you have been to the end of the table.”

“No,” she said.

“No to what.”

“You do not want me to name you. That is what the six days of thinking has come to, and you have been carrying it since the twenty-third and you have not said it.”

“I have been carrying it since the eighth,” he said. “I have wanted you to say a name to me since the eighth and I have not asked you for it and I am not going to.”

“You cannot not ask. You have told me twice in nine days that you are going to tell me things on purpose now. So tell me.”

“I want you to name me,” he said. “That is it. That is the whole of it and it has no figure on it either.”

She stood in the dark and had it in her mouth and it came out before she had a chance to be careful with it, which was the only way anything has ever come out of her that was true.

“No,” she said. “I will not.”

“You have not heard the reason.”

“The reason is on this table,” she said, “and I found it on the fourteenth, and it is the sixth cut, and it says a thing is said in the voice of the person it is about, standing, before it is a line anywhere.”

She put her thumb on the cut to show him where it was, and the dark did not take any notice.

“If I say a name to you in this room then my mouth is the first one it goes in by, and I will have put you in the record as a person I named, and you will spend whatever there is carrying that I did it.”

He did not answer for a long time.

“You found that on the fourteenth and you have been carrying it for nine days,” he said, “and you did not tell me that was why.”

“No,” she said. “I did not think of it until just now.”

## Whether He Believed It

“That is the first thing you have said to me in nine days that I did not know, and you have never done it before, and I am going to sit with it.”

She read the four lines to him and then stood in the dark with her hands on the register and asked the question she had been carrying since the twenty-third.

“Do you believe it.”

“Believe which half.”

“That is not an answer to anything and you know it.”

“It is the answer to the only version of the question that can be asked standing at four in the morning in a dark room,” he said. “You asked me whether I believe it. Not whether I want it. I want it more than anything I have ever had and I have wanted it since I was nineteen. I believe that it will be done.”

“That is not the same as good.”

“No,” he said. “It is not. A man can believe a thing will be done and want it done and still not have the least idea whether it should be done, and that is where I have been for six hundred years and it is where I am now, and you have had the argument with it and the argument was had and it was right and we did not win it.”

“And you still want it.”

“I want it more this morning than I wanted it on the twenty-third,” he said, “and that is the part I would ask you to be careful with, because a thing that gets stronger while the argument against it stays exactly as strong is not a thing that is getting better. It is a thing that is getting closer.”

She stood with that and let it be in the room.

“I have been getting closer to nine stages for six hundred and sixty-nine days,” she said, “and I have never once been stronger at wanting the next one for the reason I am told I want it.”

## What It Cost Him to Let Her

“No,” he said. “You are not like me in that. I have wanted this since I was nineteen and I have never had to climb anything to get to it. That is the whole of the difference between the two of us and I am not going to pretend it is nothing.”

She read the four lines one more time, all of them, standing, in the ordinary voice, in the dark, with the register under her fingers and her face doing the thing again.

Archived. Addressed. Remembered. A name that is not Sheng. Work that matters.

“Work that matters,” she said. “It does not say what the work is.”

“No,” he said. “It does not.”

“You have had six days with those four lines and you have not found out what the work is.”

“I cannot,” he said, “and neither can you, and I am not going to guess at it in front of you, because a man who guesses at the one thing he has been offered for six hundred years and says it out loud to the woman who named the price of everything else is a man who will believe his own guess by morning.”

She almost laughed and did not.

“None of the work is named,” she said. “Not one word of it.”

“Not one word of it,” he said. “And I want you to leave it that way for as long as you can, because you have read five mercies twice and found nothing behind any of them, and the day you start finding what is behind this one is the day this stops being a table and starts being something else, and I do not know what it is yet.”

She stood at the table with her hands on the register and read out loud, in the ordinary voice, standing, in a room with no light in it, the whole of what there was for a man called Sheng on a table at the top of a tower. He did not stop her once.

It was the loneliest thing she had done in nineteen years of keeping a record, because she was the only person in the world who had ever said out loud what he was owed, and she had said it standing, in the ordinary voice, into the dark, to his face.
