# On Time

Four hundred and twenty days since the ninth stage. Two hundred and thirteen since it was shut. Eight hundred and fifty-one days in that room, and sixty-eight days out of one. Eight hundred and fifty-eight days with this arm open at the elbow.

One thousand five hundred and ninety-three days gone, taken out of her at a rate she derived out of her own life, not one of them coming back.

She said all of it standing at the ninth hour on the nineteenth of the month beginning three thousand and one hundred and thirty-four, in the ordinary voice, at the gap in the low wall, first in her own voice before it is a line anywhere, with the road empty and about nine people watching from a long way off.

## Sitting

She had been sitting since about the seventh hour of the sixteenth and it was the nineteenth, and the whole of the three days had gone into learning that there is nothing at all to do with a wait.

She had read the incoming end every morning. She had derived everything she owned twice and got the same answers. She had walked to the far end of the road and back nine times. She had eaten nothing since the first of the month of the market before last and had not needed to and had been aware, three days running, that people were looking at her when she said so.

What she had been waiting for was the walls.

That was the whole of what she had been waiting for.

She had said nine words out in a fog on the thirteenth of the month and she had spent three days finding out whether nine hundred years of a ceiling was still a ceiling. On the second day she worked out that there was exactly one thing in the world that could tell her, and that she could not ask it a question, and that it had never once in nineteen years given her anything it had not been asked for.

## Three Days Of Sitting

The waiting had a texture she had not been in before, and the texture was that every single hour was the same size.

The ninth hour came and went on the seventeenth and the eighteenth and the nineteenth and on each of them she said the six figures in order into the open air out of habit, and on each of them they came out exactly as they had come out every morning since she was ten years old, and on each of them she found that she did not remember doing it until about a count of forty afterwards.

That was new. She had never once not remembered saying a figure.

On the second day she worked out that the reason was that a figure has nothing at stake. She had said numbers into the air every morning since she was ten years old and not one of them had ever cost her anything or told her anything, and she had kept every one of them for nineteen years, and she had believed until about the seventh of the month beginning three thousand and one hundred and thirty-four that keeping them was a kind of strength.

It was a kind of habit, and habits are cheap, and the only expensive thing she had ever said out loud was nine words in a fog.

## The Man With A Yoke

The man with the yoke came past on the second day and stopped, which he did not do.

“You have been sitting in this place three days.”

“Yes.”

He looked at the road for a while.

“I have sat three days once,” he said. “There was a roof and it was my brother’s boy and it was either that or the field. I sat and the field got done anyway, on its own, and by the time the boy was up the boy was up.”

“That is not the same.”

“No,” he said. “But it is the same sitting.”

He went on up the road, and she sat at a gap in a wall and found that somebody in nine counties had understood what she was doing without her having to say a word about it, which was the thing that four hundred people had been doing to her for twenty-nine days and which she had not once let it work.

## About The Fourth Hour

It came up under her through the ground about the fourth hour, one piece at a time, plain, in the grammar the keeping uses for everything it says, and she let the whole of it go past her the way she has let everything from that keeping go past her for nineteen years, which is all of it, without hurrying it.

[The bundle came up nine hundred and four steps on time, and the walls are kept.]

## What It Was About

She sat there at a gap in a wall with her hands open on her knees and read it four times.

The bundle of the first of the month had gone up nine hundred and four steps at the fifth hour on the first and had gone up on time, which was the same as it had gone up every thirty days for as long as that keeping has existed, and which was the plainest and smallest true thing that nine hundred years of a machine had ever said to her.

And the walls are kept.

That was the second half of it and it was there for the same reason the first half was there, which is that it was true and it was what that machine did, and it said it every time it put anything down, and she had heard the shape of it in four plain sentences in the middle of a queue on the fourth of a month.

## What It Was Not About

And then she did the arithmetic of what it had not said, standing, in the ordinary voice, at a gap in a wall, on the nineteenth of a month, and it took her about a count of eight hundred.

It had not said anything about the fog on the thirteenth.

It had not said anything about nine words.

It had not said anything about the four hundred that it had put down without being asked.

It had said a bundle and it had said the walls, and it had said them in the order it always says things in, which is the order they happened in, and a thing it did not put down was a thing that had not happened.

She sat back down on the wall.

## The Part She Could Not Work Out

And there was the thing she could not get past for the rest of that day, and she got it out loud in the evening standing at the crossing with the lamp beside her and about four people within hearing.

She could not tell whether that line had come because the sentence had not gone in, or because it had gone in and nine hundred years of a keeping had gone on keeping the walls anyway.

Both of them were a keeping.

And there was no version of anything she could put on the ground in front of herself that would tell the difference between them, because in nineteen years that machine had given her four plain sentences about the four hundred and one line about a bundle of boards, and not one word in nine hundred years had ever been about anything she had done.

She said that out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice, to a crossing where two roads meet, and it was the loneliest sentence she has ever put into a keeping.

“The one report I have ever been given that I can use is about a bundle going up a stair, and I am the only person in the world who will ever know that I said anything.”

## Eight Leaves

At the ninth hour she took the book out from against her fourth rib and opened it on her knee in the road, with the lamp on the stones beside her and the rain starting again out of the west.

Eight leaves. Five written on, and three that had been blank since the seventh of the month beginning two thousand seven hundred and forty-four and still were, and the newest of the five a line in charcoal she had put there herself fifty-six days ago in the rain and had never read back.

She looked at the three that were still blank.

Not one line of what she had said in the fog was going to go on any of them, and she had known that on the fourth of the month with a stub of charcoal in her hand and had made the decision out loud and had then not said the decision out loud, which is the only thing in nineteen years she has caught herself not doing.

She put the book back against her fourth rib and shut it, and there was not one person in nine hundred years who would ever open it.

And then she said the last of it out loud, standing, at the gap in the wall, on the nineteenth of the month beginning three thousand and one hundred and thirty-four, with the rain coming off the west and the lamp beside her on the stones and about four people in that road within hearing who had no idea at all what she was saying.

“Nine words in a fog on the thirteenth. A bundle going up a stair this afternoon. Those are the two facts of my life at this moment and one of them is about a bundle.”

She shut her mouth and sat back down on the wall.

And then, because she could not help it, because she has not been able to leave a thing alone once in nineteen years when it was on the table in front of her, she started working on what she would say to the four hundred in the plainest words she had.
