# The Old Terrace

The boy of about seventeen came down off the pass before the light with his boots full of clay and one sleeve gone at the shoulder, and he had walked from the quarry above the flat, which takes most of a night.

She was in the gap in the low wall. She had been there since the second hour because the rain had come back on the ninth and she was not sleeping, and the lamp was standing on the wall outside the gap where she had put it down on the thirtieth and where it had stayed.

He did not stop at the gap. He came the last ten paces and put both hands on the stone on either side of her and got his breath.

“How many,” he said.

That was the first question, and it was the right one, and she had not asked it.

“Tell me what happened first,” Ning Yan said. “Then how many. In that order, because the first one is the one I cannot do anything about.”

## What He Had Seen

The terrace at the quarry above the flat is cut stone, nine courses high and about forty paces long, and it is older than anything else in the flat that anybody has a name for.

He said that it moved.

“Which part of it.”

“The top. The two top courses went down and out as far as a thumb in one go, and the dust came off it, and then it stopped moving and it has sat where it sat all morning with a gap in it a thumb wide at the top. My uncle said it would hold.”

“Under the gap.”

“No.”

“Are you certain of that.”

“I was standing on the second course,” he said, “and the top two went, and I was not under anything, and there were about forty of us on the whole of it and not one of us was under the top two when it went.”

She stood there with one hand on the stone and worked out, standing, in the ordinary voice, that the whole of it came to one fact and she had it in about a count of a hundred.

Not one person was under it. There was no argument about that and there was no version of the morning in which the thing about the boy with the stone under his leg had been the whole of it.

## The Four More

She asked him four more before she went up and she asked them fast, and the answers were all short.

Who was on it. About forty. He could put names to about nine and the rest were the quarry’s own hands and four that had come up from the flat at the end of the week.

Whether anybody was under it. No. There were cellars under the bottom course and the cellars were empty, because the quarry had moved the firing out past them in his grandfather’s time.

Whether it had moved again. Once, at about the fourth hour, and it had not gone down any further after that.

Whether anybody was hurt. He stopped on that one and she let him.

“One,” he said. “A boy got his foot in the gap and it came shut. He has got it out. He is sitting on the bottom course with it up on a stone.”

And then she asked the fifth one, which was the one she had spent a month at a gap in a wall learning to ask.

“What else is on it that a person would be sorry to lose.”

## The First Of A Month

It was the first of a month and she went up at about the fourth hour.

It took her about a count of nine hundred, and it took her that long because the track up from the flat goes round the outside of the cut and comes in at the top, and she could not take the short way with that arm.

She had been braced for the morning since she was ten years old and the whole of that bracing went nowhere.

She noticed that standing at the foot of the track and she did not go back to it. Eighty days out of a room was the only figure the morning had, and she said that one out loud going up and left the rest of the column on the ground where it had been.

## Going Up It

Forty-one people on the terrace when she got to the top of the track.

There was a kiln at the foot of the cut with a firing in it, and a row of pots under a lean-to, and a marker standing on the top course.

That was the part she had not expected. A pillar of the same stone, three times a man’s height, with nothing cut on it anywhere on it, the face worn the way a face wears when hands have gone down it for a very long time.

She had seen one in the pass at about thirty years old and had not asked what it was. She looked at this one for about a count of two hundred, and then she went and got people off her terrace, because that was what the morning was for.

## Getting Them Off

She could not be given anything and she could not be paid and she could not be sent, and there was not one person in the flat with any authority she could borrow for the length of a day, so the whole of what she had was her own body and her mouth in one place.

She put herself on the top course and said it standing, in the ordinary voice, in the flat ordinary way a person says a thing to forty people on a hillside.

“There was a gap at the top and the stone went down at the top. I was not able to tell anybody why. What I had for it was this morning at about the fourth hour: about forty of us on it, not one of us under the two courses that went, and a boy who got his foot shut and got it out again. That was the whole of it. It was not enough and I put it out before anything else.”

They came off. Not all at once and not at once at all. Some of them wanted to know what the gap was doing and she could not tell them. Some of them wanted to know whether it would come down further and she could not tell them that either, and she said so both times in the same words.

## What She Did With Her Hands

She carried four.

Her right arm has been open at the elbow for eight hundred and seventy days and does not do anything, and she has known for six hundred and something of those days exactly what that means in a place with steps in it, which is that she can hold a child on her left side and go down backwards one tread at a time and that is the whole of what she has.

She carried a girl of about four down and came back up. She carried a girl of about six down and came back up. She carried a boy of about nine who could walk perfectly well and made her put him down halfway. She carried an old man of about eighty down in the ordinary way with his arm across her shoulders and his feet finding the treads.

Her breath went to nine on the second one and stayed at nine, and nine is what her body does when it is working, and she has never once been able to make it do anything else.

## The Woman And The Firing

The kiln-man’s wife would not come off.

She stood at the lean-to with her arms round four hundred pots that had been stacked to dry for a day and a night, and she said the whole of it in one sentence, and would not listen to the answer to any part of it.

“I have done this for nine years and there is one firing in it, and if it goes I am finished. You can carry the little ones and you can carry your old man. I was not standing up here for a thumb’s width.”

Ning Yan stood in front of her for about a count of four hundred.

She could not make her leave. She could not be paid for it. There is no office in nine counties that can tell that woman what her nine years were worth. So she did the only thing she had, which was to put it out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice, in front of about thirty people.

“Four hundred pots,” she said. “One firing. Nine years. I had nothing to put against any of that and I was not going to pretend I had it. I was going to carry the small ones down and the old one down, and then I was going to come back up here and stand where you were standing, and if the stone went while I was standing on it then that was a thing I had decided about and not a thing you had.”

The kiln-man’s wife looked at her for a long time.

“You have got one arm,” she said.

“I have.”

“Then you had better not go up it again.”

“I had to go up it again,” Ning Yan said. “There was one behind me on the fourth course and he was seventy-one.”

## The Order Of It

She got the last one off at about the eighth hour.

He was a man of about seventy-one who had been asleep on the second course in the shade of the marker and had heard none of it and was not pleased about any of it. She carried him down on her left side and he put his hand flat on the marker as they came past, and she let him.

Then she went back up and stood on the top course by herself.

The gap ran the whole length of the terrace, a thumb wide, and the two courses above it had dropped about as far as a thumb and no further, and the faces of the stones where they had come apart were clean.

That was not what she stopped for.

She stopped for the dust. There was no dust at all on the lower courses, and a hand’s breadth of it on the second, and none on the third, and none on the fourth, and none on any of the five below that.

She worked out, standing at the top of a stone staircase on her own, that the terrace had come apart from the top down. Every step of it, clean, in order, the way a man takes stones off a stack he built himself. Nothing out of place. Nothing shoved.

And there is no reason that stone has ever been given for letting go at the top before it lets go at the bottom.

She put her palm flat on the top course. It was warm under her hand. The day was cold and wet and had been since before the light, and nine hundred years of that stone had never once been warm.

That one did not get said out loud either. She had told four hundred people on a road that she does not put a thing into a keeping she cannot derive, and she was not going to start making an exception for a warm stone on a hillside at the eighth hour.

So she came down off it. Forty-one people, one firing, and a thumb’s width of gap.

And the sun came up about half an hour later on the first of a month, and there was nothing at all on any stair anywhere in the world.
