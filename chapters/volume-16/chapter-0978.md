# Where The Wall Was

The only way to find out how far it went was to walk it, and she could not take anybody with her, and the wall is a long thing to walk alone.

She had known that at about the fourth hour of the second day, sitting on the wall at the flat with the terrace sitting four courses low behind her, and she had known it was coming and she had not known what to do about it and there was not one thing she could do about it.

## Getting Out Of The Flat

She started at about the fifth hour of the third day with a basket, because a basket is the only excuse a person can carry for walking in a straight line all day and she had wanted somebody to ask her about it.

Not one person asked her about it. Not one person in the flat had asked her about anything since the ninth hour of the second day except the kiln-man’s wife, who came out into the road at the fourth hour with a pot in each hand and put them down in front of her and said, “That is four hundred of them and one of them is whole.”

She looked at the two pots for a moment and then she picked them up, because there was not one other place for them to go and because refusing a thing like that out loud in the ordinary voice would have taken her the whole morning.

The wall begins about two hours out of the flat on the made road and it runs with the road for as far as the road runs, and after that it goes off across country that has no road on it at all.

## The Line Of It

She walked it in the ordinary way, the way she counts things, which is with the number of her own paces and with a figure she derives out of her own life, and which is a thing she has done since she was fourteen years old and has never once had to check against anybody else’s number.

The wall was stone and it was about as high as her chest at the road and higher at the bends, and it went along the top of a bank for most of the first two hours, and every few hundred paces there was a gap in it with two posts in it.

The posts were about twice the height of a man and were the same stone as the marker on the quarry terrace, and there was nothing cut on any of them.

She counted nine gaps in the two hours.

That was the whole of what the first two hours were, and it was the whole of what she had, and at the third hour of the afternoon she came to a place where the bank stopped.

## Where It Stopped Being There

The bank did not stop.

The ground stopped.

The wall went along the top of that bank for about the length of a long cart and then the bank went down into flat grass the way a bank does when a river has been at it, and there was no wall, and there was no bank, and the ground on the far side was the same grass and the same colour and there was a thorn in it.

She went along where it would have gone for about the length of two carts, looking at the grass.

There was nothing in the grass. Not one stone, not one post, not a footing, not a scrap of mortar, and the ground had not been walked on, and the grass was the age of the grass on either side of it, which is the thing that put her down on her knees in it.

It was not rubble. Rubble is what a thing leaves when it comes down.

Somebody had taken about a day’s walking out of the world and had not left the stones anywhere.

## Two Hours Looking For Where It Began

She spent two hours trying to find where the missing ground began.

She went back west along what the wall would have gone and she went along it again on the other side of the grass, and she kept coming back to the same flat ordinary stretch of field with the thorn in it, and there was no edge to it anywhere that a person would recognise, and a person who had not been there on the day before could not have said that anything had ever stood in that field.

That was the part that took the two hours. Rubble tells you. A scar in a bank tells you. A place where a wall has been for nine hundred years has something left of itself, the way a path does, and this had not one thing left of itself, and she could not find where it started because there was no starting.

She sat down in it at about the fifth hour.

She had an instrument and it was a good one. She had been counting paces against her own arm since she was fourteen years old and she had never once been wrong about one in front of her, and she put it into that field and it returned her a figure, and the figure was perfectly good, and there was not one thing on either side of it for the figure to be about.

## What A Person Asks For

A woman came out from the far side of where the wall had been at about the fifth hour, coming through the place where it was not, and she was about thirty and she was crying in the ordinary way that people cry when they have been walking.

She came up to Ning Yan and she said, “Is this the way.”

“Yes.”

“My boy is on the far side of it. He is at a place with a wall round it and there is a door in the wall and the door is open and there is not one person standing at the door.”

She said that last part twice, in the same words, the way a person says a thing they have been saying to themselves for two days.

Ning Yan stood in the grass on the side of the wall that was under her feet that morning and worked out, standing, in the ordinary voice, in front of a woman of about thirty who had walked out through a place that was not there.

What she could say was: there is no door being kept. Not this morning. Not anywhere she had walked.

What she could not say was how she knew it for every door in nine counties, and she had walked one line of it for a day, and the line had a place cut out of the middle of it.

And what she could not say at all was what had happened to the wall, or whether it was coming back, or whether the boy was safe, or whether the open door meant something good.

She said four true things out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice, in the grass.

“The door was open. There was not one person standing at it. I had not seen a man in a door anywhere on the line I walked yesterday. That was all of what I had, and I was not able to add to it.”

The woman stood there.

“You have got less than I had,” she said.

“I was sorry.”

“I did not mean it like that.” She wiped her face with the back of her hand. “I meant, before you said it I had four things and now I have four things that somebody else has looked at.”

And she went off east across the grass, and Ning Yan stood in the place where a wall had been and let that go past her too.

## A Gate With Nothing On Either Side

The gate is at the end of the line.

She got there at the last of the light and it was standing, and it was a gate in the sense that it was two piers and a beam and a leaf of black wood that had been hung and oiled and had been shut a great many times.

There was no wall on either side of it.

Not a short bit of it. She stood in the road and looked both ways until the light was off the stones, and she could not see the end of what was not there in either direction, and she had walked the whole of the rest of the line in the ten hours behind her and she had found nine posts and one gate and one day of missing ground and no other end to it.

She had a number of paces in her. She had been counting since the fifth hour and she could have put the figure down out loud in about a count of six hundred.

There was nothing to put it against. That was the part she stood there with. A number of paces is a real thing and it is the only instrument she has ever had, and a number of paces against what, against a thing she cannot name, in a country where there was nothing left standing anywhere that a figure would go into.

So she did not say it, and there was not one person there to hear her not say it.

## What She Went Back With

She went back to the flat up the made road in the dark with two whole pots in a basket and about four hundred paces in her head and nothing at all to put them in.

Somewhere in the middle of the second hour she worked out, walking, that this was the first time in nineteen years that she had measured a thing and been unable to say what she had measured.

And then she worked out the other half of it, walking, which was worse, and which was that this was the first time in nineteen years that she had needed to say a number to somebody and had not had one.

There were three hundred and ninety-nine people in nine counties who had said yes to three sentences about oil on a road, and she had no list, and no office, and no message, and no way of following a name she was given, and she could not be met where she was expected and could not be given anything and could not be paid.

And on the morrow she was going to walk through a gate into a part of the world where she had never been, on her own, with a basket, to look for them.

She got back into the flat at about the third hour and the kiln-man’s wife was waiting at the crossing with a lamp, and did not ask her a single question about where she had been, which was the single most useful thing anybody did for her that week.
