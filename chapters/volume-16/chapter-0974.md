# The Gap In The Wall

She went back up the road to the crossing at about the sixth hour on the twenty-seventh of the month beginning three thousand and one hundred and thirty-four and stood in the gap in the low wall where she had stood on the nineteenth of the month beginning three thousand and one hundred and four and put four hundred people into a record.

It was the same gap. Somebody had taken a stone out of that wall a long time before she was born and had not put it back, and the mortar had gone soft around it, and about a foot of the road went through there and not one living soul had ever bothered to close it, because nothing anybody needed went that way.

She stood in it for about a count of a hundred and looked at it as a place to stand in rather than as a place to have stood in, which was a thing she had never done with any of the places she had been.

## Working Out Where People Would Stand

That was the whole of what she did with the morning.

If about nine of them came, they would not stand in a half circle because there is no one to put them in a half circle and she was not going to put them there. They would stand where they stood, which is what they had done every time she had said three sentences to them for twenty-nine days, in a line going back up the road because the road was the only thing there was.

So they would be behind her. Along the road. In the rain.

And she would be in the gap, facing back up the road, with the wall on both sides of her and about nine hundred steps of made road going away behind those nine people into the pass, and there is nothing at all in that to stand in front of.

That was fine. She had said a number out loud to an empty road for six thousand mornings.

What she could not get past was that the ninth hour is the hour the keeping reads, and it had been hers since she was ten years old, and she had never once done anything at the ninth hour that was for anybody else.

## Four Attempts

She tried it four times between the seventh hour and the eleventh, standing, in the ordinary voice, in the gap, in the rain.

The first one came out clean and she wrote nothing down because there was nothing to write on.

The second one she came in at the fifth word again, straight past the two words in front of it, and got to *I was told* inside about half a second, and the whole of it tipped over onto a different meaning, and she heard it happen too late to do anything about it.

The third one she got to the sixth word and stopped, because she had noticed herself about to say it slowly.

The fourth one came out in the ordinary voice at ordinary speed and she could not afterwards have told anybody which of the four she had just done, and that is the whole of what is wrong with an instrument that does not take a rehearsal.

## The Fifth Word

She went after the fifth word for about a count of six hundred, standing, in the gap, in the rain.

It was not a forgetting. She had nine words and she could say all nine of them in order and she had said them to a fog and to a carter and to a road and she had never once lost the shape.

It was that she had believed the other thing for twelve years and the mouth is deeper than the arithmetic.

That was the finding and it was the last thing in nineteen years that had ever surprised her. She had assumed for nineteen years that the instrument was the arithmetic, and that the mouth only ever carried what the arithmetic had already checked, and she had built her whole life on that, and it was the other way round, and the proof was one word in four attempts on the twenty-seventh of a month in the rain.

She said it out loud, standing, to the gap in a wall.

“Twelve years. I have had the truth in a book against my ribs for nineteen years and it took the front two words falling out of my mouth to prove which one of us has been in charge.”

## Going To Ask People To Come

And then, at about the second hour of the afternoon, she went and asked people to be there.

That was harder than any of the four attempts and she did it badly the first two times.

She went to Kan Shou at the stall and said the day and the hour and the place, standing, in the ordinary voice, and Kan Shou said, “I have not decided to come.”

“You do not have to decide. I am telling you where I am going to be and what is going to happen there and I have told you the price is on the record and that I do not know what it costs me and what it costs you. You can stand somewhere else.”

She went to He Yu at the flat and said the same three parts and He Yu said, “Will it be on the record.”

“Yes.”

“Then I will be there.”

She went to the carter with the broken shaft and he said the thirtieth was market day and there would be people about and that was the answer.

She went to Guo Sheng and told him and he listened to the whole of it and then said, “You have not asked me to come.”

“No. I have told you where I am going to be.”

“That is worse,” Guo Sheng said, “and I am going to be there anyway, and I want you to know that I noticed.”

## The Woman Of About Sixty

The woman who had wanted to know about her son was sitting on the wall where she always sat and Ning Yan sat down about four feet off her, on the same wall, which she had not done since the twentieth of the month beginning three thousand and seventy-four.

“I am going to say a sentence on the thirtieth at the ninth hour at the crossing.”

“I heard it on the seventh,” the woman said. “From a stall.”

“Yes.”

She was quiet for a while.

“My son is in a sect in a county I cannot name,” she said. “He went at fifteen and he was at nine in his twentieth year and they wrote to say so. That was a year ago and it is the last I have had.”

“Yes.”

“If the ceiling comes off,” she said, “is he still at nine.”

Ning Yan sat on a wall four feet off a woman of about sixty and worked out the whole of it standing up in the inside of herself, and it took her about a count of forty, and there was not one figure in it she had ever held before.

“I do not know,” she said. “I do not know whether he is still at nine and I do not know what happens to a person at nine when the thing that was holding him there is not there any more, and I have never been anywhere near enough to it to find out, and I am telling you that on a wall on the twenty-seventh of a month because I am going to say it again on the thirtieth and I would rather you had it twice.”

The woman looked at the road for a while.

“Then say it on the thirtieth,” she said, and then, after a while: “I will be there.”

## The Four Attempts Again

She went back to the gap after the people had gone and tried it four more times between the second hour of the afternoon and the light going, because it had been five hours since the last one and she could not stand not knowing.

The fifth attempt was clean.

The sixth one came in at the fifth word again.

The seventh one she said nine words and then stood there and found she could not remember having started, which had never happened to her before with anything, including the count.

The eighth one she stopped halfway through, deliberately, and stood with her mouth open in the rain at a gap in a wall on purpose.

And she worked out, standing, with her mouth open, that what she was frightened of was not that she would get it wrong on the thirtieth.

It was that on the thirtieth, in front of eight people, she would get it right, and there would be nothing at all in the world left to stand between her and having done it.

## The Long Afternoon Of The Twenty-Seventh

She did not sleep on the twenty-seventh after that and there was a long stretch of the day left with nothing in it.

She had spent the morning on four attempts and the middle of the day on five people to ask and a woman on a wall, and all of that had been work, and after about the seventh hour there was no work left in the twenty-seventh of a month at all.

She said the figures at the ninth hour standing in the gap in the ordinary voice, in order, because that is what the ninth hour is for and she was not going to be a woman who quietly stopped doing that in the last week of the only life she had.

Eight hundred and fifty-nine days in that room. Eight hundred and sixty-six days with the arm open at the elbow.

And then she said, standing, in the ordinary voice, out loud, to a gap in a wall on the twenty-seventh of a month:

“Eight hundred and fifty-nine. Eight hundred and sixty-six. And on the thirtieth I am going to open my mouth, and either the grammar is in the mouth or the arithmetic is, and it turns out it is the mouth, and I have known the arithmetic for nineteen years and I am going to find out about the mouth in about three days.”

## Three Days

She went back to the crossing about the seventh hour and stood in the gap again, in the rain, with the lamp in her left hand.

She had a place and an hour and about nine people who had said they would be there and three hundred and ninety-one who had not been asked and nine words she could not say the same way twice.

And the thing she had come back to the gap to work out was not the place and it was not the hour. It was that she had never in nineteen years said anything out loud that she was going to be held to afterwards, and there is no instrument on this road that can tell her whether she will get it right, and she has three days, and she is not going to sleep.
