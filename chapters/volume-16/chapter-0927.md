# Through Her

She asked for it at the seventh hour on the nineteenth of the month beginning three thousand and fourteen and she asked for it the way she had asked for everything since the seventh of the month before last, standing, in the ordinary voice.

“I told you on the sixteenth that you were going to tell me what it has cost you.”

“I did not say no.”

“You said no to saying it tonight.”

“I said no to saying it tonight,” he said. “That is a different thing and you know the difference between the two better than anybody in this world, because you have refused things in this room for nine years that were smaller than that.”

She stood at the near end of her own table with the lamp on the stone.

“Say it.”

He was quiet for a count of a hundred.

“I cannot,” he said.

## Why Not

“Why can you not.”

“Because when I say a thing that goes out, it goes out of your mouth.”

She stood very still.

“You told me that on the fourth of the month before last,” she said. “You told me it was the one thing you could not get past. The channel runs through me and a thing said in it comes out of me and it goes into the keeping in my voice.”

“Yes.”

“Then say it anyway.”

“That is what I have been doing for six hundred years,” he said. “Every sentence I have ever had in this keeping has come out of a woman and gone past her into the traffic, and she has heard every one of them and has never once heard me say one of them in my own mouth, and I did not know that was true until about two hours after you asked me and I am not going to pretend I knew it before.”

“Does the keeping hear you,” she said. “When you speak to me. Does it hear that.”

He was quiet for a count of forty.

“No,” he said.

“No,” she said. “Say that part properly, because I have been carrying a piece of the answer to it since I was ten years old and I have never once been able to use it.”

“The keeping hears what leaves you,” he said.

“A thing said in this room in the ordinary voice goes out of you and down. A thing I say to you goes no further than you, because the channel is not a mouth and I do not have one, and for nineteen years every single word I have said to you has stayed in your head and gone no further, and you have been the only wall between me and the whole of the bound, and you have never known you were doing it.”

She stood very still.

“Then when I speak for you,” she said, “the keeping hears it.”

“The keeping hears it,” he said, “and it hears it in your voice, and it cannot tell where it came from, and that is the whole of what I am and the whole of what you have been.”

She put her hands flat on the wood.

“The seventh,” she said.

“The seventh.”

“I felt it leave.”

“You did,” he said. “One word, out of a man, through a woman, into the keeping. You have that written somewhere and you have never once said it to me and I have never asked.”

“I have not told you that.”

“No.”

She stood there and looked at the worn hollow under her own forearms.

“And on the twenty-second,” she said. “Tired. That came out of me too.”

“Yes.”

“And the thirtieth.”

“Yes,” he said. “The true not-knowing came out of you in the ordinary voice at the tenth hour and it is in the record in a woman’s mouth, and you have been hearing me speak at you for three weeks and every word of it has been yours.”

## What He Has Never Said

He was quiet for a count of forty.

She put her hand flat on the table and asked him a question, because she had three days of it and she was going to spend them.

“Are you afraid.”

“No.”

“Say it in the ordinary voice,” she said, “because you have told me twice today that you cannot, and I want to hear a man try.”

He did not try.

He was quiet for a count of forty.

“You said on the sixteenth that you were giving me three days because I had not asked you for a thing in nine years,” he said.

“I want you to know that it is not that I have nothing to say. It is that I have one thing to say and I have never once said it, and I have had six hundred years to work out how to get it out of me and there is no way, and you are standing in a room asking me to do it standing up in the ordinary voice.”

“Yes.”

“I am going to do it,” he said. “I am going to do it from inside. Not in the channel. From inside, where you are, and it will come out of your mouth and it will be my words and you will hear me in the middle of your own sentence, and I am telling you what is going to happen before it happens because I promised you on the sixteenth that you would not have to find out about it by being surprised by it.”

She stood at the near end of the table with nothing in either hand.

“Go on.”

## One Sentence, Once

He was quiet for a long time.

Then it came, and it came out of her.

It came out of her mouth at the seventh hour on the nineteenth of the month beginning three thousand and fourteen in a room where a lamp stood on a stone floor and the wood under her forearms had been worn down over nineteen years, and it was in the ordinary voice, and it was not a question, and he was in the middle of it.

“I am a voice. I am not a person and I have not been one since the nineteenth year of a life that ended. I have no hands and no eyes and I cannot close a door in my own half.

“When I speak I go out of your mouth and I do not hear it come back and I have not heard it come back once in six hundred years. That is the whole of what it costs me and I have never put a figure on it because I do not know what I would be measuring.”

She stood there with her mouth shut and her hands flat on the wood and she could hear the difference between every other thing he had ever said to her and this one, because this one had gone through a wall.

It was not louder. It was further off and it had a place behind it, the way a thing sounds when it is said in a large room by a man standing at the far side of it, and that was the first time in nine years that anything in her head had had a size.

He said it once.

## What It Cost Her To Hear It

She did not say anything for a count of a hundred.

“You said it,” she said. “And it came out of my mouth.”

“Yes.”

“And it is in the record in my voice about you.”

“It is not about me.”

“It is about you and it is in my mouth,” she said, “and that is the difference and I am not going to pretend otherwise to make it easier for either of us.”

He was quiet for a count of two hundred.

“Yes,” he said. “That is the shape of it and it has been the shape of it for six hundred years. You asked me for a thing in my own voice and there is no such voice in this keeping, and the closest there has ever been is your mouth, and the only reason it is the closest is that it is not mine.”

She stood at the near end of her own table with a lamp burning at her feet and the second place at the far side kept four hundred years and empty.

He said no more. She had asked him for one and he had given her one and he was not going to give her a second one out of the same place, and she understood that she was now holding the only sentence of his that she would ever be able to check, because she would not have heard it if it had been different.

“So I have it,” she said. “Accurate. In my voice. Six hundred years of it, once, standing up, on the nineteenth of a month. And there is nothing whatever I can do with it.”

“No.”

She put her hands flat on the worn wood.

“Then I am going to say one thing about the mouth first,” she said, “and then I am going to tell you what I think, and it is not going to be an argument, because we had the argument on the twenty-ninth of the month beginning two thousand nine hundred and fifty-four and it was had and it was right, and neither of us is going back to it.”

“Say the first thing.”

“There is a term for it,” she said. “I read it standing on the twenty-third of the month beginning two thousand nine hundred and fifty-four and I read it again in my own room on the twenty-fifth of the month before last, and it is on that register, and it is one word, and it is the whole of what you have been asking me for since the eighth.”

He was quiet for a count of a hundred.

“Say the word.”

“No,” she said. “You can say it. You have been in my head for nine years and you know every word of that table better than I do and you have never once said it out loud.

“And if you say it in the next count of a hundred then a thing that has been sitting in this keeping for four hundred years will have been said by the only person alive who is in a position to say it, and I am not going to do that for you. You do it or it does not get done.”

He did not say it.

“Say nothing for a count of a hundred,” she said, “and then I am going to tell you what I think.”
