# Before It Left Her Mouth

She spent the twenty-second of the month beginning three thousand and fourteen trying to decide.

That was the whole of the morning and most of the afternoon and she did it at the near end of her own table with a lamp on the stone and the boards face up a foot in from the near end and the second place at the far side kept four hundred years and empty, and she did not get it.

It is not a thing a person can do without wanting. That is the part not one person in the world ever puts in a record. She had been a person who wanted things for nineteen years and every single one of them had been put in front of her by somebody else and then taken away, and on the sixteenth of the month before last a man had asked her the question and she had opened her mouth and there had been nothing in it.

She had had six days since the fifteenth and thirty-six days since the seventh. She wanted something and she could not find where she had put it.

She took the near board up off the face of the table about the second hour and read it and put it back face up, and she took the terms board up and read it and put it back, and she went the length of the table and put her hand on the bundle under the far end where it was corded twice and not knotted, and she stood at the boards with her hand flat on the near one for a count of four hundred without lifting it.

## What He Offered Her

“You do not have to find it,” he said.

She did not look up.

“I have wanted you to tell me what to do since the eighth of the month before last,” he said, “and I have never once asked you, and I am asking now. Say the word and I will take it as settled and I will not argue a figure with you about it for the rest of the keeping.”

She stood at the near end of the table with her hands flat on the wood.

“You want me to decide it.”

“I want you to be the one who decides it,” he said. “That is a different thing and it is worse for me and better for you, and you have four hours of it in front of you and you are not going to spend them looking, because looking is not the thing that has been wrong with every decision you have made since you were ten.”

He was quiet for a count of forty.

“Say it however you want it said,” he said. “I will take it.”

## Three Words

She stood up straight.

“You should take it.”

It came out of her mouth about a count of two words ahead of her and it went out of her the way a sentence of hers goes out into the traffic, one piece at a time, plain, except that this one had not gone into the traffic. That one was going to be a man’s decision made in a woman’s mouth, on the twenty-second of the month beginning three thousand and fourteen, at about the seventh hour, in a room with a lamp on the stone floor.

The shape of it was the shape of a sheet of her own hand on the seventh of the month beginning two thousand seven hundred and forty-four, going nine hundred and four steps in a cloth with forty boards on the top of it, to a bound where four hundred people had to live under it.

She was aware of her own hands on the table the entire time, and she did not put them in the middle of it, and she had nine years of a habit about not doing that.

She got her mouth open again.

“Stop,” she said. “That one is not mine to say.”

She heard herself say it and she heard the sentence stop about a count of four words past where she had meant it to stop, which is what a thing does when it has been going for nine years and is not a new thing.

“All right,” he said.

## The Catch

He was quiet.

“No,” she said. “I am not stopping anything. You did not ask me to say it and I said it anyway, and it was out of my mouth, and it was the exact same thing as the refusal I wrote on a sheet on the seventh of the month beginning two thousand seven hundred and forty-four and sent down nine hundred and four steps without asking one living soul.

“And I spent the eighth of the month before last calling that a precedent, because it is a precedent and I do not have one right answer about it to this day.”

She put both hands flat on the wood.

“I caught it,” she said. “I caught it before it was a line anywhere and I am telling you that I caught it and I am not making a thing of it and I am also not pretending it did not happen.”

## What He Said About It

“Do you know what you just did,” he said.

“No.”

“You decided on my behalf and then you took it back inside the same count of breath,” he said, “and that is the first time in six hundred years that anything has been done to me and undone in the same breath, and I do not know what to do with it and I am not going to pretend I am comfortable.”

She went and stood at the boards with her hand flat on the near one and left it there for a count of two hundred, and then she came back.

He was quiet for a count of forty, and then the temperature in the room changed.

“No,” he said. “I am going to say the uncomfortable part, because you have been carrying the comfortable part for nine years and I have watched you do it.

“You are choosing the hard way. You know what the hard way is. It is me in a room that cannot be got into, on my own, with the whole of a thing I have wanted for six hundred years in my hands, and no one in the world able to help me and not one person to come and stand at the table.

“That is what you have chosen, and you have chosen it because it is clean, and it is clean because it is yours, and it is not mine.”

“Yes,” she said.

## What She Had Caught Herself From

“Say the rest of it,” she said.

“There is no rest of it. You caught yourself and I have said the rest and we can stop.”

“No,” she said. “You have one thing left and I am going to have it. You decided before I opened my mouth this morning.”

He was quiet for a count of a hundred.

“I decided on the twenty-second of the month before last,” he said. “That is twenty-four days ago, and you have spent three of them asking me what the work is and three more standing up at that table trying not to make a decision for a man who had already made his own.

“At about the tenth hour, when I gave you the true not-knowing without being asked. I said then that I was not tired any more and that I had something else. That was the decision. Everything since has been a man walking toward a thing he has already picked up.”

She stood at the near end of the table with her hands flat on the wood and did not say anything for a long time.

“So I spent a whole day catching myself from a thing you had already put down,” she said. “On the twenty-second, with the figures going out at the ninth hour, in the ordinary voice.

“I stood up here and I decided not to decide for you and I have been carrying that since the fifth hour this morning, and it was worth nothing, because the thing I was protecting you from had already been settled by you at the tenth hour on the twenty-second, before I asked you a single question about it.”

“Yes.”

## The Twenty-Seventh

She put her hand flat on the table where the wood had gone down under her own arms over nineteen years, and she went over the three words twice and found that she was not angry about them.

That was the part. She was not angry because the three words had been true, and because a true thing said at the wrong hour by the right person in a room where it cannot be taken back is still a thing said, and there was no putting it in the record either, because she had caught it before it was a line anywhere, and that had been the whole of the catching and it was not enough to make it any smaller than what it was.

“Tell me one thing before then,” she said.

“Which thing.”

“Whether you want me to stop you.”

He was quiet for a long time.

“No,” he said. “I never wanted that. What I wanted on the eighth was a witness, and I told you that on the first of this month while forty boards went up nine hundred and four steps and I did not want you to stop me and I do not want you to stop me now. What I want is for you to be in the room when I say it.”

“The twenty-seventh,” she said.

“The twenty-seventh,” he said. “Say that back.”

“The twenty-seventh of the month beginning three thousand and fourteen,” she said, “standing, in the ordinary voice, in the channel, before it is a line anywhere.”

“Yes,” he said. “That is the rule and I have never broken it and I am not going to start on this.”
