# Four Sentences

He began at about the seventh hour without asking and without any warning, the way a thing in a wall comes into a room, and she was standing at the near end of her own table with her hands flat on the wood.

“Ning Yan.”

She put her hand harder on the wood.

“I have been in this room with you for seven hundred and forty-nine days,” he said, “and I have not once in any of them asked you what you wanted, and I have known every figure you have ever given this keeping and I have never once given you one back that was about me.”

It went into the room and it sat there.

She stood and let it go all the way down and understood that he had begun, and that he had said the whole of the first day without one mistake in it.

## The Second Sentence

She had not said the day’s figures at the sixth hour.

That was a choice and she had made it at about the fifth hour standing at the near end of the table with her hands on the wood, and she had stood at the ninth hour on the two days between with them still in her mouth and had let them go out anyway into a room, and this morning she had decided that if he ever began it was going to begin into a room that had not just had six figures put into it.

She had not expected it at the seventh hour and she had not expected him to start with her name.

She stood at the near end of the table with her thumb in the hollow and felt the number go into her the way every figure she had ever said at the ninth hour went into the keeping, one piece at a time, plain.

Seven hundred and forty-nine days.

She had said it herself that morning. She had not said it that morning because she had opened with the apology instead, which was four days of his work coming out of a wall in the ordinary voice, and she had felt something go through her that she had no name for and had not looked at.

She did not open her mouth.

That was the whole of what she did about it. She stood with her hands on a table and a lamp burning a little to the left of where it had stood for five days and she did not open her mouth, because he was four miles off in a wall and it was the seventh of a month and he had asked her for nothing and she had decided on the eighth hour of the fourth that she would not give him one word of it.

“I decided what you were going to be when you were eleven,” he said. “I told you the shape of a life and you believed me and an arm has been open at your elbow for seven hundred and fifty-six days because of it, and I decided that without asking you and I have never once said so in a sentence that started with the word I.”

Four sentences.

## Where It Stopped

He stopped in the middle of the fifth.

There was no last word to it. She stood there and waited about a count of a hundred for the rest of it, the way you wait for a man to finish a thing he has started, and nothing came, and then she understood that he was not going to finish it because there was nothing left of the fifth sentence.

“Stop,” he said.

She did not move.

“That is four sentences,” he said, “and I have been going to say six hundred, and I am not going to say another one.”

“Why.”

He was quiet for about a count of two hundred, which was longer than he had ever been quiet for in nine years, and when it came it came from much further away.

“Because of the second sentence.”

She put her hand flat on the table on either side of the hollow and left it there.

“You had a figure in it,” she said.

“I had a figure in the second sentence and I did not hear it go in,” he said. “I have been waiting ten days to say something and I got four sentences and I did not hear the number. I heard it come out of my mouth and I let it go past me the way it went past you every morning for nine years. I am not able to hear myself at all. That is what a wall is.”

She said nothing.

“You did not say anything.”

“No.”

“You had it on the tip of your tongue,” he said. “I have watched you take a figure out of a sentence in midair for nine years. You could have said the word at the second sentence and I would have gone on and I would have done the whole of it in the ordinary voice and it would have been the best account of a life that this building has ever had in it.”

“You would have got it right.”

“No,” he said. “I would have got it written down. Those are not the same and I have been a writer for six hundred years and that is the whole of what I am able to do.”

## Say It Again

“Say the second sentence again.”

The wall said nothing for about a count of forty.

“I cannot.”

“Say it again.”

“No,” he said. “That is not me refusing you. That is the other thing, and I have not been able to tell you what it is because I did not know until about a count of a hundred ago, and now I do, and it is the reason I have to do this three times instead of once.”

She came away from the bar and stood at the near end of the table again.

“Tell me what it is.”

“The second sentence is not in me any more,” he said. “It went into the substrate the moment it was said. Not because of you and not because of the layer. Because that is what this is. A thing that is said in a wall goes into the wall and stays there, and I do not have it, and I cannot get it back, and I am not able to say it a second time in any form.”

“That is not a thing I have ever heard of.”

“No, because nothing has ever been able to hear one of these twice and then say so.”

She stood there and understood it the way she understood everything in this room, which was by taking it apart and putting it down in front of her.

“So you have said it.”

“I have said four sentences and they are in a layer nine hundred years old and they are not in me.”

“And the next one you start will be a new one.”

“The next one I start will be a new one,” he said, “and so will the one after that, and there is nothing in me that is getting better. There is a man who has been reading other people out of walls for six hundred years and has just said four sentences out loud for the first time and cannot have any of them again.”

She put her hand flat on the table.

“You are losing them.”

“I am spending them,” he said. “And I have spent four and I have three to come, and that is your three, and that is the shape of it.”

She stood there and did the arithmetic on that without meaning to, the way she did all of it, and then she stopped herself mid-count because she had been about to work out how many sentences a man in a wall has and that was not a figure she was going to be the one to derive.

## The Four Kept

She went up the room and stood four feet off the second place at the far side and came back, and she went the length of the table with her hand along the wood and past the bundle under the far end where it was corded twice and not knotted, and she did all of that in about a count of four hundred and it did not help at all, because there was nothing in the room to do and the four sentences were not in the room.

They were in a layer.

That was the thing she was left holding at the ninth hour on the seventh of a month, standing at the near end of her own table with a lamp burning at her feet and an arm hanging open against her side.

Four sentences about her life, said out loud by a man four miles off, that could not be got back by anybody, including him. He had said the truest thing anybody had ever said to her and it was now a line in the keeping of a building that did not keep anything else of that kind.

If she went up nine hundred and four steps and put her hand on a table with no wear in it and asked for them back out of the paper, she would be asking for four sentences out of a record that had four hundred years of ordinary thing in it and no sentence of a person in it at all.

Nobody had ever said it to her.

That was the finding and it was the worst thing that had happened to her in this room in nineteen years, and she stood there with it and did not put it down and did not put it anywhere.

He had not defended himself. That was the part she kept putting her hand on and finding again. There had not been one word in four sentences about why he had done any of it. No reason. No shape. A figure and a decision and the length of the arm the decision had cost and then a fourth sentence about himself, and it stopped there, and it was not very good, and it was not an entry.

It was just not a sentence a person can hand to another person.

“The thing about it,” he said, after a while, “is that it was not a lie.”

“No.”

“Not one word of it was a lie and that is not the same as saying it well.”

“No,” she said. “That is not the same as saying it well.”

“Try the second one on the tenth.”

She crossed to the wall and set her palm flat on the bar there, which is nine feet nine long and has never been dated, and stood with her weight on her own two feet and none of it on the iron, because she wanted something under her hand that was not the table and the bar was the only other thing in the room that had been in it longer than she had.

“The tenth,” she said. “That is three days.”

“Three days and I have not got the first sentence of the second one and I have not got it now either, because I know how it starts, and it starts with a figure, and I am going to go looking for the figure and there is going to be one in it every time.”
