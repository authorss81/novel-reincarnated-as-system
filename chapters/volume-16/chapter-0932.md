# The Room She Is Not In

She heard it in the floor of her own room at about the fifth hour, before she had said a figure, before she had done anything at all, and that was how she knew.

A first of a month came up this building on the first of every month and it had come up this building for four hundred years, and it came up as a sound in the stone rather than as a noise, the way weather comes up through a mountain and does not arrive all at once. Boards, corded twice, and nine hundred and four steps of a stair that did not stop at nine.

She stood at the near end of her own table with the lamp on the stone at her feet and her hands flat on the wood and let it come up under her, the whole way, the way she had let four hundred of them come up under her while she stood in this room and did the near board and the terms board and went along the table with her hand on the wood.

Three hundred and twelve days since the ninth stage. One hundred and five since it was shut. Seven hundred and forty days with this arm open at the elbow. Seven hundred and forty-three days in this room. One thousand four hundred and eighty-five days gone at this morning, taken out of her at a rate she derived out of her own life, not one of them coming back.

She said all of it out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice, and the going of the bundle came up under it the whole time, and neither of the two of them stopped the other.

## What Came Up It

Eight flights and a stair above her a room had no wear in it. Nine hundred and four steps. A table with a lamp over it that had not been lit since she carried a lamp down them herself, and a place at the far side with nothing worn at all in four hundred years, and the register with the offer on it, and the offer withdrawn at the second hour on the twenty-seventh by a man who had said it out loud in the channel and out of her mouth.

She went to the foot of the stair and stood at the bottom of it.

It is not a small thing to be at the bottom of nine hundred and four steps that you have climbed twice in nineteen years, and both times you climbed it for a reason that was the next day, and you came down it with a lamp in your hand and fell on the tread past the twelfth and did not go back up.

She stood there and listened to it go up.

“I have not heard that go for three days,” she said, out loud, to the stair, in the ordinary voice.

“It is not for you.”

She did not turn round, because there was nothing to turn round to.

“It is not for anybody,” he said. “That is what I have been wanting to tell you for four days and I have not been able to say anything at all, so I am saying the small thing first.

“That went up nine hundred and four steps this morning and it is going to go up on the first of every month for as long as the building is standing. It does not know who is under it and it is not waiting for anybody, and it would have gone up on the twenty-seventh the same as it went up on the twenty-seventh when you were standing at the near end of your own table with a lamp at your feet.”

## The Bottom Of The Stair

She stood at the bottom of it with her hand on the wall and looked up into the dark.

“Will you come up it.”

“No.”

“Will you be in the room when it arrives.”

“No.”

“You are in the building.”

“I am in the layer and the layer is nine hundred years old and it goes through the whole of this mountain and every wall in the world at once, and I felt that bundle go up the stair on the first and I could not have told you which of the nine hundred and four steps it was on.”

She stood there with that.

In nineteen years she had had a man in her head and he had been with her on every single day of it, and she had gone through nine stages and two realms and a mountain and a wall and a room with no wear in it, and he had been in all of it, and this morning she found out that the one thing in the whole of that he could not do with her was stand in a room on the first of a month.

“All right,” she said.

“I am not saying it to be sorry for myself. I am telling you what it is now, because you have been asking me what I am since the twenty-seventh and the answer is that I am audible and I have no place.”

“I have worked that out.”

“You have worked out that you cannot reach me,” he said. “You have not worked out that there is no place in which I would have been with you and not have been at a count of four lengths, and I am telling you the difference because it is not a small one to me and you are the only person left who could be told anything.”

## Under It

She stood at the bottom of the stair for the whole of the going of it.

It took about a count of six hundred and two hundred, and she had been under it before on six firsts and had never once listened, and this time she put her back against the wall of the stair with her right arm hanging open against her side and her left hand flat on the stone and let a month go up over her head.

It made no difference to the room she was standing in. That was the thing she had gone down to the bottom of the stair to find out and she found it out in the first count of two hundred.

Nine hundred and four steps and a room at the top with no wear in it and a register on a table and an offer withdrawn.

A bundle of boards goes up on the first and comes to the top of nine hundred and four steps and is taken off and cut and goes somewhere she could not name, and it happens whether there is a woman at the bottom of the stair or not. It happened today. The lamp on the stone beside her went on burning while it happened.

## The Morning Afterwards

She got up off the wall and went back down to her own room and stood in the middle of it and found that she did not know what to do with the rest of the day.

That had happened twice in her life. Once at ten years old on the morning a voice had told her what she was going to be, and once on the twenty-second of a month near the end, when she had spent a whole morning trying to decide whether to decide for a man and had not managed it.

Both times she had gone at it with the room, because the room had never once refused to answer, and because standing in a room and naming what was in it had got her out of every morning of nineteen years that she could remember.

So she did it.

A table with wear in it, worn by her own forearms over nineteen years at the near end and by nothing at the far side. Three boards face up a foot in from the near end and a terms board a foot further along.

A strip of pale wood face up under the table at the near end of the leg, put back on the fourth of the month beginning two thousand seven hundred and seventy-four and looked at by her perhaps twice since. A bundle corded twice and not knotted under the far end. A bar nine feet nine against the left-hand wall, dated nowhere. A cage lamp out on a shelf. A second place kept four hundred years and empty.

She got through all of it in about a count of four hundred and she did not look at anything. It is not a thing to have to look at a room like this. She had been in it seven hundred and forty-three days and there was not one object in it she could not have found in the dark, and she had never once in all those days gone up the room and looked at the far side, and that was not a decision she had made either.

She went and looked at the far side.

There was nothing worn in it. Four hundred years of a table with a place at the far end of it that people had stood at and not sat down on, and a hollow in the wood about the length of a forearm that was not there.

She stood over the place where it would have been if it had ever been any of them, and she thought about a man telling her at the fourteenth hour of a night in the spring that it was kept and the keeping was somebody’s and that somebody was not her.

She went back to the near end and put her hand in the hollow and that one was there.

That was the whole of her morning. Nine hundred and four steps with a bundle on them went up over her head and came to a room she had left for good and did not go back to, and she spent the rest of the first of a month standing in her own room finding out that a room can be full of things you know and still be the room you are alone in.

And then at about the eighth hour he said, “Go up and look at the table.”

“No.”

“It is a table with nothing on it but a piece of wood with five mercies cut into it and a line under them that says it is open until it is answered or withdrawn.”

“I know what is on that table.”

“I know that you know what is on that table,” he said. “I am asking you to go up nine hundred and four steps and put your hand on the offer, because a piece of what I am went out of this building on the twenty-seventh and there is a shape left in this room where it used to be, and you are the only person alive who can find the edge of it.”

She stood at the near end of her own table and looked up into the dark past the last landing for a while, and she thought about a stair she had carried a lamp down, and she did not go up it.

At the ninth hour she stood with the lamp on the stone and said the day’s figures out loud into a room and there was nothing in the room at all, and she had not expected anything and said them anyway, because the ninth hour was hers and it was the whole of what she had.
