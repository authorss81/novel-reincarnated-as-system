# The Fire Under Nine Steps

The town of Lowwater was under the terrace and she got into it at about the seventh hour of the sixth day with about forty people from the bowl behind her who had walked out of it on their own feet and had no other idea in the world.

She had not known she was going to do that until the day before, and she had not told anybody she was going to do it, and she had worked out the whole of it standing on a wet rim at about the ninth hour with about four people lying down near her.

Three hundred and ninety-nine people were out there in nine counties. Three hundred and nine of them were somewhere in four.

So she was going to stand where there were the most of them, and the most of them in the world was a place with a terrace on it that had not moved in nine hundred years.

## Nine Tiers Of Stone

Qinghe stands above Lowwater on nine tiers of cut stone and the tiers have been there so long that the lowest of them is not dressed at all any more, it is just the ground that has not moved.

She got up onto the first tier at about the eighth hour and stopped there for about a count of six hundred.

Nine hundred years. She could derive that. There was a marker on the top tier of the same stone as the others with nothing cut on it anywhere, and the stone under her hand was cold, and the stone was cold everywhere she put it.

That was the thing she had come for and she got it in about a count of two hundred: nothing on this terrace was warm.

## Getting People Off It

There were about four hundred people on and under it.

The hall on the top tier was empty. That was the first thing and it was the worst thing, because it took her a while to understand that it was not damaged. It was swept. Somebody had swept it, recently, and put the benches in two rows, and there were about sixty people sitting in them who were not eating and not talking.

Under the hall there were four tiers of rooms and about three hundred people in them, and they were not on fire and they were not under anything at that moment, and the eastern corner of the third tier had a crack in it about as long as her forearm.

So she did the thing she had done twice already and she stood in the one place where the whole of it could hear her and she gave them the arithmetic first, out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice.

“There was a crack in the third tier this morning when I came on to the first. There were sixty people in a hall that had been swept and not one of them in it. There were about four hundred of you on this terrace and under it.

“I had been at this six days and I had got one thing in front of me that I was able to stand behind, which was that I had got two working hands and one that did not, and I was going to use them to get you off.”

Not one person argued with her about it, which she had braced for.

## What She Could Not Say

She could not say what was happening.

She had said nine words on the thirtieth of a month at a gap in a wall in front of eight people, and she had not known then that anything would come of it, and she had not been able to derive a single thing out of it since, and there was not one person on this terrace she could stand in front of and say *that is what did this*.

It was not the sentence that came out. It was the plainest thing she had and it was the only true thing she had.

“I did not know what it was. I said that standing up, and I was not going to spend the morning pretending that a woman with one arm was a woman who knew something.

“You were going to come off this terrace because there was a crack in the third tier and I was not able to give you a reason for the crack.”

They came off.

It took her from about the ninth hour until the light went.

Not because of the stair. Because of the hall with sixty people in it who wanted an answer before they moved, and because of a kitchen on the second tier where the floor had gone in about a cart’s width on the far side and nine people were standing at the edge of it not moving.

And because of a boy of about fourteen who was somewhere above her the whole afternoon and whom she found at the last of the light asleep on the top tier with his arms round the marker.

## Sixty People In A Swept Hall

They came off in the ordinary way and not quickly, because about sixty of them would not come off at all until somebody had answered them, and they had been sitting in two rows of benches on the top tier of a terrace for about six hours waiting.

A woman of about fifty stood up in the second row and put the whole of it to her in one sentence, standing, in the ordinary voice, in front of a hall of people.

“If you do not know, then what are you doing here, and are you going to be here tomorrow, and if you are, then who is looking after us tonight.”

Three questions and she had about a count of twenty before the hall answered the first one.

She said, standing, in the ordinary voice, in front of about sixty people: she was there because there was a crack in the third tier and she had two working hands and one that did not, and she was going to be there tomorrow because she had said so out loud on a rim two days ago and had not found a reason not to be.

And she said that she did not have anybody to look after them tonight, because there was not one person in four counties she could ask, and she was not able to be given anything or to be paid, and there was no office anywhere she could put a person.

The hall took about a count of a hundred over that.

Then the woman of about fifty said, “That is three answers and not one of them was any use.”

“I knew,” Ning Yan said. “I was telling you the true ones before I started making the other kind up, and if you wanted me to make the other kind up I would, and I would say first that I had made it up.”

“Go on.”

“There is a house on the west side of Lowwater with a barn on it and about forty beds in the barn and a woman in it who has told me she has room for anything that walks in,” Ning Yan said.

“I had not been in it and I did not know that it was true. I had got it out of a man with a broken shaft two days ago and he was not lying to me. That was the whole of what I had and I had just told them where it came from.”

About twenty of the sixty went to that house. The rest slept in the hall, on the benches, on the top tier of a terrace with a crack in the third tier, because there was nothing better and because it was still there in the morning.

## Under The Bottom Course

She went down under the bottom tier at about the ninth hour because the light had gone and because the underside of it was the only part of the terrace she had not looked at.

There were cellars under it. Nine hundred years of cellars, and the first two were just rooms, and the third one had a fire in it.

A grate, and a coal in the grate, and the coal had an edge on it that had not been cut by anything.

She stood on the stone floor of a cellar under the bottom tier of a terrace with a fire in it and worked out, standing, in the ordinary voice, on her own, that a fire does not sit in a grate for nine hundred years.

Coal goes out in a day. A grate that is kept warm takes fuel every few hours and anybody who has ever done it knows it, and there was no fuel in that cellar and there had not been fuel in that cellar for as long as the walls had been standing.

There was no fire in it because the fire was the terrace.

The stone nine hundred years above her head, cold at the first tier and colder as she came down, and there in the third cellar under the bottom course, held up and kept and warm the whole way, by a fire that a hundred and forty families had been told to keep.

## The Woman Who Kept It

The woman was about eighty and she had come down into the cellar to look at the fire, which had been hers to look at since before she could remember.

“It was my grandmother’s,” she said. “And it was her mother’s before that. I am not able to tell you how many, because it was not written anywhere and there was not one person who came and asked. I have brought it up once a day since I was a girl.”

“For what.”

“That is the whole of what I was told.” The woman looked at the grate. “You keep the fire. That is all of it. Not one person ever said what for and not one person ever took it off me and not one person ever put it on me either, and I have not asked, because you do not ask about the thing you were handed.”

She stood there for a while with her hands on her hips.

“It is out,” she said.

“It is out.”

“And I cannot put it back. There is not one piece of fuel in this cellar and there has not been one piece in my time.”

Ning Yan looked at a grate that had been going for nine hundred years with a woman of about eighty standing next to her, and she thought about a flat stone on a terrace above a quarry on the second day of a month, and about her own palm on it, and about the fact that she had not put that into anything she said because she could not derive it.

She could derive it now.

The terrace was being let go because the thing underneath it was not being held any more. She had no idea what the thing was. She had a figure for nine hundred years and she had a figure for six days and she had a grate with nothing in it and a woman of about eighty who had kept a fire for most of her life on the word of a family that could not say why.

And she had said nine words on the thirtieth of a month.

She said it out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice, in a cellar, to that woman, which she had never once done in nineteen years to anybody.

“I said nine words at a gap in a wall on the thirtieth of the month and I did not know what they would do and they had been doing something for six days. I did not know that there was a fire under this terrace and I did not know that there was somebody keeping it. That was on me and I was putting it out loud before anything else so that it was in front of them before anything else was.”

The woman of about eighty did not move and did not say anything for about a count of four hundred.

Then she said, “Then that was where you had been living.”

“Yes.”

“Then you had been living in the wrong place,” the woman said, “and so had I, and there was not one of us that knew.”

And she went up the stairs to look at it.
