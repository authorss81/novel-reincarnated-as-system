# Five Mercies

She did the first one at about the sixth hour on the tenth of the month beginning three thousand and fourteen, standing at the near end of her own table, out loud, in the ordinary voice, before she touched the second.

She had the whole of the day for it and she took the whole of the day for it and she did not once go up the stair, and she did not once take a board up off the face of the table and read it, because this was not a reading.

The stone was eight flights and a stair above her and she was not going back up it. Everything she said this morning she said out of her own memory, standing, and everything she had said out of her own memory for a month had gone out of this room in this room’s voice.

“First,” she said. “She is carried up and is made an Ascendant, and a Ward of her own is set in her name, and the carrying is done whole and is not a taking, and the ninth is not the end of what she is put in the world for.”

She put her hand flat on the table.

“Now say the other half of it.”

“The other half of it is a person gets a Ward when the machine has finished with them,” he said. “That is not my reading. That is what a Ward is. It is a place that is set in a name after the keeping has got what it came for, and it is not a prize and it was never in anybody’s mind as a prize.

“The ninth is not the end of what she is put in the world for, because the ninth is the end of what she is put in the world for, and the sentence after that is not a comfort. It is an account.”

She stood there and let that go past her.

A Ward. She had wanted a place of her own since she was a child and she had been given one in a sentence on the twentieth of the month beginning two thousand nine hundred and fifty-four, in the dark, standing, by somebody who was not in the room, and it had come into her body as something that was not hunger and she had not been able to say what it was for a month.

It was this. A Ward is what a person is given at the end of the useful part. It is not the useful part. It is what the machine hands over so that it does not have to go on using the word used.

She had not known that at the twentieth and she knew it now and she was not going to be able to un-know it, and there was not one person alive she could say it to.

“Second one.”

“No.”

## Going Through Them

“Do not do that,” she said. “I am not stopping at the one that costs me most. That is what you asked me for on the seventh of the month before last and I am telling you now that I am not going to do it, and if you want a witness who finds the easy ones and leaves out the hard one then you have picked the wrong woman and you have been sitting in her head for nine years.”

“All right.”

“Second, third, fourth, fifth. I am going to say all of them and put it underneath all of them, and you are going to let me, and you are not going to tell me that I am reading them wrong.”

“I am not going to tell you that.”

She stood at the near end of her own table with the lamp on the stone and went through them, standing, in the ordinary voice, out loud.

The second. The third. The fourth. The fifth: the walls stay, and the layer stays, and the mercy of a bounded world stays, no unchecked ascension, no harvested souls, no immortal war over the inside of a small place.

“None of them is a lie,” she said. “That is what I have to say about all five and it is the part I am not going to get out of the way of. I read them four times in two days in a room with no light in it and not one of them is a lie, and every one of them is real, and the thing that made them has never once told me a false word about anything.”

“No.”

“You have all been made in good faith.”

“All of it has,” he said. “That is not the part I am asking you to look at. Look at the other part.”

## The Machine Under The Walls

“The walls,” she said. “Say what holds them up.”

“The tax.”

“Say it as a chain and do not make it short.”

“The tax is what keeps the bound bounded,” he said. “Unchecked ascension inside a wall that is not a bound drains something that the wall is standing on, and the only reason there is a bound is that the tax needs one person at a time to take from and needs the world quiet enough to find her in.

“That is not a figure and it is not mine. It was worked out long before I had a mouth, and it is true, and the walls have been standing for nine hundred years on top of it.”

“So the third term and the fourth term and the fifth term are the same machine.”

“The third term and the fourth term and the fifth term are what the tax buys,” he said, “and they are the reason the tax is worth running, and both of those are true at once and neither cancels the other and you have not got a place in this room to put that down.”

She put her hands flat on the wood.

## The Rule Under The Rule

“Now the sixth cut,” she said. “And you do not get to leave that one out either.”

“A thing is said in the voice of the person it is about, standing, before it is a line anywhere.”

“A machine that takes a woman apart for nine years and then files her has built a rule into the keeping that she must say every true thing about herself out loud, on her own feet, before it is a line anywhere,” she said. “And it is a good rule. It is the best rule in this building. And it is the rule that means nothing that ever gets written down about a person like me was ever got out of me by force.”

He was quiet for a count of forty.

“Nothing in the four hundred years of that keeping has ever been written down about a Chosen One,” he said, “because a Chosen One could not be written down about without standing up and saying it first. That is not a mercy. That is the machine being polite about the only thing it cannot force.”

“Yes,” she said. “It is very good at that.”

## The One That Was His

“Then the second new term.”

His part.

“He is archived where he is, and addressed wherever he is, and remembered in the keeping of what he did and not of what it cost. He is given a name that is not the one he died under, and he is given work that matters, and neither of the two is a rank.”

She said it standing, in the ordinary voice, out loud, in her own room, to his face, and the place behind her fourth rib pulled and she let it pull.

“Now say the tax again,” she said. “Say what happens at the end of a keeping.”

He was quiet for a count of forty.

“Remembers them accurately,” he said. “A name. What they did. What it cost. Written down properly, in the keeping of what they did and not of what it cost, and then not looked at again.”

“That is the sentence.”

“That is the sentence.”

“The offer to you,” she said, “is in the same words as the thing that happens to a woman when the machine is finished with her. Addressed. Remembered accurately. Kept properly. Not looked at again.”

“Yes.”

“You are on that table getting what a Chosen One gets.”

“I am on that table getting what a Chosen One gets,” he said, “and I have read it and I have not once said so, and you have said it standing in your own room in the ordinary voice in under a minute, and I have not been able to say it for thirty-two days because I have spent six hundred years treating that as a good thing.”

## The Finding

She stood at the near end of her own table with her hands flat on the wood and her right arm hanging open against her side for seven hundred and twenty-nine days, and she had been in this room since she was ten years old, and she had been carrying the whole of it since the third of the month beginning two thousand two hundred and four.

“There is not one mercy on that register,” she said, “that does not need the machine that was done to me.”

“No.”

“There is not one mercy on it that does not need one of us.”

“No,” he said. “There is not one mercy on it that does not need one of us, and you are the one standing in the room, and I have been sitting in it for nine years telling you the mercies were real.”

She stood there and did not argue with him and did not soften anything and did not tell him he had done a thing worth telling her about. She put her hand flat on the wood where her own arms had worn a hollow in nineteen years and looked at the far side of the room, at the place that had been kept four hundred years and empty.

“The walls do not keep themselves,” she said. “There is no other sentence anywhere in the world about how the walls are kept. And the only sentence that has ever been said about it is the name of a machine that takes a person apart until it is finished with her.”

She left that standing there and did not put anything after it.

“Say something,” he said.

“No,” she said. “You wanted me to be able to say one true thing in this room without a figure in it and I have said it, and I am not going to stand here and let you find out whether it was the wrong one.”
