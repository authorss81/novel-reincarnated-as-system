# Out Of The High Country

She got clear of it on the twenty-sixth, at about the fifth hour, and the getting clear of it was not a thing that happened all at once.

It was a shoulder of rock. She went up onto it because she had been walking for the better part of a fortnight and her breath had been going to five on the rises instead of seven, and she wanted somewhere to stand still and be nine.

From up there the ground went down for a long way in scrub and then in worked lines and then it was flat enough to be somebody’s idea of a road, and there were roofs down in the flat, more than nine of them.

She looked back up.

There was nothing there.

She stood on the shoulder of rock and worked out what she was looking at, because she was a woman who worked things out and there was nothing else to do while she was standing there.

The mountain above her was one colour. The scrub on it was the same colour as the scrub below it. There was a shape somewhere up in the middle of it that was a building with eight flights and a stair behind it and it was the same dark as the rock it was made out of.

She could find it if she knew where it was, and she knew exactly where it was, and she could not see it.

Four hundred years. A table with no wear in it. Nine hundred and four steps. A room with a bar against the wall dated nowhere and three boards face up on a table and a bundle corded twice under the far end and a lamp on a stone floor a little to the left of where it had stood.

Sixteen days from the eleventh to the twenty-sixth, counting both ends, and she had not gone back up once and she was not going to.

## The Sixteen Days

She said it out loud, standing, on the shoulder of rock, in the ordinary voice, and it was the first whole figure she had put into the keeping since she had left the room.

“Sixteen days. I have been off the mountain sixteen days counting both ends and I have not been to the top of it and I am not going to. That is the first thing I have said out loud in this place and I am saying it standing, before it is a line anywhere.”

There was a wind off the flat. There was nothing else for about a count of three hundred, and she let the whole of it go past.

Then she went down onto the road.

## The Road She Was Not On

The track she had come down had been a track. It went from roof to roof and it went up and it was used by people who knew where they were.

This was not that.

The road under her was flat and it was made, and there were ruts in it that had taken a great deal of work over a great deal of years, and she stood in one of the ruts and put her hand flat on the ground and found that other people had been putting their weight into that same line of ground since before she was born.

She got on it and walked south, or what she worked out was south, because the light was coming from over her left shoulder and she had been walking on the sun for nine years out of a room with no window in it and that was the whole of her navigation and it was more than she had.

The road went out of the flat and along the side of a hill and there were carts on it, going both ways, and the first one that came past her at speed had a man on it who looked at her and then looked at her hand and then looked away again very fast, the way people look at a thing they have been told about.

## The Cloth

That happened four times on the twenty-sixth.

The first woman did it. She was about sixty and she had a bundle on her back and she saw a girl of nineteen walking up the side of a hill by herself in weather with nothing on her, and she took a folded cloth off the top of her bundle and put it over Ning Yan’s shoulders from behind without asking, the way you would put a blanket on a child.

It did not take.

That was not a figure of speech. The cloth came off the woman’s hands and it went through the place where Ning Yan’s shoulders were and it was on the ground behind her, and Ning Yan turned round with her arm already up and the woman was already saying that she was sorry, that she had not meant, that it did not matter.

“I could not take it,” Ning Yan said.

And she tried. That was the part the woman watched. She tried four times with both hands, three fingers and a thumb on the left and a right hand that could hold a lamp and nothing heavier, and the cloth would not stay on her and would not stay in her hands, and it went to the ground.

“Can you not hold a cloth?”

“I can hold a lamp,” Ning Yan said, and she picked it up and gave it back to her, and the woman took it without looking at her, and her face did something, and she went on up the hill.

Three more times on that road. A boy with a jacket. A carter who pulled his animal up out of the way for her and then could not work out how to give her the sack of grain he had brought for somebody else. A girl of about ten who put her hand on Ning Yan’s arm to steady her on the wet stone and had to take it straight back off again as though she had put it on a post.

Ning Yan worked all of that out standing in the middle of the road with an arm open at the elbow and she did not say one word about it to anybody, and the figure in it was the one she had put into the keeping every morning for nineteen years without ever once having to look at it happening in front of her.

She could not be given anything.

That was one of the twenty-one and it had been with her since she was about fifteen years old and she had never in all those years so much as been offered a bowl.

## What She Felt About It

She felt angry about it.

Not at them. She was not angry at any of the four of them and she had been close to tears twice in four hours in a way she had not been at anything since she was ten.

She was angry at the arithmetic.

Because it had cost her nothing. That was the part that would not go. There was not a mark on her anywhere and there was no wound to show anybody and she was not going to be able to put it in front of a single person she met on this road, and in exchange for it she had got the exact right to walk the whole of a road being handed things by strangers and being unable to take one of them.

## What She Counted On The Road

She counted on the road because there was nothing else to do with her hands.

She had walked out of a building on the eleventh and there was a whole set of her that had stood at the same piece of wood for six thousand mornings and put six numbers into a wall at the ninth hour, and there was no wall out here and there were still the numbers and there was still a ninth hour, and she did not know what a person was who carried the arithmetic around with her all day instead of putting it somewhere and going away from it.

So she did the days.

Sixteen days from the eleventh to the twenty-sixth, counting both ends. The arm open at the elbow eight hundred and five days and not going to close. Three hundred and sixty-seven days since the ninth stage and one hundred and sixty since it was shut.

One thousand five hundred and forty days gone at a rate she derived out of her own life. Not one of them coming back, and not one of them coming back out here either, which was the thing that had surprised her and which she had got used to by about the nineteenth.

The figures did not go up.

That was the finding on the road and she did not have it until the twenty-first.

She had gone down the mountain believing, without ever having put it into words, that a keeping was a thing that took, and that it took by being in a building. She had said six figures into a room for nineteen years and had believed that the room was the mouth of the thing.

Out on the road the figures stood still.

The arm was open at the elbow because it was open at the elbow. The days were gone because they were gone. The place behind her fourth rib was the size of her thumbnail and she had put her hand on it under a lean-to on the nineteenth and had found it exactly the same size it had been on the eleventh.

Seven hundred and eighty-nine days in that room had been seven hundred and eighty-nine days in that room and not one day of it had been a day of this road.

She stood by a wall at about the seventh hour on the twenty-first and worked out, standing, that she had spent nineteen years being afraid of a place and she had come down a mountain out of it and nothing had followed her.

Then she went and got on with the walking, because there was a road and she was on it.

## The Turn Round

She turned round once, at about the eighth hour, on the flat where the road first came out onto open ground.

The high country was behind a shoulder of hill and it was gone. She could not see any of it and she could not see the place on it where a building with eight flights and a stair behind it was standing, and it was not because there was weather in the way.

She stood there and let that be the whole of what she got out of going and looked back over it, because there was a lot in it and she was not going to pretend there was not.

She had been in a room for seven hundred and eighty-nine days when she walked out of it. She was not in a room now. She had a dead lamp in her left hand and four blank leaves against her fourth rib, one of them with her own hand on it in charcoal, and she had told a woman at a stall that the price was on the record and she did not know what it cost her.

Ahead of her, where the road crossed another one, there were people.

She could see the dust of them. There were a great many more people on that crossing than she had seen anywhere since the eleventh, and they were standing about in groups the way people stand about when they have come from somewhere and are deciding whether to go on.

She went towards them with a lamp that had not carried a flame since the fourteenth and no sentence at all, and she was the only person in the world who knew there was anything to be said.
