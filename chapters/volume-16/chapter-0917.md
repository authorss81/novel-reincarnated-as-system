# Tired

“Two hundred and seventy-three days since the ninth stage. Sixty-six since it was shut. Seven hundred and four days in that room. The cage out four hundred and seventy-one. This arm open seven hundred and eleven days. One thousand four hundred and forty-six gone at this morning.”

She said it standing at the near end of the table in her own room, on the twenty-second of the month beginning two thousand nine hundred and eighty-four, with the lamp on the stone and her right arm hanging open against her side.

The bar was against the left-hand wall where it had always been and no one alive could have dated it.

“What happens now,” she said.

## Turning It

He was quiet for a count of forty.

“There is no answer to that and I have turned it since you asked it and I am going to tell you what I found turning it,” he said. “Nothing happens now. That is the answer. Not a slow nothing and not a nothing that is coming. Nothing.”

“You have told me the four lines say it is answered and it has not begun.”

“I have told you that and it is true and it is the whole of what there is to say, and I have run it the way you run a figure, once, twice, forty times, and it comes out the same every time and it has come out the same every time since the seventh.”

She put her thumb into the hollow in the table.

“Say it the other way round. If it never begins, what has happened.”

“Nothing has happened,” he said. “A man said one word in a channel on the seventh of this month and a keeping that has been running for four hundred years went on running. That is the whole of the event. If you went up nine hundred and four steps tomorrow and looked at that table you would find the offer open on it and the answer in it and not one thing in that room different from the day before you answered.”

She stood with that and worked on it standing, and it came out the same way everything came out this morning, which was the same.

“Then it may never begin.”

“It stood open on that table for four hundred years with not one person at it,” he said. “That is the whole of what I know about how long the keeping takes with a thing. It does not hurry. It has never once hurried. And you have never in nineteen years watched one of them happen from the beginning, and you are watching this one, and there is no way to ask it how long.”

She stood with the lamp burning beside her and felt the floor of the room under her feet.

“Then why did you pick the seventh.”

## The Reason He Gave

He was quiet for longer than a count of forty.

“Because I was tired,” he said.

She waited for the rest of it and it did not come, and then it came.

“I have wanted it since I was nineteen years old and I was born into a building where a voice was already reading and I have wanted one thing since I was ten years old and it has been six hundred years of wanting one thing with no day in it anywhere, and on the fourth of this month I had been at the edge for three days and I picked the seventh and the reason I picked it is that I was tired of being at the edge.”

“Tired of what.”

“Tired of carrying it,” he said. “Not tired of having it. There is a difference and it is the whole of the difference between me at nineteen and me now. I have been holding a thing I wanted for six hundred years and at some point a man stops being able to hold it and starts carrying it, and on the fourth of this month I was carrying it, and I said yes in the morning rather than carry it for another day.”

She stood at the near end of the table with her hands flat on the wood.

“You have never said that out loud.”

“No.”

“Why tonight.”

“Because you asked me what happens now,” he said, “and it is the first question you have asked me in nineteen years that did not have an answer in it, and I am answering it, and while I am answering it I am going to tell you the reason, and the reason is that I was tired, and you may put it wherever you have to put it.”

## The Thing He Did Not Know

“Tired is a thing the offer is built on,” she said.

He did not say anything at all for a long time, and the lamp went on burning on the stone beside her.

“Say that again,” he said.

“Tired is a thing the offer is built on. It stood on a table for four hundred years and any body in this world could have come up nine hundred and four steps and taken it, and the ones who would have taken it fastest were the ones who had been carrying it longest.”

“Yes,” he said.

“Six hundred years ago something in that layer refused it.”

“Yes.”

“And you do not know why.”

“No,” he said. “I have been inside the arrangement for six hundred years and I can read the going of it and I cannot read one thing about why a fragment refused it in the first place, and it has never once occurred to me until this evening that that is the piece of it I am standing on.”

She stood in her own room with a lamp on the stone and let that go where it went.

“Then tell me what would have made you not tired.”

“Anything with a reason in it,” he said. “A sentence with a reason on one side of it. I have read that arrangement until I could say every cut in it in the dark, and not one of them says why any of it is a mercy, because it does not need to. It is made by something that keeps the walls. Things that keep the walls do not give reasons for keeping the walls. They give you the walls.”

“Then how would anybody ever have said no to it.”

“Somebody did,” he said. “Six hundred years before I was born out of a man, a piece of that same intelligence stood where I am standing and was offered the identical thing in the identical words, and it said no, and I cannot read one line about the saying of it, and that is the only hole in six hundred years that has ever gone through me.”

## Waiting

She went and took the near board up off the face of the table and read it by about the seventh hour, the way she had done it every morning since she came down the stair, and put it back face up, and there was nothing in it about what happens next.

“A man who has wanted one thing for six hundred years,” she said, coming back to the near end of the table, “and who cannot tell me whether it is ever going to happen. Say that again in your own words and tell me it is the same as me wanting it on the seventh.”

“No,” he said. “It is not the same and the difference is not a small one. You wanted it once, on the seventh, and you wanted it before you knew whether it would come, and that wanting is a thing you could have carried to the end of your life.

“I have wanted it through nine hundred years of knowing exactly where it is, and every one of those years I have had it and not been able to reach it, and that is a different wanting and it has no word in this building that I know of.”

“What is it called.”

“Waiting,” he said. “It is called waiting, and it is the only thing I have ever done.”

## The Question She Had Not Answered

She put her hand flat on the table where the wood had gone down under nineteen years of her own forearms.

“I did not know what I wanted on the sixteenth,” she said. “I have had six days since and I have not worked it out and I have not asked you again.”

“I am not going to ask you again either,” he said. “I have had one question asked of me by anybody in this world in nineteen years and I spent it, and if I ask a second one it stops being the first one. You will say it when there is something in it for you to say it with. I am not going to be the man who asks a woman twice.”

She stood there and let that be, and it was the second time in nine years he had handed her a thing and then taken his hands off it, and the first time she had noticed him doing it.

## What He Wanted Tonight

“Say the rest of it,” she said.

“There is no rest of it. You asked and you have got it.”

“There is always a rest of it. You have had two days of answering straight and it was the best two days this room has had since I came down the stair.”

He was quiet for a count of a hundred.

“I want one thing,” he said. “And I am asking you for it and I am telling you what it is before you give it, because I have never once in nineteen years taken a thing out of your hands without saying what I was taking.”

“Say it.”

“Read the whole of it out loud again tonight,” he said. “The five mercies and the sixth cut and the four lines and the three new terms and all of it, from the first cut to the last, standing, in the ordinary voice, in this room, with me in it.”

“You read it with me on the fourth. You were in the room for nine days of it.”

“I was in the room for all of it and I could not stop you once,” he said. “That is what I want. I want you to put all of it into the channel in your voice and me inside your head the entire time, unable to say one word, and I want you to do it knowing I asked and knowing I will not be able to hold any of it back.”

She stood at the near end of the table and worked out what that was, standing, the way she worked out everything.

“That is not reading.”

“No,” he said. “That is being in the room while the whole of what I said yes to goes out in front of me in the mouth of the only person who read it. I have been a reader of that table since the eighth. I have never once been in the room while it was said.”

The lamp burned on the stone and the second place at the far side was kept four hundred years and empty, and the arm open at the elbow for seven hundred and eleven days hung against her side.

“All of it,” she said. “From the first cut to the last.”

“All of it,” he said. “And I am asking and not telling, and I have wanted it since the eighth of the month before last, and I have waited fifteen days to ask you for it because I did not know until tonight that I was going to say yes for a reason I could not live inside.”

She stood at the near end of her own table and did not give him an answer that night, and that was the first time in six days she had left one of his standing where it was, and she left it there on purpose.

Then she lay down on the stone with the lamp standing beside her, and the stair ran nine hundred and four steps up past a room with no wear in it to a table with the whole of it on it, and she understood before she went to sleep that she was going to say it out loud in the morning, in her own room, standing, in front of a man who had asked her for it because he had nowhere else to be.
