# The Boards Left Face Up

She was up before the sixth hour on the eleventh of the month beginning three thousand and seventy-four, and the lamp had burned down into the last of its oil, and she did not look at it.

She took the near board up off the face of the table and read it by and put it back face up a foot in from the near end, and she did the terms board a foot further along, and then she went the length of the table with her hand along the wood.

It was not that she did not know what was on them. She had read the near board more times than any person has ever read one thing, and she could have put her thumb on a groove in it blind, and she read it anyway because it was the eleventh day of a month and it was the sixth hour and a room with a table in it is not a room you walk out of without doing the thing the room is for.

## The Room As It Was

She stopped at the near end where her own forearms had worn the wood down over nineteen years and she put her hand into the hollow and left it there for about a count of two hundred.

The hollow was the shape of her hand. That was all it was, and she had known that for a long time, and she had never once let herself stand in this room and say it.

She had been nineteen years old since the wood took that shape and it had been hers and not anybody’s, and she was going to walk away from it with a lamp in one hand, and not one person would ever come behind her and put a forearm into that hollow again, and that was the first thing in nineteen years she had ever been able to do to a room.

Then she went up the room and stood four feet off the place at the far side that had been kept four hundred years and had nothing worn into it at all, and she did not put her hand on the wood there, and she came back down.

She put her hand flat on the bar against the left-hand wall and took none of her weight on it. She did not lift it off the wall and she did not look at where its two ends went.

She went to the shelf and left the cage lamp where it had stood since the day she had put it out, and she did not pick it up, and she did not say one word to it.

Under the far end of the table the bundle was corded twice and not knotted, and she put her fingers on the cord and not on the knot, and she left it where it was.

## Standing, Before It Was A Line Anywhere

And then she stood up straight at the near end of her own table with nothing in either hand and she said it.

“I am leaving this room. I am leaving it on the eleventh of the month beginning three thousand and seventy-four at about the sixth hour and I am saying it standing, in the ordinary voice, before it is a line anywhere.

“Nothing goes up the stair. Nothing is opened. The boards stay face up where they are. The bar stays against the wall and stays dated nowhere. The bundle stays under the far end of the table and does not get pulled. The place at the far side stays as it has been kept.

“I am taking the lamp that is standing on this stone floor and I am taking the book against my own ribs, and that is the whole of what I am taking.”

She stood there with both hands flat on the worn wood.

Nothing came back to her, and nothing had come back to her since the second hour of the twenty-seventh of the month before last, and she had said it anyway, because the rule was hers and she was not going to let it lapse on the morning she finally needed it for something she wanted.

## The Lamp

She went and stood over it where it stood on the stone, a little to the left of where it had stood before the second hour of the twenty-seventh, and it had not been moved since and she was not going to move it now that she was going to take it away.

There was a boy at the foot of this stair who made oil in a building with no store in it and carried it up nine hundred and four steps in a can he could not fill from anywhere else, and she had decided on the fifteenth of a month in the ninth year that she was not going to be given oil.

That was a decision somebody had made about her and she had found it out and she had not been able to change it, and it was standing at this exact hour with a lamp in her hand about three fingers and a thumb wide on the end of an arm that would not close at the elbow.

She had never once in seven hundred and ninety days of an open arm been able to close her hand on anything at full strength, and there was four hundred years of a keeping in this mountain and not one thing in all of it had ever been able to put a full hand back on her.

So the lamp would burn until there was nothing left in it to burn, and then it would be a lamp she could carry.

She picked it up in her left hand.

## Down

She went out and down.

She went down eight flights and a stair and past the bar and past the boards and past the table with the wear in it, and she did not put her hand on anything on the way down, and at the bottom she went out of a doorway and into a morning she had not stood in at that hour since she was ten years old and had not run.

She had gone down this stair twice in nineteen years and both times she had gone down it for a reason that was the next day, and both times she had come down it with the lamp in her hand and had fallen on the tread past the twelfth and had not gone back up.

She went down it a third time, on her own two feet, with nothing to get to the bottom for except the bottom.

The stair was colder than the room had been. She noticed that at about the ninth tread and she stood on it for a moment with her hand on the wall, not because she needed to, and then she went on down.

There was no reason to stop in a stair. She had learned that on the seventh of a month in the ninth year and she had never had anybody to repeat it to.

At the bottom there were two doors and she had used one of them twice in nineteen years and she went out of the other one, because the other one went out under the overhang of the rock and the weather came in under the overhang and the other one did not, and there was no reason on earth for her to be in the wind on the morning she walked out of a building for the last time.

She had never once in nineteen years made a decision about a door.

## What The Light Was Like

The light outside was not the light in a room.

That was the whole of what she could say about it, and she said it out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice, on the track below the building, because it was the first true thing she had put into the keeping since she left and she wanted it in.

“It is not the same light out here. I am not going to stand here and pretend that it is.”

The ground went out from the building in a long tilt of scrub and loose stone, and below that there were trees, and the trees were nothing like the black rock this mountain was made of, and she had never once in nineteen years been under a sky at this hour with nothing at all between her and it.

She wanted to be off it before the light went. She said that out loud too, standing on the track, in the ordinary voice, and it was the first want she had said out loud since the fourth of a month that was not a figure, and she stood there afterwards surprised at how fast it came out.

She had not had to want anything in a room. A room gave you the next day and the next day was a wall and a table and a lamp and six figures at the ninth hour, and wanting was a thing you did to the furniture.

Out here the day was going to end in a place she had not chosen and there would be nothing to carry over from it but what she had brought down with her, and there was one lamp that could not be filled and one hand that could not close.

She stood on the track with a dead-near lamp in her left hand and a book of eight leaves against her fourth rib, and behind her the building went up out of sight the way it always had, going on keeping the walls, doing what layers do, without being asked and with not one person left in it.

There were two tracks where the ground had been worn by feet. She had never in seven hundred and eighty-three days of being in that building looked at either of them from the outside, and she had never once in nineteen years been outside this building for a reason that was not the next day.

One of them went up.

She went down the one that was going down, and it took her the first hour to work out that it was not getting any lighter, and she could not tell whether that meant the weather or meant she had gone the wrong way, and there was not one person on it to ask.

She walked until the light had come up to grey and then white, and she said the thing she had come out of the building for out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice, at a turning in the track where there was room to stand up straight.

“I want to be off it before the light goes. That is what I came down here for and I am not going to let go of it on a slope because the going is harder than the room was.”
