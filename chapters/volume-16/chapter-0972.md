# The Plainest Place

She worked out where she was going to say it for two days before she worked out who was going to be there.

She told herself on the first of those two days that it was because the place mattered more than the people. On the second of them she worked out, standing, in the ordinary voice, at a wall, that that was not true, and had never been true, and was the kind of thing she said to a road to get a day to go by.

So she did it the right way round, on the twenty-second of the month beginning three thousand and one hundred and thirty-four, out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice, at a gap in a low wall.

“Who is it for.”

## Three Ways

She took them one at a time and put them down on the ground the way she puts figures down.

One person. She had a person in mind and the person was about forty and sold oil at a stall at the bottom of the pass and had asked her a question on the seventh of the month that was still in her head.

It is out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice. A refusal does not need an audience. A container with nothing in it is not a container, and she had said that standing, in the ordinary voice, before she had bound a single person, and she had said it about a person and not about a sentence, and both of those were true.

And then she said the other half of it out loud, which is the half that finished it.

If she says nine words to one woman at a stall then that woman carries them for the rest of her life. She cannot take them back. She cannot ask that woman whether they went in. And in nine counties there is not one person in the world she is allowed to ask about it.

So: not one person.

All four hundred. She cannot assemble them and she is not going to pretend she can. She cannot be met where she is expected. She cannot follow a name she is given. There is no office and no list and no message and there are about nine of them within calling of a road in one county and the other three hundred and ninety-one are out there somewhere in nine counties doing whatever they are doing this morning.

So: not four hundred.

## The Part Of It She Could Not Say

There was a fourth way and she put it on the ground with the other three and then stood over it for about a count of a hundred, and it was the way she wanted.

Say it and find out.

That is what a person does. A person builds a thing and says it out loud in a room with other people in it and finds out. She had been doing it for twenty-nine days, three sentences at a time, four hundred times, and every single one of those had been in front of somebody who was going to have to live with it.

She is not brave. She has said so out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice, on the twenty-eighth of a month, and she meant it.

But the sentence is not a thing she can try on. The fourth term means it is said standing, in her own voice, first, before it is a line anywhere, and she has used that rule four times in nineteen years and every one of them was on a thing she was certain of, and this one goes into nine hundred years of a record whether it is right or not and she will not find out by finding out.

So there was no fourth way. There was one place and one hour and whoever was standing at it.

## What Was Left

And then what was left was standing in front of people, and she said that out loud too, at the gap in the wall, on the twenty-second of a month, and it came out of her in about three words because she had already got it.

“In front of them.”

Not in front of four hundred. In front of whoever is on that road.

And she said the next part standing, with her hands where she could see them, empty, and she said it slowly, because she wanted it to be plain and not because she was afraid.

“This is not a congregation. Not one person is sent anywhere and not one person has a place in it and there is no name for it and there is no one in charge of it and there is nothing to bring and nothing to wear. I am going to stand in one place on one day and say nine words out loud in the ordinary voice, and whoever has come down that road is going to hear it if they want to stand there.”

## Why

“Because I bound them first.”

She said that at about the eleventh hour and it was the real answer and she had known it was the real answer for about a count of two hundred and had been avoiding it.

She also said the second half of it, which she did not have to, and which she said because she had worked out at about the fourth hour that if she did not say it out loud on the twenty-second she would say something worse on the thirtieth.

“A container with nothing in it is not a container. One person is not enough weight for it. And the reason I am not saying it to one person is not that I do not trust the four hundred. It is that one pair of hands is the whole of it, and I have watched my own mouth go flat over ten days and I am not putting nine words into one pair of hands and calling that weight.”

“The price of it is on the record. I do not know what it costs me. Not one person has sent you and nothing is done to you and I am the only one who binds you. That is what four hundred people said yes to and that is all any of them were ever told.

“And the record is for a sentence. I said that to a woman at a stall on the seventh of this month in front of nine people and not one of them heard it, because there is no road to the other three hundred and ninety-one, and because I have been standing on a road for twenty-nine days saying things to strangers about what I am doing and never once said it to anybody who had actually said yes to me.”

## The Thing She Got Wrong

She tried it out loud on a carter who was passing at about the fifth hour, because that was the only rehearsal available and she had four days of a sentence in her head and a mouth that could not be relied on to say it twice the same.

It came out wrong.

Not badly wrong. Wrong at the front. She got to *I was told* in about two words and had left out the two words in front of it, which are the whole of it, and she heard it happen about half a second after it had already happened.

“I am sorry,” she said, standing, in the ordinary voice, to the carter, who had stopped, and who said it was nothing and went on up the road.

She was not sorry to the carter. She was sorry out loud because she gets it wrong in front of people and says so in the open where they can hear it, which is a thing she has done since the thirtieth of the month beginning three thousand and seventy-four and which is the only correction there has ever been.

## What She Owes Them First

And then the part that had been standing in the middle of everything since the seventh of the month came forward, and it was the one she had not been looking at.

She was going to say nine words out loud in front of people who had said yes to three sentences about oil.

She had to tell them what the record was for first. Standing, in the ordinary voice, out loud, before it is a line anywhere, before she said the nine words, and it had to be the plainest words she had and she had just used the plainest words she had on a woman at a stall on the seventh of a month and about nine people had heard it.

So she built it that afternoon, out loud, to a road, standing, the way she had built nine words on the fourth of a month out of a stone and a carter and a boy with pigs.

And she found out that the hard part was not the walls and was not the ceiling and was not what it might do to the world.

The hard part was that she could not tell them what it would do to them, and she was going to have to stand in front of about nine people at a gap in a wall and say, in the ordinary voice, that she did not know, on a subject that they had said yes to.

She said that out loud twice, to the road, to check it came out the same, and it came out the same both times, and it was the worst sentence she had ever built, and she was going to say it on the thirtieth.

## The Day

Then she set the day, standing, at the gap, out loud, in the ordinary voice, because she has never once in nineteen years said a thing she has not said standing first.

“The last day of this month. The ninth hour. The crossing where these two roads go, at the gap in the wall.”

That is the thirtieth of the month beginning three thousand and one hundred and thirty-four, and it was eight days from that wall, and she had said the three parts of it standing and in the plainest words she had and it went into the keeping before anybody else had heard a word of it.

She put the lamp down on the wall and left it there, because she was not going to be holding anything on the thirtieth, and then she said the last of it out loud to the road.

“I am going to tell them first what it is for and what it might do to them and that I do not know, and I am going to say that to about nine people on a road, and three hundred and ninety-one of them are going to hear it from whoever happens to be standing next to them.”
