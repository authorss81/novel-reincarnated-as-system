# It Is Not Mine to Make

She stood up at the near end of the table on the twenty-ninth of the month beginning two thousand nine hundred and fifty-four, with nothing in either hand, three fingers and a thumb on each, her right arm hanging open at the elbow for six hundred and eighty-eight days, and she stood because standing is for saying.

She had had three days to do it and had not done any of them.

“I am not going to stop you,” she said.

She said it standing, in the ordinary voice, in the channel where the thing that makes the sentences reads, first in her own voice before it was a line anywhere, and she felt it go out the way everything she had ever said in nineteen years had gone out, one sentence at a time, plain, and it did not come back.

He did not say anything for a long time.

## What She Said Next

She stood at the table and let the silence be a silence and did not put anything in it and did not take anything out of it, and she could hear him inside her head not speaking, which she had learned two months ago to tell apart from being gone, and it was the second and not the first.

“Say something,” she said.

“You have just done the thing I asked you to be able to do,” he said. “Give me a moment to be in it.”

“No,” she said. “You do not get a moment. If I give you a moment you will put something kind in it and I will have to take the kindness out of it before I can finish the sentence. I am saying it because I have to say it and I am going to say why once and you are not going to argue me out of it, because you have not asked for this and neither have I.”

“All right,” he said.

## Twice She Had Stopped Him

“It is not mine to make.”

“You do not have the standing to say that,” he said, after a while. “That is not me arguing you out of it. That is me telling you what is true so that you know what you are standing in.”

“I know what I am standing in,” she said. “I have stood in it twice and I know exactly what it is. On the third of the month beginning two thousand eight hundred and ninety-four I stopped you taking the small share and you went still for a count of two hundred and then you started again.”

She put her hands flat on the table edge and did not look down at them.

“On the sixteenth of the month beginning two thousand nine hundred and twenty-four I stopped you coming to me in the cells and you went still for a count of a hundred and then you started again.”

“You remember the counts.”

“I count everything. I have counted everything since I was ten years old and there is nothing in this I do not keep a figure for.”

“I did not know I went still,” he said. “I did not know there was a count.”

## The One She Did Alone

“You were still,” she said. “And I could hear it, and I could tell the difference between a man who is thinking and a man who has gone, and that is nine years of the only thing I have ever been able to do well. I can stop you. I have done it twice and I know the shape of it and I could do it again standing at this table before the lamp went out.”

“There is one,” she said, “and it is the reason, and I am going to say it once.”

He said nothing.

“On the seventh of the month beginning two thousand seven hundred and forty-four I wrote a refusal on a sheet with my own hand, and on the first of the month beginning two thousand seven hundred and seventy-four I sent it down the second stair in a cloth with forty boards on the top of it,” she said, “nine hundred and four steps, and it went out into the bound for everybody, and it was mine, and not one living soul had asked me to write it and not one living soul was asked whether I would.”

She put her hands flat on the table edge.

“You were inside my head when I wrote it. You had been inside my head for four years. And I did not ask you whether I should send it and I did not tell you what was in it before I sent it, and it cost you as much as anything that has ever been done in this keeping, and it is the best thing I have ever done and it is the only decision I have ever made on somebody else’s behalf without asking them.”

“That was different.”

## What He Said

“I know it was different,” she said. “I am not saying it was the same. I am saying I have done it once and it was two hundred and eight days ago and it went nine hundred and four steps and it cannot be called back, and it is the only piece of evidence I have got about myself, and I am not putting my hand on it again tonight for a man who has not asked me to.”

“I want you to stop me,” he said.

“I know you do.”

“I want it more than I want the four lines and the name and the work. I want you to stand there and say no and be right and have done it. That is what I have wanted since you asked me what I wanted on the eighth.”

“I am not going to do it.”

“Why.”

“Because you have not asked me to.”

He was quiet for a count of a hundred.

“That is not an argument,” he said. “That is the thinnest thing you have ever said to me and I have had nine years of them.”

“It is not thin,” she said. “It is a fact and it is the only fact in this room that has anything under it. In nineteen years of a voice in my head you have never once asked me for anything, and the reason is not a limit and I found that out on the eighth, it is you.”

She did not raise her voice. There was nothing to raise it with.

“And on the twenty-sixth you told me you are showing me the whole of the bargain on purpose so that I can stop you, and stopping a man who has not asked to be stopped is not stopping him. It is deciding for him. And I have spent nineteen years refusing to have decisions made for me and I am not going to do the last one for you, and I would rather be wrong about that than be the woman who did it.”

## What She Gave Up By Doing It

“I am going to tell you what this costs me and you are going to let me say it once.”

“Say it.”

“I cannot un-know that you said yes to me on the eighth,” she said. “Not today. Not on the thirtieth. Not ever. That was twenty-one days ago and I put it in my own mouth standing at this table and it is in the record in my voice. Whatever I decide between now and the day I die, I will always be a woman who was asked what a man inside her head wanted and was told, and there is no finding in the world that takes that out.”

“That is not a cost.”

“It is the whole of what I have left,” she said, “because the second thing is this. After the seventh of this month there will be no version of this where I did not know.”

She looked at the register under her hand and it did not move.

“If you say it and it goes wrong, I will have been asked at the ninth hour on the twenty-ninth what a man inside her head wanted and been told, and I said yes to him about it standing in the ordinary voice in front of the thing that made him. That is the paper. That is what there is.”

He did not answer for a count of a hundred.

“You have thought about this properly,” he said. “That is the first time anybody has ever said no to me and made it this frightening.”

“I have thought about it for three days,” she said. “That is what the three days were for. You had three days as well and I do not know what they were for and I have not asked.”

“I was deciding whether to tell you the date,” he said. “I have told you the date.”

“Because I made you.”

## It Is Not Mine to Make

“No,” he said, and it came out of him without any weight on it at all. “Because I have wanted you to be standing at this table when I do it for three days and for six hundred years, and I am tired of turning things.”

She stood at the table in the light the boy’s oil had given her and did not soften it and did not look for the way out of it and did not find, afterwards, that she had been right, because she had not been right or wrong. She had done a thing. That was all it was.

“You told me on the twenty-sixth that kindness would be letting me keep not knowing,” she said. “This is the other side of the same thing and I am telling you what it is so that you have it in your half the way I have it in mine. It is not mine to make.”

She put both hands flat on the table edge, one over the other, the way she had put them on it every morning since the eighth.

“Not because I am frightened of what happens if I make it and not because I think I would get it right. Because there are two of us in this and one of us has to be able to say that he was not stopped.”

He did not answer for a while and she stood there and let him not answer it.

“Three days,” he said at last. “I am not going to pretend that is nothing. Say the rest of it.”

“There is no rest of it. I said the whole of it and you asked me for the whole of it and you have got it.”

“No,” he said. “Say what you are going to do instead.”

She stood at the table with her hands on the register and found the seventh cut with her thumb, out of habit, in the dark place behind her eyes, and then took her hand off it.

“I am going to sit down,” she said, “and I am going to be here tomorrow, and I am not going to leave you alone in here with it.”

“That is the worst thing you have ever done to me,” he said, “and I would not have stopped you from doing it.”

She sat down at the near end of the table, because standing was for saying and the saying was done. The lamp stood on the stone beside her with a boy’s oil in it and a flame on it that she did not put there. The register lay under her hand with everything she had read in it and the offer standing open at the top of a tower until it is answered or withdrawn.

Eight flights and a stair below her the room was running on firsts without her and would run on tomorrow, and the day after that, and not one person in the world knew that anything had been said at the top of a tower at about the ninth hour on the twenty-ninth, and the man who had wanted one thing since he was nineteen was inside her head with his mouth shut for the first time in nineteen years, and she had not stopped him.
