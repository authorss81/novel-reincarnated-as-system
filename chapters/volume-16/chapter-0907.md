# The Door He Cannot Close

Ten days.

That was how long he had been showing her, and he had not once said that he was doing it, and she had not once asked, and both of those had been true at the same time in a room at the top of a tower where there had been no light in it for nine days.

She stood at the near end of the table on the twenty-sixth of the month beginning two thousand nine hundred and fifty-four with her hand flat on the register and she asked it standing, in the ordinary voice, before anything else that morning.

“Why are you telling me.”

## What He Meant by It

“Because I am showing you on purpose,” he said, “and I have been since the eighth, and I want you to be able to stop me.”

She stood very still in the dark with the words going into her one at a time.

“Say the whole of it,” she said. “You have had nine days of practice at not saying things and I have got very good at finding them. Say the whole of it or I will find it.”

He was quiet for a count of forty.

“I took Announcement on the nineteenth of the month beginning two thousand eight hundred and thirty-four, on purpose,” he said, “and I did not ask you, and I cannot undo it. The price of it is named once and it is that I can be anywhere but inside you and you can hear my half.”

He said the date the way he said every date. Flat, plain, exact, and it went into her and sat down where his dates always sat down, in the place where nine years of figures had taught her that when he gave a date she could build on it.

“That is the whole of it,” she said.

“No,” he said. “That is the front of it. I took it and I paid it and it has been paid every day since, and the paying is this. I cannot close a door in my own half. I have no door. I tried.”

“You tried when.”

“On the twenty-eighth of the same month,” he said, “and on the nineteenth of the next, and once on the ninth of the month after that, and it did not work any of the three times and I have not tried since.”

“Why did you stop trying.”

## What She Had Heard Without Knowing

“Because on the third attempt I found out that trying is what makes it audible,” he said. “I went at it and the going showed through and you heard it, and you asked me at the sixth hour what I was doing, and I said nothing, and I have not been able to be anything in here that you cannot hear since that morning.”

She stood at the table and put her hand flat on the stone and took it in the way she took everything he gave her, which was fully and without deciding in advance.

Nine years. Nine years of a voice in her head at ten, twelve, sixteen, nineteen, and every year of it she had believed the room was hers with him in it. Nine years of guessing at him, and she had never once known that the guessing was not guesswork on her side but a door that would not shut on his.

“You have been readable to me since the twenty-third of the month beginning two thousand eight hundred and ninety-four,” she said, “and that is two months and three days.”

“Two months and three days,” he said. “I have had it longer than that.”

“Why are you telling me this now and not two months and three days ago.”

“Because two months and three days ago you did not know the offer was on the table,” he said, “and telling you before the offer would have been telling you to fear something you had not been asked about. You have had the offer in front of you for nine days. Now it is not a fear. Now it is a decision and the decision is yours and I would rather you made it seeing.”

She stood in the dark and had to put both hands on the table edge.

“Kindness would have been letting me keep not knowing,” she said.

## The Third Attempt

“Yes,” he said. “That is exactly what it would have been, and I have been doing it to you for two months and six days and I am tired of it and you are the only person in the world I can stop doing it to, which is why it stopped when it stopped.”

She went back three days of it and stopped him at the end of it.

“The third attempt. You said the ninth of the month after that. What did I hear.”

“I do not know what you heard.”

“You were trying to be quiet. That is the one thing I have never been able to be wrong about, because I have been listening to you try to be quiet for nine years and it is a sound. What was it like.”

He was quiet for a long time, which she let him have, because this one was his and not hers.

“It was like shouting with my hands over my own mouth,” he said. “There is no mouth. There is the channel and the channel is where everything you feel goes through on the way to me, and when I went at it, everything you felt came up at me at once. Every day you had ever had.”

She stood in the dark where she was and did not move.

“The stair and the ninth and the boy and the arm and the lamp and the day I took the bill and the day I told you about the arm and the day you sent a sheet down nine hundred and four steps without asking me. All of it, at once, because I had put my weight on the one door and the door was you.”

She stood in the dark and had to put her hand out and find the edge of the table with it.

“So the price of not being able to shut you out,” she said, “is that when you try, I feel everything I have ever felt.”

“Yes.”

“Nine years,” she said. “Nine years of that has been going on and I have never once felt the door.”

## Oil on the Stair

“No,” he said. “You have never once felt it because I gave up on the third attempt, and I gave up on it because I found out that the hiding was costing you more than the thing I was hiding, and I decided in about a count of forty that I would rather be heard by you than quiet inside your own head. That was nineteen years ago and I have never regretted it and it is still the worst decision anybody has ever made about me.”

Feet came up the stair while he was saying it, slow and laden, stopping every few treads, and they did not stop at the twelfth and they did not stop at the last landing and they came up into a room that had no light in it and stopped inside it.

She knew who it was by the stopping. She had learned it on the fourth of the month beginning two thousand nine hundred and twenty-four and had not needed to learn it again.

The boy stood in the dark four paces inside the door with two lamps in his hands and the oil black on his fingers to the wrist, and he could not see her and she could not see him, and neither of them could see the table.

“I have brought the oil up,” he said. “There is a lamp out on the table at the top.”

He said it the way a working man says a thing, to the room, once, without asking anything. It went out of him and down into the traffic the way everything said in this building went out and down, one sentence at a time, plain, and it came through her and arrived in the place where his sentences arrived, and she felt it land and she did not have anywhere to put it.

She had been in this building nine years and this was the first sentence she had ever heard from anybody except Yuan that was not part of the record.

“Yes,” she said. “There is a lamp out on this table. Put the oil on the stone beside my hand and go down.”

He set it down where her voice had come from and his fingers brushed the edge of her hand on the way in, black with oil, and he did not stay and he did not ask and he went back down the stair the way he had come, slow and laden, stopping every few treads.

## The Decision She Made at the End of It

She stood in the dark with a jar of oil against her hip that he had made himself and could not buy, and inside her head a man who had just told her he was showing her the whole of the bargain on purpose so that she could stop him had gone absolutely quiet.

“That is the first sentence from anybody but you that I have heard in this building in nine years,” she said.

“Do not make it into something it is not,” he said. “He came up with oil and put it on the stone where your voice came from and went back down. That is the whole of what he did.”

“Then I will not make it into anything,” she said, and she did not make it into anything.

She got the wick lit on the third try, with her three fingers and a thumb and a piece of cloth off the end of her own sleeve, and the flame went up off the oil a boy made in a building with no store in it, and the room came back, and the table was there with no wear in it, and the register was there with everything she had read in it, and the place at the far side had nothing worn at all.

She looked at it for a while with light in the room for the first time in nine days.

“I am not going to tell you tonight whether I am going to stop you,” she said.

He did not answer.

“That is not a withholding and it is not a trick and it is not me keeping a silence the way I kept yours on the seventh,” she said. “It is the first time in nineteen years that I have made a decision and not said why, and I have found out this evening that I do not have the why yet, and I would rather hold the decision than hand you a reason I have not finished.”

“I know,” he said. “I have been listening for it since I said it, and there is no why in there, and that is the first good thing about tonight.”

She stood at the near end of the table in a room with light in it for the first time in nine days, with everything he wanted read out to his face and standing open on a register in front of her, and he had just told her in plain words that he was showing her all of it on purpose so that she could stop him, and she had asked him why and been told, and she was not going to say tonight.
