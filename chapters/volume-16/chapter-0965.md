# The Man Who Came Down The Track

She had not slept and the frost came up out of the ground about an hour before the light, which she had not expected, and it was the first of the month beginning three thousand and one hundred and thirty-four.

## The Fifth Hour

It came up under her at about the fifth hour and she put her hand flat on the earth and let the whole of it come.

A bundle of boards, corded twice, going up nine hundred and four steps of a stair that did not stop at nine.

That was the second first of a month she had felt come up outside a mountain. The first one had been on the first of the month beginning three thousand and one hundred and four, on a made road at a crossing where two roads crossed, with about nine people watching and not one of them able to feel a thing.

The boards went up. The stair took them. The room at the top took them in and cut them and they went somewhere, and not one person in the world stood under any of it.

She got up off the wet stones and walked up the track as far as the point where the track stops being a road you would expect to be on, and she stopped there and waited, because she had decided during the night that if there was going to be anything to see on a first of a month she was going to be awake for it, and there was nothing to see, and she had got that answer about four hundred counts before the light.

## About The Sixth Hour

A man came down the track.

He was coming down fast, the way a man comes down after four hundred years of going up, and he was about forty, and he had nothing in either hand.

He had a right forearm that was not the size of his left. It was the same length and it was thinner the whole way from the elbow to the wrist and it did not come when he put his weight on the rail of the track, and there was a place at the top of it where the skin had gone the colour of the boards.

He saw her standing there and stopped, and then came the rest of the way down, because he had seen the lamp before he had seen her.

“You are up here early,” he said.

“It came up at the fifth hour.”

“Did not,” he said. “It went at the fifth hour.”

That was the whole of what he said about it. Then he went past her about ten paces and stopped and turned round.

“You are the one that has been on that road.”

## What She Asked Him

She asked him his name standing, in the ordinary voice, in the frost, and he gave it.

“Han Liang.”

“How long have you been carrying that up.”

He thought about it for longer than she had expected, and she understood that he was not about to answer and was about to answer.

“I count them,” he said. “There has never been one late and there has never been one that did not come. I have been counting since I was small enough to be lifted onto the bottom step, and I am at four thousand eight hundred.”

Ning Yan did the whole of it standing, in the ordinary voice, in the ordinary way, and it took her about a count of thirty.

“Four thousand eight hundred firsts of a month. Thirty days to a month. Twelve months to a year.”

“Four hundred years,” Han Liang said. “I worked that out a long time ago and I have never had anybody to say it to.”

## The Forearm

She asked him about the arm, because she had four hundred people in her arithmetic and every one of them had been asked about the price before they answered and she was not going to do it differently to a man on a track.

She said it standing, in the ordinary voice, out loud, before it was a line anywhere.

“The price of it is on the record. I do not know what it costs me. Not one person has sent you and nothing has been done to you and I am the only one who binds anybody.”

Han Liang listened to all of that on a frosty road at about the sixth hour in the morning.

“No,” he said.

And then he held up the arm.

“I have been in it four hundred years,” he said. “You are asking me about the price of going into something I have been inside since before I could walk.”

## The Thing She Wanted To Say

That was the moment she found the thing she had been carrying since the twentieth of a month on a hillside, standing, with a book of eight leaves against her fourth rib.

He did not know.

Not because the keeping had kept it from him, which it had not, because it had never told him anything at all. He did not know because there was not one person in the world to tell, and no question had ever come down that track, and not one person had ever come up it who had a reason.

He had carried four thousand eight hundred bundles up nine hundred and four steps and not one of them had been late, and he had got a forearm out of it, and there was not a single thing in his life, standing on any road, that had ever asked him a question about what he wanted.

She wanted to say it. She wanted it more than she had wanted anything since she came off the mountain.

And she stood in the frost about two paces off a man of forty with the wrong forearm and she did not say it, because she had nothing at all to give him.

She had no word for the next bundle and no knowledge of whether there would be one and no way of finding out. A woman who says “this is the last one” to a man who has counted four thousand eight hundred of them is a woman guessing out loud about something that matters to a person.

She does not say numbers she cannot derive.

## What He Said Instead

He looked up the track for a while and then he said, “Are you going to be on that road.”

“Yes.”

“For how long.”

“I do not know.”

Han Liang nodded, and it was not a bored nod. It was the nod of a man who has heard that answer before and has never once been given a straight one and has gone on coming up the track anyway.

“My forearm does not do anything,” he said. “I have asked about it three times in my life. The answer was that it is on the record.”

He said it without any weight at all.

“Then it is not mine to take off,” he said, “and neither is yours, and that is a thing I have had forty years to get used to and I have.”

Then he went down the track at the speed he had come down it, and the frost was coming off the stones, and Ning Yan stood at the side of a track with the lamp in her left hand and did not follow him and did not call after him.

## The Thing She Wanted To Say And Did Not

She was still standing there about a count of two hundred after he had gone out of sight.

There was no office and no report and no figure and no line anywhere in nine hundred years that had ever told a man what he was carrying or what it had taken out of him or that there would not be another one.

And she was the only person in the world who had ever wanted to say it out loud to him.

And she had wanted it, and she had not said it, and there was no rule in nineteen years that had anything to do with that at all, and she was going to have to do without one.

## What She Did With His Name

Her hand was shaking and it was the hand with three fingers and a thumb on it and it was not the open arm, and she had been out on the stones all night and she had not eaten since the first of the month of the market before last.

She stood on the track in the frost and said his name out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice, once.

“Han Liang.”

It went into the keeping, because everything she said standing went into the keeping, and she had not bound him and was never going to bind him and was not going to ask him again, and a name said once on a track at the sixth hour of the first of a month is in nine hundred years of a record and is not a binding and cannot be taken off him and cannot be given to anybody else.

That was all she had. It was less than the three sentences and it was the only thing on offer.

Then she went back down the track to the crossing in the frost and stood at the gap in the wall in the plainest place she knew, and there was no market and no queue and about nine people on a road who had heard the word from the bottom of a hill.

And there was one thing left over from the sixth hour of that morning that she had not put anywhere.

A man of forty came down a track with four thousand eight hundred bundles behind him and a forearm that does not do anything, and asked her how long she was going to be on that road, and she said she did not know, and that was the only false thing she said to him all morning, because she had known exactly how long it was going to be for nineteen years.
