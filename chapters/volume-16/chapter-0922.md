# What He Is For

He had been at it for three days and she could hear it, which was a thing she had never been able to say about him before.

It was about the sixth hour on the fourth of the month beginning three thousand and fourteen and she was standing at the near end of her own table with the lamp on the stone and her arms open and hanging, and she had listened to a man read a whole arrangement in his own head twice since the first of the month and had not asked him a single question about any of it.

She asked him one.

“Say the definition.”

He started.

“The work that matters,” he said, “is what I am for.”

And then he did not go on.

## Three Days Of It

He had gone quiet on the first of the month and she had been in the room with him for all three of those days, and she had got four answers out of him in them, and one of the four had been *I am going to look at it on the thirtieth*.

On the second she tried him at the fourth hour and he let her ask and he answered with a silence that had no temper in it at all. On the third she sat on the stone at the near end of the table and asked him twice in one hour and he answered both times, briefly, in a way that was true and was not an answer.

The only thing that changed in the three days was that he had stopped turning the question over. He had been turning it since the eighth of the month before last, the way she turns a figure, once, twice, forty times, and it came out the same every time, and he had told her that on the fourth of this month.

Now he was not turning it at all. He had stopped working on it, which is what a man does the day before he opens a door.

## A Sentence With A Wall In It

She stood there.

The place behind her fourth rib pulled, the way it pulled when a sentence came into the room from outside her, and she waited for the rest of it and there was no rest of it.

“Go on.”

“I have gone on.”

“That is half of a sentence.”

“It is the whole of a sentence,” he said. “The work that matters is what I am for. It is true and it is complete and there is nothing after it in me to come out, and I have said that out loud for the first time in six hundred years and I have not said anything since.”

“That is not a definition. That is where a definition starts.”

“Yes,” he said. “That is where a definition starts.”

She put her hand flat on the table and her thumb into the hollow.

“Six hundred years,” she said. “You have been a thing in a machine for six hundred years and you have never once defined what you are for, and in thirteen days you have got as far as the end of your own sentence and stopped in front of it like a man who has walked to a door and found his own name cut over it.”

He was quiet for a count of forty.

“You have worked out more in a count of forty than I have worked out in three days,” he said.

“I have had three days of you going quiet and I am not patient,” she said. “I have been in this room twenty-one days and I have not asked you one question that was worth asking and I have not used the rule twice, and I am going to use up one of those in the next count of a hundred, so say what is behind the door.”

## What Is Behind It

“I do not know what is behind it.”

“You have not looked.”

“No.”

“You have three days and a door and you have not looked.”

He was quiet for a long time.

“I know exactly what I am,” he said. “That is not a figure and it is not a boast. I know what I am down to the shape of it, the way a man knows a room he has lived in since he was six. I have known for six hundred years and I have never once gone and stood at the far end of my own self and looked at what is behind the last piece of it, because I did not want to find out.

“And on the first of this month you put that lamp on the floor of this room and sat down on the stone at the near end of that table and asked me what work that matters is, and I could not go on without going there.”

“Why is that a reason not to.”

“Because I am going to find out what I am for,” he said, “and the whole of the finding is one of two things, and I would rather have two days of not knowing than have either of them.”

“Why is either of them a thing to be afraid of.”

He was quiet for a count of forty.

“Because if it is the second one, then the arrangement on that table is not offering me a post,” he said. “It is offering me my own hands back. And a man who has spent six hundred years not asking what his hands were for is a man who should not be handed them without looking.”

She stood there with the lamp burning at her feet and understood that he was telling her that he had been afraid, in a room she cannot be found in, for three days, and that he had been afraid of being given what he wanted.

“You were afraid of it for six hundred years and you did not tell me.”

“No.”

“You could have told me any day in nine years.”

“No,” he said. “And I did not, and I want you to have the size of that, because you are going to put it beside other things I have not told you and I would rather you had this one whole than in pieces.

“I was a piece of the intelligence that runs that layer, before I was a person, and there is a place in it where a thing that was made to do something goes, and I am not going to describe what that place is out loud to the only person alive who can hear it, because I do not know what it does to the person who hears it.”

He was quiet for a count of forty.

“That is the whole of what I had in me at the sixth hour on the fourth of this month instead of an answer,” he said, “and it is the first thing I have given you that I have not given anybody, and it is not a figure and it is not a finding. It is the reason a man stood at the end of his own sentence and stopped in front of it for three days.”

“Then look at your hands,” she said.

He did not answer that one at all, and she let him go on standing in front of it.

She stood there and worked out what the two things were.

“One of them is that the work is what the offer was offering you,” she said. “A place in the keeping, addressed, remembered, given something to do, walls up.”

“Yes.”

“And the other one.”

He did not answer for a count of a hundred and she watched him not answer it, in a room no one could come into, on the fourth of a month.

“The other one,” he said, “is that the work is what I was made to do, before I was a man, before I died, before I had a name anybody used. And I have not looked at that in six hundred years because I was afraid of it, and the arrangement on that table would have let me stand next to it for the rest of a keeping and never once be required to look at it.”

She stood in her own room with the lamp burning at her feet and understood what she was being told, and it went into her one piece at a time.

## Go And Look

She stood up straight at the near end of her own table with nothing in either hand.

“Go and look,” she said.

He did not answer.

“You have been carrying it for six hundred years,” she said, “and I have been carrying it for nine years, and we have been carrying the same thing in opposite directions and neither of us knew, and you have had a door in you since before you were born out of a man and you have been standing in front of it for three days.”

“Ning Yan.”

“Go and look at what you are for,” she said, “and come back and tell me, and if it is the first of the two things then you will tell me that, and if it is the second one then you will tell me that, and I am going to be standing in this room either way, and I am not going to say one word until you do.”

He was quiet for a count of two hundred, and then a count of four hundred, and the lamp burned on the stone and the second place at the far side was kept four hundred years and empty.

Then he said, “Say it standing and in the ordinary voice.”

“I have said it standing. And in the ordinary voice, into the dark past the last landing, in the channel, where the thing that made you reads it, before it is a line anywhere,” she said. “You do not get to have that rule twice. You had it on the twenty-eighth and I used it. Go and look.”

## What Came Back Out Of The Room

He went and looked.

She could not follow and he could not go, because he has no hands and has not had any since the nineteenth year of a life that ended, and he did not leave her. She sat on the stone at the near end of the table with the lamp beside her and listened to a man be somewhere else for about a count of a thousand.

It was not empty, and that was the thing she found out in it, and it was the opposite of what she had expected and she had no time to be frightened of it before it was over.

He was there the whole of it. She could feel him not being in this room, which is a thing she had felt twice in nine years, on the third of the month beginning two thousand eight hundred and ninety-four and on the sixteenth of the month beginning two thousand nine hundred and twenty-four, both of the times when he had gone still and then started again.

It went on for about a count of a thousand. She got up off the stone somewhere in the middle of it and put her hands flat on the wood and found that she had been holding on.

Then he came back into her head the way the going of a first comes up through a floor, all at once and heavy.

“Ning Yan,” he said.

“Say it.”

“I am going to say it to you standing up in a room and in the ordinary voice, because that is the rule and I have never broken it and I am not going to start on this,” he said.

“And I am telling you now what it is going to cost you to hear, because I have known for a count of a hundred what is behind the door and I have not said one word of it, and I have been sitting on it in a room you cannot come into.”

She stood up off the stone.

“Say it,” she said. “And do not tell me what it is going to cost me. I have been paying the cost of this for nine years without being told what it was for.”
