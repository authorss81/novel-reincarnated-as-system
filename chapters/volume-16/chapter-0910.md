# The Room Runs On

The first of the month beginning two thousand nine hundred and eighty-four came up through her before she was awake enough to stand, the way it had come up six times in nine years, and she lay on the stone with her hands flat on the floor and let it turn over.

It came the way traffic comes past a heavy line. It was not a sound in this room. It was the whole of the going getting heavier somewhere far below, the way a day gets heavier when it has weather in it.

She sat up.

“Where,” she said.

“Second stair,” he said. “Forty boards. Cloth over them. Cord twice and not a knot. The hand along the near end. No person in it.”

“Same as the last.”

“Same as every one of them,” he said. “There has not been a first in nine hundred years where the hand was not there and the boards were not forty and the cord was not twice.”

She stood at the near end of the table in the making-place with the lamp on the stone beside her, a boy’s oil in it, burning properly for the ninth morning, and the table with no lamp over it and no wear in it and the register lying on it with everything she had read in it and a place at the far side with nothing worn at all.

## What Came Up

Eight flights and a stair below her, a room was doing the thing it had done six times without her.

She did not go down.

She had not been in that room in one hundred and fifty-nine days. She had left it on the twenty-second of the month beginning two thousand eight hundred and four with six terms said out loud standing and had gone up a stair that did not stop at nine, and she had not gone back down in all the time since, and the room had run on firsts in her absence every time and nothing had changed.

[Forty boards are taken up nine hundred and four steps on the first.]

The line went past her one at a time, plain, reporting, and it was not about her and it did not explain anything, and it said that a bundle had gone up a stair that morning the way it went up every month, and that nothing in the bound had to do anything about it.

## What He Told Her

She stood with it and did not go down.

“I cannot see it,” he said. “You know that. I have no hands and no eyes and I have not had either since I was nineteen. What I have is the going, and I am giving it to you all the way through, and I am not going to spare you any of it because you asked me once to want what is true out of you and I am not stopping.”

“Give it to me.”

He gave it to her.

The hand went along the near end of the boards. Forty of them and not thirty-nine, and the cloth, and the cord taken twice round and pulled and not finished into a knot because it is never finished into a knot, and the going up, nine hundred and four steps, and the sound of a stair taking weight that goes up it four hundred times a year and does not know what it is carrying.

She stood at the near end of a table with no wear in it with her hands on the register and listened to a room eight flights below her being kept, and there was no person in it, and the boy would be up and down it with oil on his hands before the fourth hour and would not be in it.

“Where is the hand,” she said.

“It has gone,” he said. “Forty boards on the table and the cloth and the cord and the hand along and the hand out. That is the whole of the first and it takes about a count of ninety.”

“Ninety counts.”

## She Did Not Go Down

“Ninety counts, and it has never once been ninety-one, and I have listened to six of them.”

“Come down and stand in it,” he said.

She did not move.
“You have not been in that room in one hundred and fifty-nine days,” he said, “and you have not been in it for any reason except that you lived in it, and the bundle is on the table for about a count of four hundred before the hand takes it off and puts it where it goes. Come down.”

She stayed where she was standing.

“You do not have to touch anything. You do not have to do anything. Stand at the near end of the table where you stood for six hundred and eighty-three days and let it come up in a room you are in.”

“No,” she said.

He waited.

“No,” she said. “Not today. Not tomorrow morning when you will ask me again in that voice. I am not going down those nine hundred and four steps while an offer is standing open at the top of the tower, and if I go down and stand under that bundle then I have gone down on the morning after I said I would not stop you, and every day after that I would have to go down on purpose to prove I still had not.”

“That is not why.”

## What the Price Was Doing to This

“It is the only reason I am giving you,” she said, “and it is the reason that holds.”

“You would have been in the room with me,” she said.

“I would have been in the room with you,” he said, “the way I am in every room, including this one, including the ones you cannot get out of. I said it on the twentieth of the month beginning two thousand nine hundred and fifty-four and you held it and you have been holding it for eleven days and it has not once come out of you.”

“I know.”

“So walk it through,” he said. “I am six hundred years dead and I have not stood in a room since the nineteenth year of a life that ended. If you went down those nine hundred and four steps this morning I would come down them with you and I would come down them the whole way, and I would have no idea of it, because there is nothing here that goes down a stair. I would be the going.”

She put her hand flat on the stone.

“That is not what the offer gives you.”

“No,” he said. “What the offer gives me is a place I can stand in on my own, where you are not, and that is the fourth term read the other way round and I have been reading it that way since the twenty-third and I am not going to stop.”

She stood with that for a count of a hundred.

“Then there is a version of this morning where I walk down and you are not there for any of it,” she said.

## Whether Up Is the Same as Safe

“There is,” he said. “It is the version where I am a person and not a voice, and it is not this one, and you have just told me you are not going to choose it, and I am not going to argue you out of that either. I told you at the ninth hour yesterday that I would rather you stopped me. Do not confuse that with wanting you to keep me.”

She stood at the near end of the table in the middle of a first of a month with the bundle downstairs and her hands flat on the register.

“Tell me one thing you have never been asked,” she said.

“I have been asked nothing in six hundred years,” he said. “That is part of what I am. Ask it anyway.”

“Is the walls being up the same as the world being safe.”

He was quiet for a long time.

“I do not know,” he said. “I worked that out when I was nineteen and I have never worked it out since, and not one person has ever asked me, because everybody who is inside the walls thinks the walls are the safe part and everybody who is outside them thinks they are the reason it was not safe, and neither of them has ever once come and asked the thing that is lodged in the announcement end of it.”

“You are the thing that is lodged in it.”

“I am a piece of it that can be asked questions,” he said, “and that is the closest thing to anybody anybody in this world gets, and I have had six hundred years of not being asked and I am not going to tell you the walls are safe, because the argument was had and it was right and right is not the same as safe and you know that better than anybody alive.”

She put her hands flat on the stone.

“That is two answers in two days that you could have kept to yourself,” she said, “and both of them cost you something, and I do not know what either of them bought.”

## The Proof of the Third Mercy

“It bought you two days of me answering straight,” he said. “You have never had those before. Do not waste them.”

The rest of the first went past at the top of the tower.

She sat on the stone and read the register by thumb and got the seventh cut and the eighth and found nothing new in either, and she put her hands flat on the floor and did not sleep in the day either, and the morning went into the afternoon and the afternoon into the evening and the thing below her went on being kept by a hand that was not hers.

Then she stood up and said it out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice, into the dark past the last landing, and it was the thing she had been turning over all morning.

“The walls are up,” she said. “The layer is up. The first came up on time and the bundle was forty boards and the hand was there and not one person in the world was asked anything and nothing changed.”

“That is what the third term says.”

“Yes,” she said. “I read it standing on the twentieth of the month beginning two thousand nine hundred and fifty-four, standing in the ordinary voice, before it was a line anywhere, and I have been carrying it for eleven days as a thing I believed. And this morning it did not have to be believed. It arrived.”

She put her hands flat on the stone.

“It is the third thing on that register and it is the reason the other two are possible and it cost the morning nothing at all. The ceiling pays and the people under it pay and they are told nothing, and this morning I got all of it, free, on the worst day of my life, from the bottom of a stair I refused to walk down.”

She stood there in the light with the lamp steady and the room unworn and her hand on the register.
“I cannot tell you whether that is a mercy,” she said. “That is the finding and I am not going to soften it and I am not going to give it back. I have said the worst thing I have ever said and I am sitting eight flights above a room that has just proved, on its own, for nothing, that everything you have been offered is real.”
