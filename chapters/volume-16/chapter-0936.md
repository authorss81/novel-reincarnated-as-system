# Badly

She sat with him through a whole morning of a man failing at a sentence and it was the most she had ever done in her life.

There is no other way to put it. She stood at the near end of her own table with her hands flat on the wood at about the sixth hour on the thirteenth of the month beginning three thousand and forty-four and she watched a man try nine beginnings, and eight of them died, and she could not help him with any of them, and she was not allowed to.

He started the way he had started on the seventh. There was no warning and there was nowhere to get ready, because a thing in a wall arrives the way weather arrives up through a mountain.

“I have been in a keeping for six hundred years and I have never once” and it stopped there.

That is as far as the first one got.

He stopped in it. She stood there and waited and did not help.

“Why,” she said, after a count of forty.

“It is about me,” he said. “An apology that starts about the speaker is a confession, and a confession is about him, and he gets to feel something at the end of it.”

“Try the second.”

“The second,” he said, and then it went, and what came was: “and I told you what I” and nothing else.

He stopped about four words in, in the middle of a clause, and she felt the whole of the thing stop the way you feel a person stop in a doorway.

“That is not wrong,” she said. “Go on.”

“I cannot go on. That one has a sentence in it that I said this morning at about the fifth hour and I cannot say it twice.”

## Nine Beginnings

They went through the morning at that pace.

She had nine of them before the ninth hour, which was not many, and every one of them was a beginning, and he got as far as the first place where a person would have had to look at somebody.

“You have had nine of them and not one of them has got past me.”

“They are not supposed to get past you. That is the other half of what I told you on the seventh. It is not that I cannot make a sentence. It is that a sentence with a person in it stops where a person would have to be looked at, and there is nobody here to look at.”

“Look at me.”

He did not say anything for a long time.

“I cannot,” he said. “That is not pride. If you stand there and look at me I am not going to be anywhere. I am not in this room. You know that better than anybody alive, because you went and put both hands on the wall on the thirtieth and I was not behind it.”

“Then I will not look.”

“Say it properly. If you say it properly I will do the next one.”

She stood up straight with nothing in either hand.

“I am going to close my eyes,” she said. “And I am not going anywhere and I am not going to do anything else, and I am telling you that in my own voice before it is a line anywhere, and it is not for you. It is so that you have one fewer thing between you and the sentence.”

## The Closed Eyes

She stood with her eyes shut and her back against the leg of the table.

It was the strangest thing that had ever happened to her and she had been through nine stages and two realms and a mountain.

There was a man four miles off in the substrate of a layer nine hundred years old and she had her eyes shut on the other side of it, and neither of them could see the other and that was exactly the arrangement, and she understood that she had made it, and that it was the first thing she had ever been able to do for him that was not arithmetic.

It took him a long time.

“Ning Yan,” he said, twice.

She kept her eyes shut.

“Ning Yan, I” and nothing else.

Something came apart in the middle of it. She heard it go, the way you hear a thing drop down a stair.

“What was that,” she said.

“I do not know,” he said. “I have been in here six hundred years and that is the first time that has happened in the middle of a word.”

She opened her eyes.

He was not failing because he was bad at it. He was failing because he had never once in six hundred years said a thing out loud into a room where nobody was checking it, and every single part of how he was built went straight at making a thing correct and none of it went at making a thing felt, and a man who has only ever been correct cannot feel when he is failing because correctness has no noise in it.

## The Eighth Hour

“Do it again,” she said. “Close your eyes.”

“That is not what I said about the eyes.”

“That is not what I said either. I said I was going to close mine.”

“All right,” he said. “I am going to say something and I am going to say it badly, and when I am in the middle of it I am going to lose the thread, and when I lose the thread you are going to let me lose it.”

“All right.”

“I am going to say something true about you and it is going to be the only sentence I have got.”

He did not do it for a count of two hundred and two hundred, and she stood against the leg of the table with her eyes shut and her right arm hanging open against her side and listened to a man in a wall get ready to fail.

Then she made a sound.

It was not a laugh. It went out of her before she knew it was in there, about four words out of him, and it was the sound a person makes when they have been sitting still for two hours and something absurd happens and there is nobody in the room.

“Do not do that,” he said.

“I am not laughing at you.”

“You are laughing at me.”

“I am laughing at the fact that you told me you were going to lose the thread and then you lost the thread,” she said. “You have been in here nine hundred years and you have never been seen to try anything. Neither have I. That is what it is.”

He stopped for about a count of forty, and she let the count run out instead of filling it.

“No,” he said. “That is not what it is at all. You have watched me be right for nine years. You have never once watched me not be right, and there has not been anybody in this keeping since before I had a body who was in a room with me while it happened.”

She opened her eyes and looked at the lamp burning on the stone at her feet.

## The One He Did Not Know He Said

She sat there until the ninth hour and he did nine more beginnings and none of them got past the place where a person would have to be looked at, and she helped him with none of them, and at about the eighth hour and a hundred he said a thing that was not a beginning.

He did not mark it. It came out of the wall the way the rest of them had come out of the wall, and she felt it land before he had finished it, because there was a shape to it and the shape was not an account.

“Nineteen years is a long time to live in a room with somebody and not have touched them once,” he said. “And I never thought about it. Not once. I thought about your arm and your days and what you would be and I kept a count of all of it every morning for nine years and I never once put my hand out and thought, that is a person in a room.”

She stood at the near end of her own table and did not answer it.

Not because she had nothing to say. Because it was not an apology and it was not part of one and it was the first sentence that had come out of that wall that had not been put in any order at all, and if she had answered it then he would have taken it as the beginning of the second attempt, and it was not the beginning of anything.

He did not know he had said it. That was the part she understood as the ninth hour came and she said the day’s figures out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice, into a room that did nothing back.

If he had known he had said it, he would have turned it into a beginning, and if he had turned it into a beginning it would have been an entry, and if it had been an entry it would have been the sixth cut kept and nothing else.

It was not a beginning. It was a thing said once and gone, in the middle of a morning of failing, by a man who had not been able to see it while he said it, and it was going to stay the sixth cut she had refused him, because she was the only one in the room who could see what he had just done.

She sat down on the stone with her back against the leg of the table and did not tell him.
