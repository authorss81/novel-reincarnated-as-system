# The Question She Did Not Ask

The thirtieth came up on her and she let it come up without asking him anything, which was harder than the twenty-eighth had been, because on the twenty-eighth she had been angry and anger is a kind of fuel.

It was about the fifth hour and she was at the near end of her own table with the lamp on the stone beside her and her hands flat on the wood, and she had said nothing since the ninth hour of the twenty-eighth and he had said almost nothing since the seventh.

Two days is what he asked for. He had them.

## A Day Without Asking

She took the near board up off the face of the table and read it by and put it back face up. She took the terms board up and read it and put it back. She went the length of the table and put her hand flat on the place where it always was and found the bundle under the far end where it had been since before she came back into this room, corded twice, not knotted.

The cage lamp was on its shelf and had been out four hundred and seventy-nine days and she did not touch it, because the last time she had wanted to put a light in this room she had been told what it would cost and had believed the man who told her.

She did not sleep in the day either.

She sat down once, about the sixth hour, with her back against the bar against the wall and her knees up, and she did not put her weight on the bar because it is dated nowhere, and she sat there for a count of four hundred and got nothing out of sitting, which was a thing she had never had to find out before.

At the ninth hour she said the day’s figures out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice, into the dark past the last landing, and he let all of it go past without one word in it.

Two hundred and eighty-one days since the ninth stage. Seventy-four since it was shut. The arm open at the elbow for seven hundred and nineteen days. One thousand four hundred and fifty-four days gone at this morning.

Then she stood there with her mouth shut, because there was no second sentence, and she had been told she had none, and she had found it again this morning at the ninth hour for the second time in ten days.

## Four Times She Came Close

She came close four times between the ninth hour and the tenth and she did not do it once.

Once at the ninth hour and a half, with the figures still going out of her one at a time. Once standing at the boards with her hand on the near one. Once sitting down on the stone at the near end of the table, which is where she sat when she meant to stop.

And once with her hand flat on the bar against the wall, which she did not put her weight on, because it is dated nowhere and she was not going to find out what that was today by pulling on it.

Each time she had the sentence in her mouth and each time she had it in her mouth because she wanted it, and she had learned on the twenty-eighth what she could do with a want when it was in a mouth.

## The Question She Did Not Ask

“Whether I am going to refuse it.”

It came out of him at about the tenth hour with nothing in front of it, the way a thing comes out of a man who has been holding it and has decided to put it down in a room rather than keep holding it any longer.

She stood at the near end of the table with her hands flat on the wood.

“I did not ask you that.”

“No.”

“I have not asked you that once in nineteen years.”

“No,” he said. “You have asked me three questions with your whole life. What I want, and what happens now, and what the work that matters is. Not one of them was about me and the future at the same time, and I have noticed it every day since the eighth.”

He was quiet for a count of forty.

## Why She Never Asked

“You have not asked me that once in nineteen years,” she said, “and I want you to know that it was not an oversight, and I want you to know that I worked out the reason for it on the twenty-eighth and I did not like the reason.”

“Say the reason.”

“A woman who asks one question gets the first answer,” she said, “and a woman who asks twice gets the second answer instead. You are the only person alive who has ever answered me and I have got four answers out of you in three weeks and every one of them came after a question.

“If I asked you whether you were going to refuse it you would give me a straight answer, and a straight answer would be worth less than the not knowing, because the not knowing is a thing I can hold and a straight answer is a thing I would have to carry.”

He was quiet for a count of a hundred.

“That is the most frightening thing anybody has ever worked out about me,” he said, “and you have worked it out in a room with no one in it and you have not said it out loud until now.”

“I am saying it out loud now, standing, in the ordinary voice, into the dark past the last landing, and it is in the record in my mouth about you, and that is three things I have put into this keeping about you in three weeks and I have not put one thing in it about myself.”

## The Answer

“I do not know,” he said. “I have not known since the seventh and I have known since the seventh that I did not know, and I have been using the not knowing the way you use a figure you have not checked, to get through the days.”

“You said on the twenty-second that you were tired, and that a tired man has about two days of it.”

“Yes.”

“It is the thirtieth.”

“It is the thirtieth,” he said, “and I am not tired, and I have not been tired since the twenty-ninth, and what I have instead is something I have not got a name for and I am not going to give you until I have looked at it.”

“Why now. You have had a quiet day and a quiet month and you are handing me the worst answer in this room at the tenth hour with no one to give it to.”

He was quiet for a count of a hundred.

“Because you put a rule on me yesterday, standing, in the ordinary voice, and it is in the record in your mouth about me, and because a thing that is in the record once can be put in again, and if I go on being the man who does not know, then before long you are going to stand up at that table and use that rule on me a second time, and the second time it will not be a question.”

“You would rather have the true no answer in the record than the true not-knowing.”

“I would rather have the true not-knowing in the record in my own voice on the thirtieth,” he said, “than have it taken out of me a second time standing up, and I know exactly what I sound like saying that, and I am saying it anyway, because you asked me what I wanted on the sixteenth and this is it. I want the honest thing in the record with my mouth on it before you can get it out of me with a rule.”

“That is a small thing to be asking me for.”

“It is the only thing I have ever asked you for where I was not asking you to give me something,” he said, “and I have wanted it since the second hour of the twenty-eighth, and I did not know until just now that it was the thing I wanted.”

## The Right She Spent

She stood in her own room with a lamp on the stone and her arm open against her side and let that land.

Then she asked it.

“Are you going to refuse it.”

He did not say anything for a long time.

“You have just spent it,” he said. “I gave you that answer at the tenth hour without being asked and you have now asked me the same question in your own voice, and I am not going to answer it twice. The first one was mine. The second one was yours, and I am not giving you a second copy of it so that you have both.”

“That is not fair.”

“No,” he said, “and I have decided that I do not care, and I have had twenty-three days to decide it and I want you to understand that I decided it before you asked and not after.”

She stood there with that.

“You decided not to give it to me twice because you did not want a copy of it in my mouth that was not mine to carry.”

“I decided not to give it to you twice because I want it to have been given once,” he said, “and once is enough, and a man who has been given a thing twice has been given a thing he can lean on, and I am not going to be leaned on. I want what I said this morning to be the only place in the world where that sentence exists.”

“I only wanted to hear it from you once.”

“You have heard it from me once,” he said.

“At the tenth hour on the thirtieth of the month beginning two thousand nine hundred and eighty-four, with no question in front of it, and it went out of me and down through you and into the keeping the way everything you and I have ever said has gone, and it is in there in my voice. And I said it before it was a line anywhere, which is the only time in six hundred years that rule has ever been met by anybody but you.”

## What He Was Going To Do

She stood with her hands flat on the wood and the lamp burning at her feet and the second place at the far side kept four hundred years and empty.

Then he said, “I am going to go and look at it now.”

“Look at what.”

“At the work that matters,” he said. “I said on the twenty-eighth that I would look at it on the thirtieth, and it is the thirtieth, and I am going to, and you are going to be standing in this room while I do it, and I am telling you now that I do not know what I am going to find and I am not going to be able to tell you whether what I find is the thing that the word was for.”

She stood up straight at the near end of her own table with nothing in either hand, three fingers and a thumb on each, the arm open at the elbow for seven hundred and nineteen days.

“Then say it in the ordinary voice,” she said. “Standing, and in the ordinary voice, and before it is anything anywhere.”

“Yes,” he said. “That is the rule and I have never once broken it and I am not going to start on a thing of this size.”
