# All Of Them

She knew at about the fifth hour of the ninth day that it was the ninth day, because she counted, standing at the weir in the ordinary voice at the ninth hour every day and the ninth day is what it was.

There was not one part of her that said the ninth day meant anything.

## Looking For The Last One

She had a thing to do on the ninth day and it was the only thing she had, and she had built it out of nothing over nine days without knowing she was building it.

She had been counting stones.

The quarry terrace above the flat. Nine gaps in the wall line with two posts in each of them. The ring of twenty-two around a bowl. A ring of nine on the far side of that bowl, which she had not been able to reach and had counted off the west rim. A marker at the top of a house on the road east.

Five of them, and five more that had come at her one at a time over nine days.

The Nine Steps Terrace at Lowwater. One at the head of the pass. One at the top of the weir, where the water comes off. One on the made road out of the flat, that she had gone past on the sixth day without stopping. And one at a gateway by the weir that four families use every day and have never once looked at.

Ten places in four counties where the same stone stands with nothing cut on it anywhere.

She had watched eight of them go.

So the ninth day was for the other two.

She took the boy of about seventeen with the boots full of clay, because she wanted one other pair of eyes on it that had not been looking at the world for nine days with a fixed thing in its head.

She did the made road out of the flat first, from about the seventh hour, walking it on the side the carts used, past a gate that four people waved at her from, and there was nothing at it and there had never been anything there that she had ever noticed.

Then she walked back to the weir and out to the gateway at about the eighth hour, which is a stone arch with a field gate hung in it that four families have gone through every day since before she could walk, and she stood under it and put her hand flat on the left-hand pier, which is the same stone as all the rest of them and which was warm.

Nothing there.

Not fallen and not broken and not turned over. The same pier, in the same gateway, with the same field gate on the same hinge, and a pillar of the same stone standing in it with nothing cut on it anywhere.

She came off the pier and went back to the weir, and she did not say anything to anybody about a warm stone, and there was not one person on this earth she could have said it to.

## When It Stopped

The ground stopped at about the ninth hour.

Not in one place. She found out about it at the weir from about nine people in about a count of a hundred, all of them saying it, none of them saying it the same way.

A carter said the road had been jumping under him for two days and had stopped jumping.

A woman of about forty said her house had been settling on the north side every night since the second day and had stopped settling.

A boy of about fourteen said the stream had been running fast all week and had slowed down, and had gone back to the ordinary place it runs in, up above where it runs now.

Nine people, nine counties’ worth of nothing happening at once, all of it at the ninth hour, which is the hour that has been hers since she was ten years old and which she has never once had anything arrive on.

She stood at the weir and let about a count of four hundred go past and then said it out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice, before anybody else in four counties had put a single thing about it anywhere.

“It has stopped.”

That was all she said then, and it was about half past true, because she had one thing else and she was not going to say it in the same breath as a thing she had not checked.

## What She Could Derive

She did the rest of it at about the tenth hour, standing on the weir, in the ordinary voice, in front of about thirty people, out loud, in order, the way she does.

She put down the ten places.

She put down the eight that were gone.

She put down the two that were not there when she went to look for them on the ninth day, at the seventh hour and at the eighth, with a boy of about seventeen.

And she put down nine hundred years, which is a figure she has carried since she was a child and has never once had to derive, because it is the only figure anybody in nine counties has ever been able to agree about.

Then she said it, standing, in the ordinary voice, with her hands where people could see them.

“I was going to say this once and I was going to say it plainly and I was not going to say it again today if anybody asked me, because I had not got a way of checking any part of it and I was the only person in the world who would be able to check any part of it and I was not going to.

“There were nine hundred years of a ceiling on this. That was not a figure I had made up. Every person in nine counties was held under it at nine and not one of us had ever been told by anybody what it was made of.

“There were ten places in four counties where that stone stood. I had a list of them and I said it out loud before I started walking, and eight of them came apart over nine days in the order I had said, and I watched every one of them.

“This morning I went back to the other two and there was nothing at either of them. Not fallen. Not broken. Not there.

“It was the ninth day and the last of them had gone and there had not been one since. That was what I had, and it was nine days and ten places, and I had said it out loud standing up before anything else so that it was in front of them first.”

## Nine Hundred And Forty-One

Then she counted, because that was what she did and because it was the only thing she knew how to offer anybody at all.

She counted everybody she could reach, in person, by standing in front of them, and she did it from the fifth hour of the ninth day until the light, and it took nine hours and she did not stop once.

Lowwater, off the terrace and out of it. The bowl. The flat with the quarry on it, where a kiln-man’s wife was rebuilding four hundred pots out of one. The town with the weir. Every road out of the weir town to the length she could get to and back.

She got a figure and she made herself do it twice from both ends, the way she has done about everything since she was nine years old, and she got the same figure both times, and then she sat down on the low weir above the water with her hands on her knees.

Nine hundred and forty-one people.

Nine hundred and forty-one on the ninth day of the month, out of a terrace, a bowl, a flat, a quarry town, a weir town, and the roads between them.

Not one of them had been killed.

## Saying It Twice Slower

Nobody died.

She said it out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice, at about the ninth hour of the ninth day of a month, to about sixty people who had come down off the terrace and out of the bowl and off the roads to stand on a weir because a woman they had known for nine days had been standing in the same place every night and saying a number out loud.

She said it in the voice she uses for figures, at the ninth hour, standing up, with her hands where people could see them, because that is the only way she has ever been able to say a true thing to a person, and she had not found another way in nine days and she was not going to look for one now.

Nine days, and about four hundred people off a terrace and three hundred and nine out of a bowl and nine hundred and forty-one counted on the ninth day, and not one.

The weir was quiet for about a count of a hundred.

Then the man with the yoke, who had walked past her at a gap in a wall and told her once that there were two kinds of sitting, said, “Say the last part again.”

She said the last part again. And she said it slower, because he had asked her to say it slower, and that is what she does.

And a woman near the back of the weir started to cry, which Ning Yan had not braced for and could not afterwards explain, because a woman of about thirty had lost a house in the bowl on the fourth day and had slept on a rim ever since.

## What She Was Left Holding

She had told about sixty people the best news of their year at about the ninth hour and she had said it in the voice she uses for figures and not one face had done what she had expected.

Not one person cheered. Not one person went off to say it. They stood about on a weir in the dark.

A man of about forty came up at about the tenth hour and asked her the question that had been standing in every road in four counties since the fourth day, and she had no answer to it, and for the first time in nineteen years she was not going to have to give him the same one.

“What are we meant to do now.”

She stood on the weir and looked out over the water for about a count of two hundred, and what came out was not *I do not know*, and it was not a victory either, and she knew both of those things about it before she had finished the sentence.

“Go and find out where the low place was,” she said.

He waited for the rest of it.

“That was all of what I had and I wanted them to have it before they went. They were all in the bottom of it, and I did not know why, and I knew for a fact that not one road that came into that bowl had ever gone out of it upward, and everybody in it had been born there and had died there for longer than the stones round it were standing.

“They were the lowest place in four counties and none of them knew. That was not anybody’s doing. It was just where they were, and something was not holding them there any more, and I had not got one word for what that meant and I was not going to invent one at the end of a nine-day count.

“Find out where the low place was. That was the whole of the instruction and I was not able to give them any more than that, and I would be standing there every night at the ninth hour and they could come and say whether it had been any use.”
