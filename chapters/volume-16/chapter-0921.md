# It Came Up Past Her

“Two hundred and eighty-two days since the ninth stage. Seventy-five since it was shut. Seven hundred and thirteen days in that room. The cage out four hundred and eighty. This arm open seven hundred and twenty days. One thousand four hundred and fifty-five gone at this morning.”

She said it standing at the near end of her own table with the lamp on the stone, on the first of the month beginning three thousand and fourteen, at about the fifth hour, and she had it in her mouth before she had finished the last of it.

The first was coming up the stair.

## Where She Was Standing

It came the way traffic comes past a heavy line, the whole of the going getting heavier somewhere far below.

She was four paces inside the door of the room where the first is taken up. She had been in this room since the twenty-second of the month beginning two thousand nine hundred and eighty-four and had not gone up the stair once, and the stair ran nine hundred and four steps to a table with no wear in it, and she was not going up it today either.

On the first of the month beginning two thousand nine hundred and eighty-four she had refused to stand under a bundle in this room while an offer stood open at the top of the tower. She had said that standing, in the ordinary voice, and she had been right about the reason and she had been right about all of it.

The first came up the stair and she was standing in it.

“You are in the room,” he said. “You are in the room you came down into and you have not gone up the stair once since the twenty-second, and the first is four paces inside the door of where you are standing.”

“I know where I am standing.”

“You refused this on the first.”

“I refused it on the first and I have been right about it for thirty days,” she said, “and it is not going to make any difference at all to what happens this morning, and that is the whole of what I have learned about being right.”

“Say it.”

“I said what happens this morning is not going to make any difference to anything, and I did not say it standing, and I am not going to say it again standing either, because I said something else standing on the first and I meant it and it has held.”

He was quiet for a count of forty.

“All right,” he said. “You do not have to be in the room to be in the room.”

“Say it,” she said.

“I am going to say it all the way through,” he said. “You asked me to and I am not going to spare you any of it.”

He gave it to her, and for the first time in nine years she did not need him to, because the thing was about four paces inside the door of the room she was standing in.

## Forty

The hand went along the near end of the boards.

She heard it and then she found it, which was a thing she had never had to do before: the near edge of the table against the worn wood, under her own hand, where the bundle came up to sit.

It came up the way a thing comes up when it has come up six times without her, which is to say without any ceremony in it at all. Forty boards under a cloth, laid flat, and the cloth going over the edges and being put back the way it had been folded, and the cord already round them twice before she understood that anybody had been in the room.

Forty of them. She counted them the way she counted everything, going along the near end one at a time with the pad of her thumb, and it was forty and not thirty-nine, and the cloth was over them, and the cord was taken twice round and pulled and not finished into a knot because it is never finished into a knot.

Then the going up. Nine hundred and four steps of a stair taking weight that goes up it four hundred times a year.

She stood in her own room with her thumb on the edge of the table and her hand off the bundle, because she had not put it on the bundle.

It sat on her table for about a count of four hundred after that. She stood four paces off and looked at it. Forty boards and a cloth and a cord, and nothing written on any of it, and nothing on the cloth, and nothing about her on any part of it.

She had walked down nine hundred and four steps on the twenty-second of the month beginning two thousand nine hundred and eighty-four with a lamp in her hand and put it down and walked up again, and she had looked at it twice in nineteen years and both times she had been looking for her own name.

There was not one on it. There was not one mark of any kind on any of it, and it was the ninth time in nineteen years she had looked at something made by a hand that never wore anything.

Then the hand went out, and the going went up, and the room was a room again.

She went and stood at the boards and put her hand flat on the near one, face up a foot in from the near end, and did not lift it.

“I am not going to write it down.”

“I was going to ask you to.”

“Do not ask me to.”

“I am not going to ask you to,” he said. “I have watched you not write down a first of a month for eight days and I have not said one word about it and I am not going to start. There is no board up here and you have not wanted one since the third of the month beginning two thousand nine hundred and fifty-four, and I have never once asked you why, and if you told me today I would rather you did not.”

She stood at the boards with her hand on the near one.

“Why would you rather I did not.”

“Because I do not want anything out of this morning,” he said. “That is the whole of it, and it is the first morning in this building that I have wanted nothing out of, and I have noticed it happening and I have left it alone.”

## A Witness Asked For

“You counted them,” he said.

“Yes.”

“You have never counted them. I have listened to six firsts go up this stair and you have not been in this room for any of them and you were not in this building for five of the six.”

“I counted them because they were there and I count things.”

She put her hand flat on the table where the bundle had been and found it cold, which was the one thing about the morning that had a temperature to it.

“You counted them because you wanted to be able to say you were in it,” he said. “And I want you to have that. I have wanted you to have it since the eighth of the month before last, and I did not ask you for it on that day, and I am asking you for it now in the only way I can, which is by telling you that I want it.”

“Why.”

He was quiet for a count of forty.

“Because I promised you the thirtieth and I did not do it, and I am not going to do it this morning either,” he said, “and when I do do it, it is going to be a thing I have never wanted you to hear said, and I am going to be standing in this room when you hear it, and I am not going to be able to argue with anything you say afterwards.

“And I want there to be one thing you have seen with your own two hands behind your back, in a room, on a morning, that no one argued with. Forty boards. A hand that came and went. A thing kept.”

She stood at the near end of her own table with her thumb on the wood.

“You are asking me to be a witness for you.”

“Yes,” he said. “I am asking you to be a witness for me and I have never once done that and I am not ashamed of it and I am telling you what it is first.”

## The Kind Thing

She did not touch the bundle and the bundle was gone up the stair and the room was the room.

At the ninth hour she stood with the lamp on the stone and said the day’s figures out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice, into the dark past the last landing.

One thousand four hundred and fifty-five days gone at this morning, taken out of her at a rate she derived out of her own life, and not one of them coming back.

Then she stood there with the lamp burning at her feet and the worn wood under her hand and the second place at the far side kept four hundred years and empty, and she worked out what she had done this morning the way she worked out everything, standing, and it took a count of a hundred.

She had stood in the room where the first is taken up, on a first of a month, in a building that had been keeping the walls for nine hundred years, and not one thing had touched her. The bundle had not wanted her. The hand had not wanted her. The room had gone on doing the thing it had done four hundred times a year since before she was born without knowing that she was in it.

And then she said it out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice, in the channel where the thing that made the sentences reads, first in her own voice before it was a line anywhere.

“It was kind.”

He did not answer.

She stood there with her hand flat on the table where her own arms had worn the wood down over nineteen years, and the sentence had gone out one piece at a time, plain, the way everything she had ever said in this building had gone out, and it could not be gotten back, and it was the first thing she had put into the keeping since the twenty-fifth that had not been read straight off a stone.
