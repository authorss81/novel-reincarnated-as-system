# What A Number Is Made Of

Three hundred and eighty-seven days since the ninth stage. One hundred and eighty since it was shut. Eight hundred and eighteen days in that room. Five hundred and eighty-five days since a cage lamp went out on a shelf. Eight hundred and twenty-five days with this arm open at the elbow. One thousand five hundred and sixty days gone, taken out of her at a rate she derived out of her own life, not one of them coming back.

She said all of it standing at the ninth hour on the sixteenth of the month beginning three thousand and one hundred and four, in the ordinary voice, at the gap in the low wall, first in her own voice before it was a line anywhere.

And then she said the other one, which was the number of people in the arithmetic of a woman standing at a gap in a wall.

Three hundred and ninety-nine.

## What She Did With The Number

She did not go and find the next one for about a count of two hundred, which was not like her.

She sat down on the wall with her back to the stone and put her hands flat on her knees and worked the count over the way she worked every figure she had ever had, which was from the top, down, taking each part and putting it against another part.

The count was one hundred and forty-nine on the fourth at the fifth hour. It was two hundred and twenty-one on the seventh at the ninth hour. It was two hundred and eighty-eight on the tenth at the ninth hour, which was the one that had come out of her mouth with nothing behind it. It was three hundred and forty-four on the thirteenth at the ninth hour.

And it was three hundred and ninety-nine this morning and she had derived all of it, every night, standing, out loud, out of a count she started with on the twentieth of the month beginning three thousand and seventy-four at the bottom of a hill with two in it.

She had never in nineteen years derived anything out of anything.

## The Part That Is Not In It

Then she went through them.

That was the part of the morning she had not planned. She sat on a wall at a gap in a road and went through three hundred and ninety-nine people, one at a time, and looked for them in herself the way she looked for a figure in her head, and found out where the number stopped being made of people.

She could put a face to Kan Shou, and to Dai Yu, and to Bao Ren and his broken cart shaft, and to Fen and his thumb, and to the woman of fifty who had asked her what happened when she died, and to about thirty others.

She could put names to maybe fifty.

The rest of it was a number.

That was the finding and she sat with it for about a count of a hundred and twenty without moving.

A count is not a list. She had known that for nineteen years the way a person knows that a year is not made of days, and she had never once had it be true of anything that had happened to her. Every figure she had ever carried had had a thing behind it. The arm had a day. The place behind her fourth rib had a date. The days gone out of her had her own life in them.

Three hundred and ninety-nine had nothing behind it but the fact that she had said them out loud in the ordinary voice and the keeping had taken each one after her.

She said that out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice, at a gap in a wall, and then she said the other half of it.

“I put three hundred and ninety-nine of them in a keeping nine hundred years old and I cannot put forty faces to them and I cannot find one road between here and where most of them live. That is what a number is. A number is a thing that does not remember what went into it.”

## What The Road Had Left

The road was running out and she could see it from the gap.

There had been about sixty people on that road on the first of the month and a hundred by the fourth and a great many on the last two days of the third. There were nine on the sixteenth, and Guo Sheng had been down to the foot of the hill twice that week and had reported back that the word had gone as far as the next valley and no further.

“A queue runs out at the edge of the thing that made it,” he said. “That is all a queue is. It is where people have heard.”

She had got a thing she had not gone after, on the fourteenth, and it came back to her at the gap while he was talking.

A woman of about thirty had brought her daughter to the queue on the fourteenth and had asked Ning Yan to say the three sentences out loud again with the child standing next to her, because the child was fourteen and had not been told anything and was about to say yes in the afternoon.

She said them again, standing, in the ordinary voice, twice over, with a fourteen-year-old girl standing four feet off holding her mother’s sleeve.

And then the girl asked her, without any difficulty at all, whether the third sentence was true, about the sending, and Ning Yan stood on a road and realised that the thing had left her hands.

That was not a defeat. It was what she had come down the mountain for. A sentence said out loud standing in the ordinary voice before it is a line anywhere is a thing that carries in a county once it has been said twice with a child standing next to it.

She had no way of controlling that and she did not want any.

She needed one more and there was one more out there somewhere in nine counties and she had no way at all of finding a single person in nine counties, because she could not be given anything, and she was not sent, and not one person knew she was here except the people who had been at the queue.

There was no office. There was no one to ask. There was a road and a gap in a wall and a queue that went down it, and it was the only instrument in the world she had ever had that worked on anybody else.

## The Twelfth Day Of Standing

Her body had started making the decisions for her, which was a new thing and she did not like it.

She had been on her feet from about the sixth hour to the ninth for eighteen days.

The left leg had gone at the knee by the eighth day and she had stopped noticing it. Her left hand, the one with three fingers and a thumb on it, had a shake in it in the evenings that had not been there on the eleventh of a month at about the sixth hour, and she had worked out that it went worse in the cold and better when she got hold of something.

## The One She Could Not Find

She went looking for the four hundredth on the fifteenth.

That was the last day of a queue and she understood on the fourteenth that it was the last day, and she spent the fifteenth going down the road on her own two feet for about as long as the light lasted, because a queue only exists where people have heard and there were eleven people in nine counties who had heard and she had already got them all except one.

She went down the road past the flat and up onto the first rise and along the side of the hill and back again, and she asked about eleven people on the way, and ten of them had heard of it and had been and had said no and had reasons.

The eleventh was a man who said he had four of his own and could not put a fifth under a thing he could not see, and he said it kindly, and he said, “You will find one. There is always one. They come in at the end because they have been standing at the side of it for two days deciding whether they are the sort of person who does this.”

That was not a fact about a woman of about fifty. It was a fact about the whole of what she had spent eighteen days doing.

She stood on the side of a hill at about the seventh hour on the fifteenth and said out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice, what she had just worked out.

“A queue is not a hunt and I have been treating it like one, and a hunt is something you do to somebody and this is something people do to themselves, and I have no instrument for that at all and I never had.”

And then she walked back up towards the road, because there was one person still standing at the far end of it in the rain and it was the fifteenth and she had not asked her yet.

The open arm had not changed. That was the part that frightened her most. Nineteen years of a keeping had taken an arm, three fingers and a thumb, seven holds out of eleven, a place behind her fourth rib the size of her thumbnail, and one thousand five hundred and sixty days out of her own life, and in eighteen days of standing in a cold queue on a road it had not taken one single thing out of her that she could find with an arithmetic.

She had told two hundred people on that road that the price was on the record and that she did not know what it cost her, and she had not been able to find out since the seventh, and she had not been harmed, and she had no idea which of those two things was the real answer.

## At The End Of The Day

She said the count out loud at the ninth hour on the sixteenth, standing, in the ordinary voice, and it was three hundred and ninety-nine and she had derived every one of them since the twentieth of a month on a hillside, and there was no part of the arithmetic she could not do again with her hands behind her back.

Then she put the lamp down on the wall, which was an old habit, and the lamp had not carried a flame since the fourteenth, and she turned round to go and see whether the road had emptied out.

It had not.

There was one person standing at the far end of it, about fifty yards off, in the rain, on the edge of where the queue had been, and she had been standing there since about the fifth hour, and Guo Sheng had told her at about the seventh hour that the woman had asked him three times what she had to bring and he had not been able to tell her anything, because there was nothing to bring.

She had not come forward. She had not gone away.

Ning Yan picked the lamp up off the wall and went the length of that road to the woman at the end of it, and she did not know what she was going to say when she got there and she had not got a sentence, and the woman had been standing in the rain for two days waiting for somebody to come and ask her.
