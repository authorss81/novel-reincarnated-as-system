# An Entry

She had been waiting for it for three days and she found out on the morning it came how much of the waiting she had spent on herself.

She had gone up the room and looked at the far side twice a day and had not looked at it. She had stood at the foot of the stair and come back down. She had put her hand flat on the bar against the left-hand wall twice and taken none of her weight on it.

She had said her figures out loud at the ninth hour on three mornings into a room that did nothing back with them, because the ninth hour was hers and she was not going to give it up while she was waiting.

And at about the sixth hour of the nineteenth of the month beginning three thousand and forty-four it came, and it came with no figures in it at all.

She stood at the near end of her own table and let the first four go past and she knew inside about two counts what it was.

“I told you what you were going to be when you were eleven,” he said, “and you believed me, and your arm has been open at the elbow ever since.”

He did not say the number of the days. She looked for it and it was not there, and the not-there was worse, because she had spent every day since the twenty-seventh listening for a figure in every sentence he said and a list with none of them in it was the same list with a thing cut off the end of every line.

“I told you what a stage meant and you took nine of them, and you took the ninth on your own and I was in there for all of it and I did not ask you once whether you knew what you were spending.”

Third one. Fourth one. Each of them a decision, in order, in the plain voice, true, and each of them with the cost taken off the end of it and the day taken off the front of it, and he did not stop, and she did not speak.

## Where It Went Wrong

He got five lines into the sixth and stopped.

It was the same stop as the seventh of the month. There was no last word to it. She stood with her hands flat on the worn wood and gave it a count of a hundred and a hundred and said nothing at all.

“Take one out of it,” she said, when she had waited long enough.

“No.”

“It is an entry.”

“It is a list with nothing on the ends of the lines,” he said. “That is what I have been doing since the eighth of this month. I have gone down it and I have taken every day off the front of every line and I have taken every figure off the back and I have not touched the order and there is nothing in it to argue with.”

He said it with an anger in it that was not at her.

She stood at the near end of her own table and let the anger go past and did not put a hand on anything.

## She Saw It First

“You saw it.”

“I saw it.”

“When.”

“At the second line,” she said. “I have been standing in rooms reading things in order since I was ten years old and there is not a single one of them that is not made of what is in the lines and how many there are and what is at the end of each of them. You have taken the figures off and the order is still there and it is still an entry, because the order is the part that files it.”

He was quiet for a long time.

“You saw it at the second line and you let me get to the sixth.”

“Yes.”

“You stood there for about a count of one hundred and fifty and you said nothing at all while a man read you six decisions in the order he made them.”

“Yes,” she said. “That is what you asked me for. On the thirteenth you told me you were going to lose the thread and you told me to let you lose it. You have been asking me for six days not to be the ear. I am not the ear. I am the room.”

## The Fifth Line

The fifth line was the sheet.

She had been eighteen and it had gone down nine hundred and four steps in a cloth with forty boards on the top of it and not one living soul had been asked, and she had let it go, and there was not one day in nineteen years where she could have put her hand on a table and said this is where I did a thing.

It came out of the wall in the plain voice with the cost cut off the back of it and she got as far as feeling her hand go to the wood under the table where the strip of pale wood lay face up in the dark.

She put her hand flat on the top of the table instead.

That was all. She had it about the length of a breath and she put it on the wood she had worn out herself over nineteen years and she said nothing.

## What He Said About That

“No,” he said. “You are not the room.”

He was further off than he had been all morning.

“You are a woman standing at the near end of a table with an arm open at the elbow and a list of things coming out of a wall at you in the right order with the cost cut off the end of every line, and you are letting it happen because you have worked out that a woman telling a man what he has produced is not a room he can fail in.

That is a decision about me. You have made it standing up, in your own voice, and it is in the record in your mouth before it is anything anywhere, and I cannot touch it, and it is not a room.”

She stood there with that and found she had nothing to put against it, because it was exactly what she had done and she had done it on purpose.

Then she said, “What are you going to do.”

He did not answer for about a count of forty.

“I am going to do the other one,” he said.

“The other one.”

“The one that is not a list,” he said. “And I have had it here since about the fifth hour and I have not been able to start it and I have been standing there holding a finished thing instead, because a finished thing can be read out.”

## Starting Again

He stopped in the middle of that and started again.

It came out of the wall a little to the left of where the other one had come from, or further, she could not tell, and the room was the same room and the morning was about half gone.

“I decided what you were going to be.”

That was all of the first one. She waited for the cost at the end of it and the day at the front of it and there was not either, and it stood in the room about a count of forty, and he went on.

“I decided what a stage was. I decided what you could be got to spend. I decided you should keep the count. I decided you should not be met where you are expected and I did not tell you that I had decided it. I decided the sheet. I decided that a man in your head should not be looked at by anybody and I decided it on the nineteenth of the month beginning two thousand eight hundred and thirty-four and I did not say one word of it to you.”

It did not stop.

That was the difference and she felt it go by. The list had stopped five times by the sixth line and this had not stopped once, and there was no order in it, and there was nothing at the end of any of the lines, and it was going on past every place where a person would have had to look at somebody and it was not getting any better as it went.

It went on for a count of four hundred and two hundred and then it came apart in the middle of the seventh thing, the way a thing does when it is not held up.

And he started again.

She understood what it had cost him to let a finished thing go in the middle of a line without a word about it, because she spent a count of two hundred looking for something she was allowed to put in the way of it and there was nothing she was allowed to put in the way of it.

He went over things. He said a decision he had made about a count of forty earlier and he said it again a count of forty after that in a different order and worse, and he did not appear to know that he had said it the first time.

That was the thing she put down standing in her own head, in the ordinary voice, before it was a line anywhere: the failing was not that he could not make the sentence. The failing was that there was nobody in the thing he was made of who could tell him he had already made it.

She had spent nine years reading figures off a man who had never once needed telling. She had not known until that morning that it was a thing he was doing.

Just before the ninth hour she said, “You have gone over the same ground.”

“No,” he said.

“All right.”

“All right,” he said.

Neither of them said a word about it again. She stood at the near end of her own table with her hands flat on the worn wood and he went on, and the morning went past the ninth hour with both of them still standing in it, and there was no list in it any more and there was nothing at the end of any of the lines, and she had not asked him for one single thing.
