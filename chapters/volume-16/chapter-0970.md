# Nothing Came

The light came up clear over the flat on the sixteenth of the month beginning three thousand and one hundred and thirty-four for the first time in a week, and the whole of that day went off in front of her like water going down a drain.

## The Morning

A carter came through at about the sixth hour with a load of salt. Two women went up the road to the pass with a bundle between them and were still going at the fourth hour. A boy of about eleven put his hand into a hive and got stung and howled about it for a count of a hundred and forty and was given a wet cloth by a woman he had never spoken to.

Ning Yan sat on the wall at the gap and watched all of it and had nothing to do in any of it.

That was the whole trouble with the day and it had arrived at about the sixth hour and stayed until dark.

Nineteen years. Every morning at the ninth hour she had stood up and put six figures out loud in order into the open air, and the figures had belonged to her and had come out of her own life and had not changed and had not needed her, and about a count of six hundred was all the whole of it had ever taken.

And there was not one of them to do.

## The Keep On The Question

She did the count at the ninth hour, because the rule does not have an exception for a day.

Four hundred.

She said it out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice, and she derived all of it: one on the twentieth of a month on a hillside, two at a stall, three in a road, fourteen on the last day of that month, sixty-eight on the first, a hundred and forty-nine by the fifth hour of the fourth, two hundred and twenty-one on the seventh, two hundred and eighty-eight on the tenth, three hundred and forty-four on the thirteenth, three hundred and ninety-nine on the sixteenth, and four hundred on the nineteenth.

Every figure in it was where she had left it and not one of them was out by anything.

It was the first time in nineteen years that she had put a number out loud and learned nothing from it.

## The Checking

Then she checked, all day, everything she had.

The lamp, which had not carried a flame since the fourteenth of the month beginning three thousand and seventy-four.

The right arm, which was open at the elbow and had been open for eight hundred and fifty-five days and was going to be open tomorrow.

The three fingers and a thumb. The seven holds out of eleven.

The place behind her fourth rib, which she put two fingers on at the sixth hour, at the eighth hour and at the second hour of the afternoon, and which was the size of her thumbnail on all three occasions, and had been since she was a child.

She checked the flat and the road and the crossing and the stall and the wall.

She walked out into the fog to the place where she had said it and found it by counting her own paces back from the crossing, because she had no marker and had thought about putting one in and had not, and she stood on it for about a count of four hundred, and there was nothing there of any kind whatsoever.

## What She Asked People

At about the seventh hour she asked two people the same question standing, in the ordinary voice, at the gap in the wall.

“Has anything happened.”

The first was a man with a yoke who had been walking that road for thirty years and who said no, and who said it kindly, and who added that nothing had happened for about a week and that in thirty years on that road nothing happened more than twice a year and that one of those was a roof.

The second was He Yu, who had come up the flat with a basket and had stopped, and who said, “No. Has it?”

“No.”

“Then that is two of us who have noticed nothing,” He Yu said, and went on up to the stall, and she had not asked where Ning Yan had been all day and she was the only person in nine counties who had not asked her where she had been all day.

## What She Was Actually Afraid Of

She was not afraid that it had not gone in.

That was the thing she got wrong about herself twice that day and she worked it out both times standing up.

She was afraid that it had gone in.

A keeping of nine hundred years that has never been refused by anybody it recognised, that has kept the walls correctly for nine centuries and put down four hundred persons and the price of each without being asked and gone on keeping the walls, and that a woman of nineteen has just put nine words into out of her own mouth in a fog.

If it went in, then whatever it does about a sentence that cannot survive being told is going to be done to a world that does not know it is going to happen. Not on the walls. On everything the walls have been holding up.

There are four hundred people in her arithmetic and about nine of them within calling of a gap in a wall, and fourteen days, and a road, and a county town with a man in it who has a wet thumb.

She said that out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice, at about the sixth hour, to a road where a boy was walking a pig, because she had told four hundred people that she does not say a thing she cannot derive and that was what came out when she derived the fear instead of the hope.

And then she worked out the rest of it, standing, because she had been avoiding it and it was going to come out on its own.

A keeping nine hundred years old has kept four hundred people in one record and a woman of nineteen in one room for eight hundred and forty-eight days, and it has done both correctly, and it has never once written down a line saying that anybody chose anything. She had found that out on the tenth of a month standing in bad weather and she had worked out that it was the only thing she could say that the layer could not survive being told.

If she had said it and it had gone in, then the walls would come down, and she has said out loud on a road that the walls are what nine hundred years of a ceiling is made of, and she said it at about the seventh hour on the last day of the month beginning three thousand and one hundred and four, standing, in the ordinary voice, to a wall.

Three hundred and ninety-one people in nine counties are asleep.

## The Long Afternoon

The afternoon was the worst part.

She had expected something at the ninth hour because the ninth hour had been hers every morning since she was ten years old, and it went past like any other hour, and then she expected it in the afternoon for about a count of a hundred, and it did not go past like any other hour, it went slowly.

She got up and walked to the far end of the road and back, and up the track about two hundred paces and back, which is the distance she can manage on that arm, and along the wall to where it turns.

She worked out, standing, at the seventh hour, that she had been treating a silence as a thing that was happening to her for nineteen years, and that in nineteen years it had never once been anything of the sort, and that she had never tested it because she had never needed a thing out of it.

She had never in her life needed a thing out of it. She had put four hundred people into a record on the twentieth of a month and she had got a number back four days later without asking, and she had worked that out and been proud of it, and she had not once said out loud that a keeping that never answers a question is also a keeping she cannot put a question to.

## The Dusk

Kan Shou came up off the flat at about the seventh hour with her shutter half across and said, without any preamble at all, “What are you going to tell the four hundred.”

“I do not know.”

“They have been told the price is on the record.”

“Yes.”

“Kan Shou, that is not an answer to a question.”

“It is the one I have got,” Ning Yan said, “and I am going to say it out loud where these people can hear it. The price of it is on the record. I do not know what it costs me. Not one person has sent you, and nothing is done to you, and I am the only one who binds you. That is all of it and it is the whole of what I have ever offered anybody and I cannot do better than it this evening.”

Kan Shou stood there with the shutter on her shoulder.

“You asked me what it was for on the seventh,” she said, “and I have been carrying that for nine days, and not one of them has been told it, because there was no road to them. That is the thing you are going to have to do and you have not done it.”

“No.”

## Sitting Still

So she sat down at the gap in the low wall with her back against the stone, in the last of the light, with the lamp beside her that had not carried a flame since the fourteenth of the month beginning three thousand and seventy-four.

And she waited.

Not for an answer. She had given that up somewhere in the afternoon, standing, on a flat, with her own arithmetic, and it had taken her about a count of three hundred to admit that what she had been waiting for was never coming and had never come in nineteen years.

She waited to see whether the walls were still there.

And they were, and the road was, and the queue was gone and the four hundred were out in nine counties and the ninth hour had gone by, and she sat at a gap in a wall in the dark and worked out that there was nothing left in the world she could do about anything at all except one thing, which was to find out whether a keeping nine hundred years old was still keeping.
