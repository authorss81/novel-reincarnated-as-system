# The End She Had Never Read

Three hundred and ninety-six days since the ninth stage. One hundred and eighty-nine since it was shut. Eight hundred and twenty-seven days in that room, and there had been no room for forty-four days now. Eight hundred and thirty-four days with this arm open at the elbow.

One thousand five hundred and sixty-nine days gone, taken out of her at a rate she derived out of her own life, not one of them coming back.

She said all of it standing at the ninth hour on the twenty-fifth of the month beginning three thousand and one hundred and four, in the ordinary voice, at the gap in the low wall, first in her own voice before it was a line anywhere, with about four people standing at the far end of the road who had come off a hill and had nothing to be doing there.

## What Comes In

Then she stood still and read the other thing, which is the thing she can do.

She had been reading the incoming end since she was seven years old. It came up through the ground under her feet in the open the way it had come up through the dark past the last landing in a building, one piece at a time, plain, in the grammar the keeping uses for everything it says.

It does not come to you because you asked. It comes the way a day comes, and it is about what has already happened, and it is put down in the order it happened in, and you read it or you do not and nothing is lost either way.

She stood at the gap in the wall and read what had come in that day in nine counties, all of it, the way a clerk reads a day.

A carter had broken a wheel and had taken four days over it and had not told the man he worked for.

A woman had buried a mother on the eleventh of the month and had paid for the ground in a coin worn so smooth that the face on it could not be seen.

A boy of about fourteen had gone into the water up to his knees in front of two people and had come out, and the two people had not been his family.

And the four hundred were in it, still, at the number she had last said out loud, and the price of each was in it, and nothing had been done to any of them.

She read all of it standing up. That was the whole of her morning and it took her about a count of six hundred, and she had been doing it since she was seven, and she had got faster at it every year, and it had never once cost her anything to do.

## The Thing She Had Never Asked

At about the eleventh hour she asked herself the question and had to sit down on the wall to ask it properly.

She had been reading one end of it since she was seven years old and she had never asked whether there was another.

She had nineteen years of a grammar in her head. She knew every shape the keeping puts a thing in. She knew that it reports and does not ask, that it counts and does not judge, that a thing goes into it and the going in is silent, and that nothing has ever come back out to whoever sent it.

She had never in her life once seen the other end.

## Until The Light Went

She waited from the eleventh hour until the light went off the flat.

She stayed where she was through the long grey middle of the day and the whole of the afternoon and into the night. She sat on the wall with her feet in the mud and the lamp set down beside her and her mouth shut, which is the one thing she has been able to do without stopping for nineteen years.

Nothing came.

The road went on being a road.

Kan Shou shut her stall at about the seventh hour and put a board across the front of it, and the road went quiet, and a dog went along the flat for about a count of four hundred and came back the way it had gone.

About the fourth hour she tried the thing she had done for a fortnight on that road, which was to put a question out loud into the open air and wait to see whether the world changed its shape.

She said, standing, in the ordinary voice, at a gap in a wall, in a village where about nine people lived within calling: what does it sound like when you say a thing out loud.

She said it three times. She gave it until the light went. The road stayed the road and the mud stayed mud and the four people who had come off the hill went back down it and did not look at her on the way.

She had known that was going to be the answer. She had done it a fortnight already on the same road for exactly this and had got four plain sentences up under her feet at the fifth hour on the fourth and not one word of them had been the answer to the question she had been asking.

So she stopped, and sat down, and waited out the rest of it in silence, and the waiting was not the hard part.

## What Came Down The Track

He Yu came up from the flat about a third of the way through the afternoon with a bundle on her arm and the two days’ work done in a house with a sound roof.

She put the bundle down on the wall on the other side of the gap from Ning Yan and stood there in the rain, and she waited to be asked about something, and Ning Yan could tell that she had come to ask.

“You came up here to ask me a thing,” Ning Yan said.

“Yes.”

“Then ask it.”

He Yu looked at the wall.

“Is it soon,” she said. “Whatever it is you have not got.”

Ning Yan sat there with a dead lamp in her left hand and a woman of about fifty standing on the other side of a gap in a wall and understood that she had been asked for a date by the four hundredth person she had ever put into a keeping, and that she had no date and no answer and that saying so out loud was the only thing she had.

“I do not know when it is,” she said, “and I do not know what it is yet, and that is the true answer and you have heard it.”

“All right,” He Yu said, and stood there a while longer, and then said, “My daughter-in-law asked me last night why I had gone down a hill to say yes to a woman with a lamp that is not lit.”

“What did you tell her.”

“I told her the truth,” He Yu said, “and she has not asked me anything since, so that is one of the two of us who is managing it.”

She shouldered the bundle and went back down the track, and Ning Yan sat on the wall and found that the question she had asked at the eleventh hour had come back to her with a person attached to it, which it had not had when she asked it.

## What She Worked Out

At about the seventh hour of the night she got the whole of it out in one piece, standing, on the wall, in the dark.

The keeping reads out.

She had been inside the room it reads into since she was seven years old. She had the output and had never once had the input, and she had been so certain for so long that a machine and the things a machine says are the same object that she had never once noticed the difference.

A person who has read a thing faithfully for nineteen years does not usually think to ask what happens at the other end of it.

She said it out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice, to a road with a pig track on it and not one person standing on it.

“I have read that keeping every morning since I was seven years old and I have never once seen it do the thing it was built for.”

## The Lamp On The Shelf

And then she put nineteen years up beside that and made it say something, which is what she does.

Five hundred and ninety-four days ago a cage lamp had gone out on a shelf in a building in a mountain, and the lamp had not been reported to her, and nothing had gone dark in the keeping’s grammar because of it.

The four hundred had gone into the same keeping at the fifth hour of the fourth of the month, and it had put four plain sentences up under her feet in the middle of a queue, and it had said the price of each and that it was not asked for them.

A lamp going out on a shelf had gone in on the same day and it had said nothing.

It was not a thing it kept. That was the finding. It was not keeping lamps and it was not keeping her, and it had been reading its own account out to one child in a room for two years and then for nineteen, and it had not told her once what it was for, because what it was for was the other end.

## The Cost Of Knowing The Grammar

She got down off the wall and stood in the road in the dark with her left hand shaking, and she went through it one more time because she wanted to be certain, and she was.

She knew the grammar better than any person in the world.

That was the whole of what she had, and it was the wrong half of it, and she had built nineteen years of arithmetic on the assumption that a machine and its output were one object, and the assumption had held on every single day of her life because it had never been tested by anything that needed the other end.

She said the last of it out loud to the pig track.

“The only half of that thing that could ever have told me what one of its sentences sounds like is the half I have never seen, and it has been going out past me every day for nineteen years.”
