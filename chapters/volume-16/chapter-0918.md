# The Second of the Four

She got up off the stone and put the lamp on the edge of the table, because a reading is done standing, and it does not matter that there is nothing on the table to read.

It was the twenty-fifth of the month beginning two thousand nine hundred and eighty-four at about the eighth hour, and the register was on a table at the top of a tower four hundred steps and eight flights above her with nothing worn in it anywhere, and she was going to say all of it out loud in her own room.

“I am starting,” she said. “You asked for this and I am going to do it the way you asked for it, and that is standing, and that is the ordinary voice, and I am not going to stop at anything.”

He said nothing.

That was the first thing. He had nine days of reading on the page in front of him and he had told her on the fourth that he wanted it and now she was giving it to him and he had not said one word in four hours.

## The Five Mercies

She stood at the near end of her own table with the lamp at the edge of it and the light going two paces along the worn wood, and she read.

The first, that she is carried up and is given a place of her own to keep, and the carrying is done whole and is not a taking.

Not this time. She had not come to the first one to find anything. She read it the way she read everything now, standing, out loud, one line at a time, and the open place behind her fourth rib pulled when she got to her own and she let it pull.

The second. The third. The fourth. The fifth, about the walls and the layer and the mercy of a bounded world, no unchecked ascension, no harvested souls, no immortal war over the inside of a small place.

Not one of them had a condition in it. She had read them twice by her fingers in the dark at the top of that tower on the fourteenth of the month beginning two thousand nine hundred and fifty-four and found none, and she found none again now, standing in her own room at the eighth hour with a lamp on the edge of a table, and the not finding was the fourth time she had done it.

Her right arm went about the fourth mercy.

She did not stop. She had told him at the eighth hour that she was not going to stop at anything, and she meant it the way she meant every thing she said standing, which was completely and without deciding in advance. She put her weight off the arm and onto her left leg and kept the reading going and the lamp went on standing at the edge of the table where she had put it, throwing two paces of light along worn wood.

She had read the whole thing four times. Every time she had gone at it looking for a thing somebody else had left in it, a condition, a price, a small piece of grammar with teeth in it. There was nothing. She was not finding a thing for the fifth time. She was finding out, for the fifth time, that there was nothing, and that a table with five mercies cut into it can sit in a room for four hundred years and mean exactly what it says.

Then the sixth cut, which she did not need to look at and had not needed to since the fourteenth.

“A thing is said in the voice of the person it is about, standing, before it is a line anywhere.”

She said it standing, in the ordinary voice, in her own room, about herself, and the lamp shook a little on the edge of the table and steadied.

## His Part Of It

Then she got to the part that was his and she read it out loud in her own room with him in it, standing, in the ordinary voice, the four lines she had stood at a table in the dark and read to his face on the twenty-third of the month beginning two thousand nine hundred and fifty-four.

He is archived where he is, and addressed wherever he is, and remembered in the keeping of what he did and not of what it cost. He is given a name that is not the one he died under, and he is given work that matters, and neither of the two is a rank.

He did not make a sound for the whole of it.

She got to the last of the four lines and said the name line, the one that carries no name, and then she was standing in her own room with the whole of what he said yes to in her mouth and off it again.

“You did not say a word,” she said.

“You told me at the eighth hour that you were not going to stop at anything.”

“That is not why.”

“No,” he said. “That is not why. That is a woman reading the whole of what she is owed out loud in front of the only person in the world it is owed to, for the second time in nineteen years, and it is the second time and I am not able to do a single thing with it.”

She went on.

## The Four Lines

The first: it stands open until it is answered or withdrawn.

The second: it is answered in the channel, out loud, standing.

The third: a yes given there cannot be un-said.

The fourth: one line of it carries no name.

She had those four the way she had her own hands and she said them out loud one after another in about a count of sixty, and then she went back to the second one, and she stood there.

“The second,” she said.

“I heard you say it.”

“You said it about the channel,” she said. “I remember you saying it. You said the answer has to go out in the ordinary voice, in the channel, in front of the thing that made you, and there is no writing and no sign.”

“Yes.”

“Then read it back to me as it is on the stone.”

He was quiet for a count of forty.

“It is answered in the channel, out loud, standing.”

“It does not say where the channel is.”

“No.”

“It does not say a room or a floor or a building or a place a person could be found in.”

“No,” he said. “It does not, and I have read it four times as well, and I did not see it either until you stopped on it just now.”

## Where It Was Answered

She stood at the near end of the table with her thumb on the wood in the hollow her own forearms had worn there.

“The channel,” she said. “Where is the channel.”

“It is the way everything in this building speaks,” he said. “It is not a place. It is the going, and it goes out of you and down and through, and everything said anywhere in this tower goes down it, and a voice in a head goes down it too, and that is how a thing in there can be heard by the whole of the bound at once.”

“So it is a place.”

“No.”

“It is the only place the offer names, and you said yes in it, and it is in me.”

He did not answer for a long time.

“It is in you,” he said. “You are the location. There is no other place in the world that the second line of that register names, and I have been standing in it since the seventh of this month without knowing I was standing in a location, and you have just worked out what it is by asking me where it is.”

She stood there with her hands flat on the wood.

“You said yes in a place that cannot be met,” she said.

“Yes,” he said. “And the whole of the arrangement is in the same place, and the five mercies and the four lines and the offer are all standing in a woman in a room in a tower at the eighth hour, and if you go to sleep tonight they will still be standing there in the morning, and there is not one person alive who can come and put a hand on them.

“Including me. I have been in there for six hundred years and I have never been able to put a hand on anything.”

## What The Keeping Wrote Down

She said the day’s figures at the ninth hour, standing, in the ordinary voice, into the dark past the last landing, and the lamp was on the edge of the table and the room was her own and there was no one else in it.

One thousand four hundred and forty-nine days gone at this morning, taken out of her at a rate she derived out of her own life, and not one of them coming back.

The line came past her one at a time, plain, in the grammar the keeping used for everything it said, and it was not about her and it did not explain anything.

[The answer of the seventh is held and has not begun. One thousand four hundred and forty-nine days stand taken.]

She stood at the near end of the table and let it go past.

Then she said, “It has my figure in it.”

“It has your figure in it,” he said. “Not yours this morning. Yours on the day before. You said it standing in this room with no one in it and it is the first time that keeping has had that figure in that form, and it has written it down, and it has not said anything about the fact that it did.”

She stood at the near end of the table and worked on that standing, the way she worked on everything, and it took a count of a hundred.

“The keeping does not ask for a figure,” she said. “It has never once asked me for a figure. It has taken every one I have given it and used it for whatever it was already using.”

“Yes,” he said. “That is what a keeping is. It is not interested in your day. It is interested in the days, all of them, in a row, going the way a stair goes up. You have been the one bringing it something every morning for nineteen years and this morning it went on without you for about the length of one sentence.”

She put her hand flat on the table where the wear was, and the hollow under it took her palm the way it had taken it every morning since she was ten years old, and she found that she had been afraid of something small all afternoon without knowing what it was.

It was not the offer. She had read the offer four times.

It was that she had spent nineteen years being the reason that keeping knew what day it was, and she had built her whole arithmetic on the assumption that somebody wanted the numbers, and there had never been anybody on the other end of them at all.

The offer stood open on a table at the top of a tower she was not going back to, and the answer was held, and the whole of it was standing in the only room in the world that no one could come into, and she had been holding it since the seventh without knowing that was what she was doing.
