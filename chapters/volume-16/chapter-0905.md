# A Ward of Her Own

She read it in the dark, which she had not done standing before, and standing is what readings are.
The lamp had gone on the seventeenth and there was no oil in the building and there had not been for nine years, and the only light past that was what came in through a doorless room at the top of a tower past the last landing, which was nothing.

So she stood at the near end of the table with her left hand on the register and moved it a breadth at a time, three fingers and a thumb finding the start of each cut the way a hand finds a step in the dark, and she said every line out loud before she let it be a line anywhere.

## The First of What Is Past

There were two days of it between the fourth of the four and where the cuts ran out under her fingers.

The fifth thing she found said this.

“She is carried up and is made an Ascendant, and a Ward of her own is set in her name, and the carrying is done whole and is not a taking, and the ninth is not the end of what she is put in the world for.”

She read it twice, standing, in the ordinary voice, first in her own voice, and the second time it came out of her differently and she stopped at the end of it and stood there.

“I am not hungry,” she said out loud, standing, into a room with no light in it. “I have not been hungry since the ninth. That is the first thing my body does at a strange sentence and it has never once done it at a figure.”

“That is not hunger,” he said. “Tell me what it is.”

“I do not know yet,” she said. “That is not a thing I have said out loud before and I am not saying it twice.”

## How It Sounds

He let it go. She had learned nine years ago that he let things go when she told him to and not before, and that was the single largest thing about him and the only one she had ever been able to rely on.

She read the five lines again and this time she listened to them, which was not the same work.

Read, they were a description of an arrangement. Spoken standing in her own voice in a dark room at the top of a tower on the twentieth of the month beginning two thousand nine hundred and fifty-four, they were something else entirely.

They sounded like somebody describing the rest of her life to her and calling it a mercy.

She stood very still in the dark with her hand flat on the stone and she heard it, and it was not a voice reading. It was a woman of nineteen hearing a thing about herself that she had never once been told, said in a room by a person who did not know her, on a night when she had nothing in her hands and nowhere to be.

Nineteen years. A voice since she was ten. Nine stages and a peak shut at the top of a mountain a month ago. An arm open at the elbow for six hundred and seventy-nine days and not going to close. One thousand four hundred and fourteen days gone at a rate she derived from her own life and not one of them coming back. A bound boy she had never spoken to. A brother four miles down and a long way off.

Nineteen years of arithmetic, every morning, standing, alone, with the figures going out into the machinery one at a time, and no one at any point in any of it had said one word about what it was for.

And here was a sentence in a register cut into a table, in a hand four hundred years old, that said she would be carried up whole and given a place of her own to keep.

A Ward of her own. She had stood behind the Wall once with the whole of the keeping in front of her, and a man who kept walls and a list had told her what those two things were for, and the whole of what she had learned from that week was that keeping is something people are given and not something they are owed. She had wanted a place of her own since she was a child.

## The Part That Said Yes

She had never said it out loud to anybody, including herself, and there is a difference between not saying a thing and not having said it, and the difference is that one of them can be taken away from you later by somebody who knows you.

The part of her that said yes said it before her mouth did, which was the thing she had come up nine hundred and four steps to be able to rule out.

She felt it go the way she felt the open place behind her fourth rib pull, which is to say she felt it without deciding to, and it was not small and it was not shameful and it did not go away when she looked at it.

She stood in the dark and let it be there.

“Yes,” she said out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice, to the room and to the stone and to the man. “That is the first thing anybody has ever put in front of me that is about me. Every other thing in nineteen years has been about a price or a stage or a record or a wall, and this one is about me, and a part of me is saying yes before I have finished reading it.”

“I heard it,” he said.

“I am telling you so you have it in your half. I am not asking your permission and I am not asking you to hold it for me.”

## What He Wanted

“I am not going to hold it for you,” he said. “I want you to want it.”

She stood with her hand on the register and waited for the rest of it, because there was always a rest of it.

“I want you to want it,” he said. “Not for me. For you. I have wanted one thing since I was nineteen and it has taken me six hundred years to get it into a sentence and I am not going to use that to get you to want a second one on the same morning. That is mine and it is mine to carry and I am carrying it.”

“And you want me to say so out loud if I do.”

“Yes,” he said. “I want you to say so out loud if you do, and I do not want you to say it if you do not, and I want you to want it in daylight the way you refuse in daylight, not in the dark where no count is being kept.”

It was the hardest sensible thing he had ever said to her and it sat in the room with her, unmeasured, with no figure on it at all.

“Why.”

“Because in the dark you said two words to a refusal and it went nine hundred and four steps and it could not be called back,” he said, “and I would rather you had not done that than that you had.”

## What He Knew About Wards

She stood in the dark with that and did not have an answer ready and for once did not go looking for one in arithmetic.

“A Ward,” she said. “Say it again and tell me what it is, because I have stood behind the Wall for a week and I have watched one man keep a list and I have not the least idea what the word means.”

“I have been inside the keeping for six hundred years,” he said. “I have never seen one that belonged to a person. I have read the word in places where it is applied to a place and never once in a place where it is applied to a man, and I do not know what it is, and I am not going to make it up for you at two in the morning in a dark room because you would believe me.”

“So I find it on the table.”

“You find it on the table,” he said, “and when you do you will not find it in the first block, because the first block is about you and not about what you would be given to do. It will be further on. And I want to warn you about one thing before you get to it.”

“What.”

“That you will find it the way you find everything,” he said, “by working out what is behind it, and this time there is going to be something behind it that is not about you at all, and you are going to want to put it down.”

## The Decision

She stood in the dark with that and did not ask him what he meant, because she had learned nine years ago not to ask him for the end of a thing he had not got to.

“No,” she said, at last, standing.

“No to the offer.”

“No to answering it today,” she said. “That is a different thing and I am going to keep them apart in the air where anybody can see them, because in two days I have entered this offer into the record in my own voice and I am not going to be a woman who can put a yes on the same table nine days later without having stood here first and read all of it to the end.”

She lifted her hands off the register and found the edge of the table with them instead.

“I am not refusing it. I have not refused anything since the twenty-fourth of the month beginning two thousand nine hundred and twenty-four and I am not starting at this. I am reading. Reading is not answering and it has been that way since the sixth line and it stays that way until I have been to the end of what is on this table and can stand at the end of it and know what I am saying yes to.”

“Read it all,” he said. “I want you to read it all and I want to be in the room for it.”

“You are in the room. You are the only thing in the room. That is the arrangement and it has been the arrangement for nine years.”

She stood at the near end of a table with no wear in it in a room with no light in it, with her hand off the register and five new lines in her mouth that she had put there standing in her own voice before they were a line anywhere, and she was nineteen years old, and nothing had ever been put in front of her that was about her before.
