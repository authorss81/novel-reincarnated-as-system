# The Words Went On Without Her

Five hundred and forty days is a year and a hundred and eighty days, and she said it out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice, in a lane outside a village in the third county, at about the ninth hour, to about nine people, and it was the first time she had ever said a number that covered a stretch of her own life which had not been a series of next days.

“A year and a hundred and eighty days,” she said. “That is what has happened since the seventh of the month beginning two thousand nine hundred and fifty-four. Three hundred and thirty-seven days since the thirtieth of a month beginning three thousand and one hundred and thirty-four, and nine of them were the fall, and the rest of it was roads.

“I have never had a year before. Not one. I had next days and I had a room and I had a wall, and the wall is nine hundred years of a thing and I have never once had a year inside it, and I did not know until this morning that that was an unusual thing to have gone without.”

The woman in the front of the nine said, “You are twenty.”

“I am twenty,” said Ning Yan, “and I worked that out twice on the road this week because I did not believe it the first time.”

## The Version That Got There First

There was a man of about thirty in the second county road who had been told she was going to take something from him.

He stood in the road with a cloth over his arm and a look on his face that had been built over about four days by other people, and when she came round the bend he said, “I have got it here.”

“What have you got.”

“What you are owed,” the man said. “It is not much. I want it done with.”

She stood in a road with about twenty people behind him and she worked out, standing, in the ordinary voice, in front of all of them, what had happened, and it took her about a count of two hundred.

It was that she had stood in a village twenty days back and said the three sentences to a woman of about thirty, and that the woman had asked whether there was a price, and that she had said she did not know what it cost her, and that the woman had heard *I do not know what it will cost you* and had gone and told four people that there was a price and that it was going to be taken out of the first person who could be found.

“Give it to the woman you heard it from,” Ning Yan said. “And tell her I said so, and that if she asks me again I will say the same thing again.”

The man said, “You are not going to take it.”

“I am not able to take it. I have not been able to be given a thing since I was nineteen years old and I am not going to manage it this morning.”

“You have just told me what to do.”

“I have told you who was wrong and what to do with the wrongness,” Ning Yan said. “That is a different thing and it is the only thing I have.”

The man stood in the road for a while and then put the cloth down on a wall and went and picked up his coat and stood beside it, and about four people watched him do it, and not one of them looked at the man who had brought it.

## What She Cannot Put Right

She tried, after that, to work out standing in the ordinary voice what she was going to do about the rest of it.

She could not send anybody. She could not follow a name she was handed. She could not be met where she was expected. She could not be given a thing, could not be paid, could not have a message brought, and there was not one office in nine counties that would take a word off her hands and carry it to the next place.

And a thing said once in a village is now in four mouths by the morning, and in sixteen by the third day, and there is not one instrument in the world that goes back along it.

She said that out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice, on the road, to about nine people, because it was true and because the rule was the rule.

“So I can put it right in front of the person it is in front of,” she said, “and there is one of those people for every one of the four that heard it wrong, and I am one woman and I am on foot, and there are six counties behind me.”

The man by the wall said, “Then how does it get right.”

“It gets right slow,” Ning Yan said, “and only in front of people, and one at a time, and every time somebody stands in front of another person and says it that is not me.”

That was the cost of it and it was the first thing the walking had taught her that she had not already known.

## What Three Hundred And Six Days Had Been

She did the rest of the arithmetic at the seventh hour, standing, in the ordinary voice, in the lane behind the village, because the ninth hour had gone past and she had said the two figures that were about her own life every morning for nineteen years and had stopped, and she wanted to know whether saying them out loud on the three hundred and sixth day of walking still came out the way it used to.

It did.

“Four hundred and sixteen days out of a room,” she said. “One thousand one hundred and ninety-nine days in that room before it. One thousand two hundred and six days with this arm open at the elbow, and it is not going to close, and I have checked it against the days twice this week because I did not believe the first answer.”

She stood in a lane with a lamp in her left hand that would never be lit again and said the three figures out loud to about nothing at all, and they came out the way they had every morning since she was ten, in the same order, in the same voice, and the order had nothing in it that she had put there.

That was the whole of it. Three hundred and six days of walking, and about nine thousand paces a week of them, and thirty-one villages where she had said it three and a half times, and about four hundred times she had said it to one person at a time, and not one of those villages knew anything about the other thirty.

## The People Waiting At A Place She Had Left

She got to the next village at about the fourth hour of the second day and there were people standing in the road.

Nine of them. They were not working and they were not passing through. They had been standing there long enough that one of them had moved a stool out of a door and sat down on it.

Ning Yan stopped about thirty paces off and she did not go any closer straight away, because she had learned in three hundred and six days of walking that people standing in a road looking for somebody were the most frightening thing she had met since the ceiling came off, and she had never once got the better of it by walking at them.

A man of about forty came to meet her in the middle.

“We were told you came through here,” he said. “About nine days back. A man with a broken shaft said a woman walked through and said three things.”

“A man with a broken shaft said it on the seventeenth day of a month beginning three thousand and one hundred and sixty-four,” Ning Yan said, “and I was here on that day and I said three things to about nine people, and I was not coming back.”

The man looked at her for a while.

“We have been here since the sixth day.”

“Why the sixth day.”

“He said you would come back through by the sixth day.”

And Ning Yan stood in a road in the fourth county, in front of nine people who had been standing in it for three days on a date a man with a broken shaft had made up in a village twenty days ago, and she worked out standing, in the ordinary voice, that she was not able to be met where she was expected, that she had said that out loud at a gate ten days after the fall, and that she had never once thought about what it would do to a person to be told that a date.

That was new. That was the first thing in three hundred and six days that she had not had a shape for.

“I am sorry for the three days,” she said. “I cannot stop it happening again and I am not able to tell anybody a date, because I do not know where I will be and there is nothing in the world that would carry a message.”

The man said, “Then stand here and say it.”

So she stood there and said it three and a half times over to nine people, in about two hours and a half, and the fourth time through a woman of about thirty came up the road behind them having heard from a boy in the next lane that there was a woman in the village saying a thing out loud.

That woman stood at the back and listened to the whole of it and then said, “I have been looking for you for a day.”

Not one person told her to come. She came because a boy said there was a woman saying a thing out loud, and the boy had heard it from the man with the broken shaft, and the man with the broken shaft had got it wrong about the sixth day and had been right about her.

She had spent three hundred and six days building a thing out of standing in front of people one at a time, and the thing had got out of her hands while she slept, and it was running ahead of her into nine counties at the speed of a boy on a lane, and she could not make it stop and could not make it right and could not be in two places.

At the ninth hour she had said it to thirteen people, and four counties were behind her, and she went west with the lamp in her left hand and a boy of about nine somewhere ahead of her with a slate on his knee who had been told she was the woman who counts.
