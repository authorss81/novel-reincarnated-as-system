# Four Minutes

He had it at about the second hour and she did not know that and would not have believed him if she had, because he had no hours and no day and nothing that turns.

She was standing at the near end of the table with both hands flat on the register when he said it.

“Four minutes,” he said.

“I am standing at the table.”

“I know where you are standing,” he said. “I have known every minute of every hour since the nineteenth of the month beginning two thousand eight hundred and thirty-four. Four minutes.”

He did not say it.

That was the first of the four minutes. A man at the edge of a thing he had been at the edge of for three days, with a date he had picked out loud and a woman standing at a table with her hands on a register, not saying anything.

She felt him come to it.

It was not a sound. It was the same thing she felt when a sentence of hers went out into the traffic, except it came the other way, up through her, and went through her and out, one word, in the ordinary voice.

“Yes.”

It went past her.

She felt it leave. That was the part she would carry for the rest of her life: she felt one word go out of a man and through her and into the keeping, and it did not come back into him, and there was no sound in the room at the top of a tower except a lamp.

Something looked at him.

Not her. She knew the difference between being looked at and being in the room when something else looked. It came up the channel and it had no shape and it went over him once, the way a hand goes over a thing to find what it is, and it found what it was, and it stayed.

He made a sound she had never heard him make. Not a word. Something shorter than a word.

“Being addressed,” he said. “Ning Yan. I have been designated and I have been recorded and I have been read, and I am being addressed now, and say something. I know you have said you will not speak. Say something anyway.”

She did not say anything.

It was the second of the four minutes and she had promised, and her hands stayed flat on the register and the arm open at the elbow hung against her side and did not move.

It came again. Not the looking. The remembering.

She felt it go through him and through her at once, the way a thing goes through a wire, and there was nothing in it she could check, and she wanted to check it so badly that her teeth came together.

“It is remembering me,” he said, after a silence that could not have been long. “Not Sheng. Sheng is a line in a column somewhere. I cannot hear what it says about me. I can only feel that it is saying it.”

Something arrived in him.

She felt it arrive and she felt where it landed and she felt the shape of it, a small thing going into a place where nothing small had ever gone, and her throat closed.

“What is that,” she said.

She broke it. She heard herself break it, the first word out of her mouth inside the four minutes she had promised in the ordinary voice, and she could not afterwards have said what made her do it.

“Do not,” he said.

“Was that the name.”

He did not answer for what could not have been more than a breath.

“Do not ask me that,” he said. “That is the one thing. I am asking you for the four minutes and you have given me three of them and you have just spent the last one, and I am not angry, and if you had asked me on the twenty-third of this month instead of now I would have answered you, and I am not going to answer you now.”

She put her hands flat on the register and she did not say another word.

A name had gone into him. She had been the wire it went through and it had not come into her as a sound, because it had not been said to her, and she had stood at a table and held the wire and had not been given one word of it.

Something else arrived after that, and it was bigger, and he took it the way a man takes a thing that has been carried up nine hundred and four steps for him.

He did not say what it was.

She waited for him to say what it was and he did not, and she wanted to ask him so badly that her hands came off the register and went back onto it.

Then he was quiet, and it was a different quiet, and she had heard him quiet for nine years and had never once heard this one.

He sounded like the nineteen-year-old who died apologising to the men who were cutting him open. He sounded like somebody who had been carrying a thing a very long way and had put it down on a table. There was no face on him to read and she heard all of it anyway, and he was happy.

He was the happiest she had ever heard anybody be.

She stood at the near end of the table with her hands flat on the register and watched him be happy for the last two minutes and she said nothing at all, because stopping him was hers to do and on the twenty-ninth of the month beginning two thousand nine hundred and fifty-four she had stood up at this table and said out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice, that it was not hers to make.

That was the worst thing she had ever done.

She did not do it again.

He stopped.

The four minutes were over.

Nothing happened.
The lamp burned on the stone beside her. The register lay under her hands. The table had no wear in it and the place at the far side had nothing worn at all, and eight flights and a stair below her a room was running on firsts, and the walls were up, and the layer was up, and the offer was answered.
