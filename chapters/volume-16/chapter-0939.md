# It Does Not Stop

The morning of the twenty-second of the month beginning three thousand and forty-four he began at the sixth hour and she knew inside about a count of ten that this one was going to be different, because he came in at the edge of it instead of at the front.

“I decided what you were going to be.”

She stood at the near end of her own table and let it go past her and waited for the cost at the end of it, and there was no cost at the end of it, and she put her hands flat on the wood and did not move.

It was not very good.

It was not very good because he went over the same ground twice inside the first three days of it, and because about the fourth thing in he started to say why and she had to stop him.

“Take the why out.”

“It belongs there.”

“It is a defence,” she said, “and you have not got to one, and if you put a why in the middle of that then everything on either side of it stops being a decision and becomes an argument about a decision.”

He was quiet for about a count of forty.

“That is a thing I have never once had to do,” he said. “I have explained six hundred years of things to people and not one of them has ever told me to leave the explanation in.”

“I am telling you now.”

“Take it out.”

It came out.

And he went on, and he did not get better, and he did not stop either.

## The Long Morning Of It

She sat with it from the sixth hour to the ninth hour the way she had sat with it on the thirteenth when nine beginnings had died in front of her, and this time she was not watching a man fail at the first place.

This was worse, and she understood why inside about a count of one hundred, and it was that there was nowhere for her to put anything. On the thirteenth she had known by the fourth beginning that he was not able to do it, and a woman can be patient with a thing she can see failing.

This did not fail visibly. It came out of a wall in the ordinary voice, one decision after another, and it went over the same ground and it forgot itself and it came back, and she had nothing to say about any of it because there was nothing wrong with any single piece of it that she could put a finger on.

About the seventh hour he said one of her decisions and got it slightly wrong, and she knew it was wrong, and she did not correct him.

It was the decision about the lamp in this room, and about what was on the shelf, and about what she was to do with the oil a boy makes in a building with no store in it.

He said it as one thing and it had been three things done on three separate mornings for reasons she could have named, and she stood at the near end of her own table and let a man in a wall get it wrong about the lamp in a room she has lived in for seven hundred and sixty-four days.

That was the part of the morning she was not going to put back in order and was never going to tell him about.

“Say it again,” he said, at about the eighth hour. “The lamp. Say the decision about the lamp as it was.”

“It was three.”

“I know it was three. I am asking you to tell me.”

“You put the oil on the shelf first,” she said. “You decided I was not to be given anything.

And then the second morning you decided I was to put it in what a boy makes and not be given that either, and you said it to me as one instruction when it was two, and on the third morning you decided that I was to stop asking where it came from, and you have been a voice in my head for nine years and you are asking me to check a thing you have had nine years to have right.”

“Say all of that again.”

“No.”

He was quiet for about a count of two hundred.

“You are the only person in the world who has ever made me repeat myself,” he said, “and you are doing it to correct a decision about an oil lamp.”

“I am doing it because you said a thing about me that was wrong and I have spent nineteen years being careful about what I let go past.”

“All right,” he said. “Let it go past.”

“Why did you not correct me,” he said, at about the eighth hour.

“You were in there nine years.”

“No. That is not the reason and you know that is not the reason.”

“It is not that I wanted to hear it right,” she said. “I want to hear every one of them right. I have wanted to hear every one of them right since I was ten years old.”

“Then why.”

“Because you are saying it for the first time and you are hearing yourself do it,” she said. “If I correct you, you will hear it from me, and you will not have heard it from you.”

He was quiet for about a count of two hundred.

“That is very bad of you,” he said.

“Yes.”

“That is the worst thing anybody has ever done in this room for a man trying to say something and you have done it because it was useful to you and you knew it while you were doing it.”

“Yes,” she said.

He went on.

## Where He Went Past The Hardest Part

There is a thing in a life where the worst of it is, and everybody who has ever lived has one, and she knew where hers was without having to look for it because it had been on the ninth of the month beginning two thousand eight hundred and thirty-four in a furnace crew in Qinghe and it had been a girl of ten who did not run.

She sat at the near end of her own table on the twenty-second of the month beginning three thousand and forty-four with a lamp burning at her feet and waited for him to come to it, because it was the middle of the whole of what he had done to her and there was no way to get to the far end of it without going through.

He got to about the ninth hour and it did not stop, and it did not slow down, and it went straight past.

She had told him on the sixteenth of the month before last that she had been made less solid for nine years by a voice in her head. He had known that since the eighth of that month and he had said it out loud in this room on the sixteenth of the month before last, in her own voice, standing, and the layer had taken it.

He did not apologise for it on the twenty-second.

He went on past it to two smaller things and then to a thing about the ninth stage and then to something she had not heard of at all.

## The Place At The Far Side

“There is a place at the far side of that table.”

She stopped breathing for about a count of two and started again.

“It is kept,” he said. “It has been kept four hundred years and nothing has ever been sat in it and you have not looked at it and I have not said one word to you about it in nine years.

“I decided it would be offered to you. I decided that before it was offered. I did not decide who would offer it and I did not decide what would be said when you said no. I decided that you would not sit in it.

“And I want you to hear the shape of that, because there is a count of a hundred in it and I am not able to put the count in: I knew what you would do and I let a thing be put in front of you to find out.”

The lamp burned on the stone at her feet and the far end of the room was dark where it had been dark for nineteen years.

“You decided I would refuse.”

“I decided that you would not sit in it,” he said. “That is not the same and I have had thirty days to be exact about it and that is the exact shape of it. I never knew when. I never knew how many times. I knew the answer and I let the question be asked.”

She did not say anything to that.

She went up the room.

She had been in this room seven hundred and sixty-four days and she had not gone up it and stood over that place once. She went the length of the table with her hand along the wood and then she went past the far end of it and stood four feet off the place and looked at it for the first time in nineteen years.

It was not worn. That was the thing she had gone up the room to look at and she found it out in about a count of forty. Four hundred years of a table with a place kept at the far side of it, and there was no hollow in the wood there at all, and she had spent nine years reading hollows off that table like a woman reading a page, and she had walked past the one that was not there every morning of her life.

There was nothing worn at all in a place that had been kept for four hundred years for somebody.

She stood over it and understood that the whole of it had been decided before she was offered it, and that she had been asked, twice, by a thing that had known her answer, and that a woman of nineteen had gone on reading a worn table her whole life and had not once looked down at the one place on it that had never been touched.
