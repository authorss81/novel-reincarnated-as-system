# As Long As Useful

“Two hundred and ninety-four days since the ninth stage. Eighty-seven since it was shut. Seven hundred and thirty-two days with this arm open at the elbow. One thousand four hundred and sixty-seven gone at this morning.”

She said it standing at the near end of the table in her own room on the thirteenth of the month beginning three thousand and fourteen, at about the sixth hour, and then she stood there with her hands flat on the wood and set the other figure up beside it.

“One thousand four hundred and sixty-seven days in my body,” she said, “and eleven before me.”

He did not answer.

“Set them against each other,” she said. “That is what you said you would do and I have been doing it myself since the seventh of this month and I cannot finish it. Give me the other side of it.”

“I have given you the other side of it.”

“You have given me a word,” she said. “You have given me as long as it takes to be useful. That is not a figure. That is a rule with no number in it, and I have been standing in this room for thirteen days trying to turn a rule with no number in it into a number, and I am not able to do it, and that has never once happened to me.”

## The Arithmetic She Could Not Do

She walked the length of the table and back, twice, with her hands along the wood.

“How long was the first one.”

“I do not know.”

“How long was the second one.”

“I do not know.”

“How long was the eleventh.”

“I do not know,” he said, “and it is not because I am not willing. There is no column and there is nowhere for the length to have been kept and I have looked for it in the one place I can still look, which is the going of the thing itself, and there is no length in it. It is exactly that, over and over, for nine hundred years, and the only thing it has ever had a figure for is you.”

She put her hand flat on the table.

“Then say what you know.”

“The tax has been running for nine hundred years,” he said. “It finds one at a time. There has been one of these at a time in the whole of that, and there have been eleven finished ones and you, and there is nothing else in the nine hundred years I can put next to them.”

She stood there and worked on it, standing, the way she worked on everything.

Nine hundred years. Eleven finished. One running.

“Then the interval is not fixed.”

“No.”

“Then it is not ninety years and it is not eleven years and it is not whatever I could divide nine hundred by and get.”

“No,” he said. “It is however long the last one took to be of use. That is the whole of the answer and it is the only one there is and it is not a figure and there is not going to be one.”

## The Worst Of It

He was quiet for a count of a hundred.

“I want you to have the worst of it,” he said.

“Which is.”

“I want you to stop turning it,” he said. “You have been standing in this room for thirteen days trying to get a number out of a machine that does not keep numbers, and every morning you have come back to it and every morning it has not had one, and you are going to keep coming back to it until something in you breaks or something in me gives, and I would rather you had it plainly today than worked it out of me over the next month.”

She stood at the near end of the table with her hands flat on the wood.

“You have never once in nineteen years told me to stop doing a piece of arithmetic.”

“No.”

“Why now.”

“Because on the seventh of this month you watched me be the happiest you have ever heard anybody be for four minutes without speaking,” he said, “and thirteen days ago you stood up at that table and used the one rule in this room on me, and both of those were you putting something down for me. I have noticed both of them and I have not said one word about either and I am saying one word about this one now.”

## Her Own Length

“Then tell me what mine is going to be.”

“No.”

“You do not know.”

“No,” he said. “I do not know. And I want you to hear the shape of the not knowing, because there is a shape to it and I have had six hundred years to get it right. When a person is useful, the machine does not decide anything. There is no moment.

“It goes on being kept and going on being needed, and not one person in it is told, and one day it is not needed any more and that day was never on anybody’s board. Eleven people have been through that and not one of them knew the day while they were in it. You will not know your day either. That is not cruelty. That is the whole design.”

She stood very still.

“So I am not going to be told when.”

“No.”

“And it will not be a day I can feel.”

“No,” he said. “And I have had six hundred years to want to tell you and I have not, because there is no way to tell a woman the length of her own use and I have never wanted to be the one who has to try.”

She sat down on the stone at the near end of the table, because standing was for saying and there was nothing left to say standing.

A count of breath. Four on the quiet. Nine sitting still.

The lamp stood on the stone beside her and threw its light along the floor and up the leg of the table and no higher, and the bar was a foot from her knees and the boards lay face up a foot in from the near end and the strip of pale wood lay face up under the table at the near end of the leg, and the far side of the room was not in any of it.

She looked at the near side of her own room for a while, the way a person looks at the part of a thing that has been in front of them for nineteen years.

Nine stages and a peak shut at the top of a mountain a month and a half ago. An arm open at the elbow for seven hundred and thirty-two days. Three fingers and a thumb. Seven of eleven holds. A small open place behind the fourth rib the size of her thumbnail. One thousand four hundred and sixty-seven days gone at a rate she derived out of her own life.

Every one of those was hers and every one of those was the price of a voice in her head, and she had carried all of it for nine years, and she had been told on the seventh of a month that it was the cost of keeping the world bounded and gentle.

She put her hand behind her own ribs and left it there.

## The Same Answer

“Then tell me one thing with the same shape in it.”

“Say which.”

“How long are the walls going to stand.”

He was quiet for a long time.

“The same answer,” he said. “Nine hundred years so far. No column. No day on anybody’s board. I have been on the inside of it since before there was an inside to be on and I cannot tell you whether the bound is a keeping or a building that is going to fall down, and I have never once been asked, and you have just asked it on the thirteenth of a month in a room with a lamp on the floor.”

“Nine hundred years and eleven people.”

“Nine hundred years and eleven people and one of you,” he said, “and that is every figure in it, and you have just heard the two of them in one sentence and they are the same figure.”

She sat there with that.

“That is the first time the arithmetic has ever come up short,” she said. “Nineteen years. I have counted everything since I was ten years old. Every day, every stage, every cost, every interval between two dates I could put my hands on.

“And I cannot get a figure on how long I am going to be useful to the thing that is using me, and I cannot get a figure on how long the walls stand, and neither of those is because I am stupid. It is because the machine does not keep numbers and it has never once pretended to.”

“Yes.”

“The one thing you can do with an unnumbered length,” she said, “is be somewhere in it on purpose.”

He said nothing at all.

“I have a stair above me that goes nine hundred and four steps to a table with no wear in it and a stair below me that goes down and out of this building, and I have used both of them for reasons that were the next day, and there is a day coming where I will stand somewhere that is not this room for a reason that is not the next day, and I will not have a figure in my hands when I do it.”

“You do not know that.”

“I know it the way I know everything else in this room,” she said, “which is by standing in the dark and having it come up. I am not asking you to confirm it. I am telling you I have one thing that does not carry a number and that it is the only one, and that I have had it since the seventh of the month before last and I have not spent it.”

She put her hand up under her own ribs on the left side, high, behind the fourth one, and found the small open place that was there, the size of her thumbnail, unhealed since the third of the month beginning two thousand two hundred and four.

The place was smaller than her thumbnail and it did not feel like anything. She had worked that out on the third of the month beginning two thousand two hundred and four when she found it and had gone looking at it for most of a night, and it had not felt like anything then either, and she had looked at it every few years after that and it had never once felt like anything.

It was not a wound. She had spent nineteen years expecting it to be a wound, and it had never been a wound, and there was nothing in her hand when she touched it, and the only thing that had ever been in her hand about it was the knowledge that it was there.

She had touched it by accident more times than she had ever counted, in nineteen years.

She had not touched it once on purpose until this morning, and she sat on the stone floor of a room no one could come into with her hand on it and did not say anything else at all.
