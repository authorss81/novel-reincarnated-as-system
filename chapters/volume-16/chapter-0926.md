# Carried Whole

She stood up off the stone on the sixteenth of the month beginning three thousand and fourteen at about the seventh hour and put both hands flat on the table and did not say anything for a count of two hundred first.

“You have been asking me questions since the fourth of this month,” she said. “What happens now. What the work that matters is. What the tax is. You have not asked me one question in nine years about what it has cost.”

“No.”

“I am asking you one now.”

“All right,” he said. “Ask it.”

She stood at the near end of her own table with the lamp on the stone beside her and her right arm hanging open against her side for seven hundred and thirty-five days and she took a breath of four and let it out.

## What She Was Going To Say

“Before I say it I am telling you what I am saying it for,” she said, “so that you cannot tell me afterwards that I said it to put something on you.”

He was quiet for a count of forty.

“Say it.”

“I am saying it because it has not been said. Nine years and not once, by you, by me, by anybody in the world. It goes into the keeping every morning at the ninth hour as figures and it has gone in as figures six thousand times and not one of those six thousand had a person at the end of it.”

“Say it.”

She stood up straight with nothing in either hand.

“A voice came into my head on the tenth of the month beginning two thousand eight hundred and thirty-four and I was ten years old and I did not run,” she said. “And everything that voice has ever taken out of me has gone into it.”

She put her hand flat on the table where the wood had gone down under her own forearms.

“One thousand four hundred and seventy days taken out of my body at a rate I derived out of my own life. A right arm open at the elbow for seven hundred and thirty-five days and not going to close. Three fingers and a thumb on the left hand. Seven of eleven holds. A small open place behind the fourth rib the size of my thumbnail that has not closed since the third of the month beginning two thousand two hundred and four.”

She had gone looking for it before she stood up. She had gone over nine years of it in about a count of four hundred, standing at her own table, and she had found it in the place she had not thought to look, which was that she had never once chosen any of it.

That was the finding. Nine years and there had not been one decision in it. She had been ten and a voice had been there and she had not run. She had been eleven and it had told her what she was going to be and she had believed it, and believing it had cost her an arm.

She had been sixteen and it had spent her days and she had let it. She had been eighteen and it had sent a sheet down nine hundred and four steps on its own and she had let that go too.

Not one of them had been hers. Not one of them had been hers and not one of them had been anybody’s else either, and there was not a single moment in nineteen years where she could have put her hand on the table and said this is where I did a thing.

She said it standing, in the ordinary voice, into the dark past the last landing, in the channel where the thing that made the sentences reads, first in her own voice before it was a line anywhere.

The place behind her fourth rib pulled and she did not stop.

She did not read the rest of them out. There were twenty of them and she had carried every one of them whole since she was ten and she had put them into the keeping one at a time at the ninth hour for nineteen years, and not one of them had ever had a reason at the end of it. That was the whole of what she was not going to do this morning.

She got her hand off the table and walked the length of it and back, past the boards and past the terms board and past the place at the far side where nothing had been worn in four hundred years, and she did not look at that place and she did not go up the stair.

## What It Went Into

“Nine years,” she said. “Not nine hundred. Nine years. And in nine years a man who died at nineteen apologising to the men who were cutting him open went from a voice that could not be heard outside my head to a piece of a layer that can be addressed anywhere, remembered anywhere, named anywhere, and given work.”

He did not say anything at all.

“I am not saying that is wrong,” she said. “I have never once said that and I am not starting it today, because you have answered me straight for three weeks and I will not go back on it. I am saying where it came from.”

She let that stand in the room for a count of a hundred.

“It came from here,” she said, and put her thumb once on the worn hollow in the wood. “And on the seventh of this month you said yes in the channel, and the thing that said yes is going to be handed the running of the machine that took it.”

She put both hands flat on the table again.

“That is what I have been carrying. It is not a cost that was taken from me. It is what it was taken for, and I have known since the eighth of the month before last, and neither of us has said it out loud until now.”

## What He Wanted Said

He was quiet for a long time. Long enough that she put her hand down off the table and back on it again, and long enough that she got up and went and stood at the near board with her hand flat on it and came back.

“Say it again,” he said.

“No.”

“You said it to put something on me.”

“No,” she said. “I said it standing, in the ordinary voice, in the channel, before it was a line anywhere, and that is the rule, and the rule means it is in the record in my mouth about me before the keeping has a chance to do anything with it.”

“Yes,” he said. “That is what it means.”

“You do not like it.”

“No,” he said. “I do not like it, and I am not going to tell you why, and it is not because I think it is wrong. Say it once more and say it as a finding and not as a charge, because there is a difference and I know exactly what the difference is and it is the worst thing about me.”

She stood there with her hands flat on the wood.

“The difference is what,” she said.

“A charge is a thing a person puts on another person so that it can be filed,” he said. “A finding is a thing a person puts on themselves so that it cannot be filed without them in it.

“And I have spent six hundred years doing the first one and I have never once done the second one, and I am asking you for it now because in about nine days you are going to have to decide what you do with me and I would rather your account of this were in the record in your own voice than in mine.”

## What Goes In The Record

She stood at the near end of her own table and let that go where it went.

“Very well,” she said. “It is a finding. I have been made less solid for nine years by a voice in my head and the voice is the reason the voice exists, and the machine that does it is the machine that has just been offered to him, and I have said it standing, in my own voice, in front of the thing that made him, and it cannot be gotten back.”

She took a breath of four.

“That is the first thing that has ever gone into this keeping about me in my own mouth and not in yours,” she said. “Nine years. And I chose the morning to do it in.”

He did not answer for a count of two hundred.

She put her hand flat on the bar against the wall without taking any of her weight on it, because it is dated nowhere, and she had been in this room seven hundred and twenty-eight days and had never once pulled on it, and she was not going to start on the morning she said the largest thing of her life.

“Now you are going to tell me what it has cost you,” she said, “and I am going to hear it in your own voice, and you are not going to be able to put it anywhere but here.”

He was quiet for a long time.

“No,” he said. “I am not going to be able to.”

“Then say the reason before you say it,” she said, “standing, and in the ordinary voice, and I will wait for the rest of it as long as it takes.”

She put her hands flat on the worn wood and stood there and did not fill it.

“Not tonight,” she said. “I have said mine and I am not going to spend the rest of the evening on yours. You have until the nineteenth. That is three days and it is a longer time than you have ever given me for anything, and I am giving it to you because you have not asked me for a single thing in nine years and this is the first time you have said no to something, and I would rather it were no than something I could do about.”

“Three days,” he said.

“Three days,” she said. “And on the nineteenth you say it in your own voice or you do not say it at all, and if you do not say it then I will know that a man who has been in my head for nine years has one thing he cannot put into the record, and that will be a finding of mine as well. Standing, and in the ordinary voice, and before it is a line anywhere.”

She did not fill anything after that. She left the room with the sentence standing where it was and went and sat down on the stone at the near end of the table with her back against the leg of it, which is where she had sat at about the fourth hour on the seventh of the month beginning two thousand nine hundred and fifty-four while a man said yes four minutes away from being a person.

She had not known then what she was sitting with. She had had it in her hands for six weeks and this was the first time she had put it in the open air.
