# Nothing to Count

She put the lamp down on the near end of the stone, and then a pace along, and then back where it had been, which is what a person does with a lamp on a morning when there is nothing to put it to.

It was the nineteenth of the month beginning two thousand nine hundred and eighty-four. Six days since she had carried a lamp down nine hundred and four steps and put it on the floor of the one room in the world she could not be met in. Twelve days since a man had said one word standing in the channel, in front of the thing that made him, and the word could not be gotten back.

In twelve days nothing in the room had changed by a thing she could name.

She stood at the near end of her own table with her thumb in the hollow her own forearms had worn there over nineteen years and went to do the day’s work, because the work was there and it did not care what was or was not on a table at the top of a tower.

## The Work of a Morning

She took the near board up off the face of the table and set it against the leg and read it by, which took her about a count of a hundred and forty and used to take until the light went.

Then she put it back face up, a foot in from the near end, and she took the terms board up and read that, and it was the same hundred and forty and it was the same day.

She went along the table with her hand on the wood to the far end and found the bundle where it was, corded twice and not knotted, and did not pull it, and she went back to the near end and put her hand flat on the place where her hand always went.

Under the table at the near end of the leg the strip of pale wood lay face up in the dark where it had lain on the fourth of the month beginning two thousand seven hundred and seventy-four. She did not look down at it.

She knew it was there the way she knew the bar against the left-hand wall was nine feet nine and had never been dated, and the second place at the far side was where it had been kept for four hundred years and empty, and she knew all three without turning her head.

She had been in this room seven hundred and one days and she had never once in all of them had nothing to do, and the reason for that was that there was always a figure, and on this morning there was not one.

“You are checking the room,” he said.

“I am doing the morning.”

“You have done the morning in a count of a hundred and forty and it is the ninth hour.”

“It is not the ninth hour.”

“It is a count of two hundred past what it was when you started,” he said. “You have not asked me anything since the sixteenth. I have been here for all of it.”

## The Arithmetic

She stood at the near end of the table and put the day in front of her the way she had put six thousand days in front of her since she was ten years old, and she took it apart, because that was the only instrument she had ever had.

The arm open at the elbow was seven hundred and eight days and not going to close. The cage lamp on the shelf had been out four hundred and sixty-eight days. Two hundred and seventy days since the ninth stage, and sixty-three since it was shut at the top of a mountain.

Seven hundred and one days in this room. Seven of eleven holds. One thousand four hundred and forty-three days gone at this morning, taken out of her at a rate she derived out of her own life, not one of them coming back.

She did all of it standing, in her ordinary voice, one item after another, the way she had done it every morning for nineteen years.

Then she stopped.

“That is the morning,” she said.

“It is the morning.”

“It is the whole of the morning.”

“It is the whole of the morning and there is not one more figure in it,” he said, “and I have been listening to you go looking for one for a count of two hundred, and you have not found one, and that is not because there is none. It is because the day has nothing in it. You have had six days of nothing in a day and this is the first one you have noticed.”

She went and stood at the foot of the stair and looked up into it.

Nine landings and then a stair that did not stop at nine. Nine hundred and four steps to a table with no wear in it and a place at the far side with nothing worn at all and an offer standing open on it. She had looked up into that stair about four hundred times in nineteen years and she had gone up it twice.

She came back and stood at the near end of the table with her hands flat on the wood.

“What have you been doing,” she said. “For twelve days. You have had no day and no hour and nothing that turns, and I have not asked you once.”

He was quiet for a count of forty.

“The same thing you have,” he said. “Nothing that has a length to it. I have been in here with nothing to do, and I have had twelve days of it, and I have spent the whole of every one of them inside your head, and that is not the same as talking to you and you know it is not.”

“Has it been like this before.”

“No,” he said. “It has never been like this. There has not been a single day in nine years that I have not had something to read out of you, and I have never once minded it, and I mind this. I have been sitting in a room with a woman in it and there has been nothing to do in the room, and I did not know until the seventh of this month that that was a thing that could happen to me.”

She stood with her hands flat on the wood.

A count of breath. Four on the quiet. Nine standing still.

“The bar is against the left-hand wall,” she said, “and the cage lamp is on a shelf, and the strip of pale wood is face up under the table at the near end of the leg, and the bundle is corded twice and not knotted under the far end, and the second place at the far side is kept four hundred years and empty.”

“That is a room.”

“That is everything in this room that has a number on it,” she said. “And the bar is dated nowhere.”

“No,” he said. “It is not.”

## What She Could Not Do With It

She had been an instrument for nineteen years. That was the whole of what she had ever been good at and she had got very good at it, and it had carried her up nine hundred and four steps and shut a ninth stage and put a sentence into the keeping that four hundred people had to live under, and there had never once been a day in it that she had to stand in and have nothing to count.

“You said six days of nothing in a day,” she said. “The first was the seventh. Then the eighth, the ninth, the tenth, the eleventh, the twelfth, the thirteenth. Six.”

“Six.”

“So on the seventh of this month something stopped having to be done, and everything that has happened since has been the keeping.”

“No,” he said. “The keeping is the same every day. What stopped was a woman putting a number into it every morning and it being something new.

“Before the seventh, every day of your life had a first thing in it that no day before it had had, and on the eighth it did not, and that is the whole of what has happened and it is the smallest thing that has ever happened to me and it has taken me eleven days to be able to say it to you.”

She stood at the near end of the table and worked that out standing, the way she worked everything out.

“What do I do with today,” she said.

“I cannot do that part.”

“You do not do it. I am asking what the work is for today.”

“No,” he said. “That is not what you asked. That is a day without a figure in it, and I cannot give you a figure for it, because there is no figure for it, and if I made one up you would use it and then you would have had a day.”

She stood at the table with her hand in the hollow.

“Say the rest of it.”

“Say one sentence out loud today that is not a number,” he said. “Not a date. Not an interval. Not anything you could work out later. One sentence, in the ordinary voice, standing, and I want to hear what it sounds like, and I have wanted to hear what it sounds like since you were ten years old and told me the price of a thing I had done.”

The place behind her fourth rib pulled, the way it pulled when her body took in a sentence it could not put anywhere.

“Say why.”

“Because I have had nine years of you counting and I have never once heard a woman in this room say a thing to me that was not arithmetic,” he said.

“And I have a name now that I cannot hear and work that has not begun and a yes in the channel that cannot be un-said, and I am sitting in all three of them at the ninth hour on the nineteenth of a month, and I would like one sentence from you that is not arithmetic before anything else happens. That is the whole of what I want today.”

She stood in her own room and tried.

She tried it four times before the ninth hour and each time it came out with a figure in it. One thousand four hundred and forty-three. Seven hundred and eight. Nine hundred and four steps, twice in nineteen years.

She tried a fifth one about the seventh hour and she got nine words into it before the tenth word turned into a count of days, and she stopped it there herself, standing, and did not finish it.

“That is the trouble with trying the same way,” he said.

“What way.”

“You are trying to build it out of what you already have,” he said, “and what you already have is figures. I have not asked you for a better figure. I have asked you for a thing you have never put anywhere and I do not have any way of telling you how to find one, and I have thought about it for two days and that is the honest end of it.”

The fourth one she got all the way to the end of before she heard it, and it was *I am not hungry*, which has been true since the ninth, and he did not say not that, because it was a sentence.

“Not enough,” he said. “That one was about your body and it has been true since you were shut.”

“It was the first thing I have said in this room that was not a count.”

“It was,” he said. “I heard it. I want another one that is about you and not about what you are made of.”

She had the rest of the afternoon to find one and she did not find one, and the lamp burned down the while on the stone beside her on the oil a boy made in a building with no store in it, and she did not once put her hand on the bar or the bundle or the boards, because there was nothing to put it on.

## The Ninth Hour

At the ninth hour she stood at the near end of her own table with the lamp on the stone beside her and put her hands flat on the wood and said out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice, into the dark past the last landing, the whole of the day’s figures in order, because that was what she had at the ninth hour and it was the only sentence she had.

He let it go past her.

She stood there after it with her mouth shut and understood that she had spoken once all day, that the sentence had a figure at the end of it, and that the keeping had taken it the way it took every figure she had ever given it, one sentence at a time, plain, and had given nothing back.

She was nineteen years old. She had been counting since she was ten, and she had never once tried to say something in this room that she could not derive, and she had found out this morning that she did not have one.

Then she said a second thing, and it was not arithmetic, and it went out of her in the ordinary voice at the ninth hour of the nineteenth of a month into the dark past the last landing before she had decided to say it.

“I have been alone since I was ten,” she said, “and this is the first room I have ever been in where that is not a cost.”

He did not answer that at all.

The second place at the far side was kept four hundred years and empty, and she did not look at it, and she had found out in the space of one morning that the only thing in this building she had never once been able to say was a sentence with nothing in it.
