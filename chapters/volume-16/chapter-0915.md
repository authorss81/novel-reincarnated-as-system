# What She Wants

Three days in her own room and she had not once minded it, and on the sixteenth of the month beginning two thousand nine hundred and eighty-four at about the ninth hour she stood up off the stone at the near end of the table and found out why.

The ninth hour was when the keeper read. She had put a number into the channel at the ninth hour on the twenty-second of the month beginning two thousand eight hundred and ninety-four and it could not be gotten back, and she had stood in this room and said it standing in the ordinary voice, and the whole of the bound had heard it at once.

She stood at the near end of her own table with the lamp on the stone floor beside her and her hands flat on the worn wood, three fingers and a thumb, the arm open at the elbow for seven hundred and five days.

“I am here,” she said, out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice. “I am in the room I have been in for six hundred and ninety-eight days and I will answer anything that is asked of me.”

## The Ninth Hour Again

Nothing answered.

She gave it a count of a hundred and then said it again, and it went out the way everything she had ever said had gone out, one sentence at a time, plain, reporting, and it did not come back and nothing came the other way.

That was the part. She had been listening for a whole count of a hundred with her hands flat on a table that had wear in it, and there had been no shape in the silence of the kind she had learned to tell from being gone, and there was no shape in it of any kind.

“Not the layer,” he said. “Say it out loud, because I want to be standing with you when you work it out and you are not going to get there on your own in a count of four hundred.”

“Not the layer.”

## What Cannot Be Met

“No. I can hear the traffic going past this room the way I have heard it every day for six hundred and ninety-eight days. It is going past. What it is not doing is stopping.”

She stood at the near end of the table with that and did it in the arithmetic, which was the only way she had ever got anywhere.

She could not be met where she is expected. She had carried that since the third of the month beginning two thousand two hundred and four and she had never once had it in her mouth as a finding because she had had no reason to, and the reason was that not one person had ever come looking for her.

Six hundred and ninety-eight days in this room, and before this room a stair in the same tower, and before that other rooms she cannot name in the order she passed through them. A boy who carried oil up past the ninth landing twice a day for two hundred days and put it down on the twelfth and had never once come through a door. A bearer who came and went and was paid out of a forearm and did not stay.

A voice in her head since she was ten. And a woman who could not be met in any of it, standing at the near end of a table with wear in it, saying out loud at the ninth hour that she was here.

“Not one person has ever come into this room,” she said.

“No,” he said. “Not once in nineteen years. Not when you were ten and not last year. You have been somewhere she cannot be found since you were ten years old and you have been keeping figures there the whole time.”

“That is the cost,” she said. “That has been the cost the whole time and I never once worked it out, because I was being met everywhere else. The reach answered. The voice answered. The stair was a place to be found on. And this was the one room I was sure of, and it is the one room in the world I cannot be met in.”

He was quiet for a count of forty.

“Yes,” he said. “That is what it is. And you are standing in it.”

“I am not leaving it.”
“No,” he said. “I have watched you not leave things for nineteen years and I am not going to be the one who argues you out of the one room you have ever had. You are not leaving it and I would not want you to and I want you to hear me say that, because you have spent six days thinking you have given something up and you have not.”

She put her thumb into the hollow in the table that her own forearms had worn there over nineteen years.

“You have walked nine hundred and four steps down a dark stair to the one place you cannot be met in, and that is not a loss. That is a woman going where she is known.”

She stood with that and put her hands flat on the worn wood where her forearms had gone ten thousand times.

“Being known is not the same as being found,” she said. “I have been known since I was ten and being known has never once put anybody in this room. You have said the word as though it settles something.”

“It does not settle anything,” he said. “I said it because you have spent six days walking down a stair telling yourself you gave something up, and you did not, and I would rather say something that is not useful than say nothing.”

“That is the first useful thing you have said to me since the seventh,” she said.

## What He Asked Her

“It is the first true one,” he said. “I have had four days of not being able to check my own word and it has made me worse company, not better. You are going to have to bear with me while I say things I cannot support.”

“Nineteen years,” he said, and she heard him change, the way he changed when something was coming that he had decided to say rather than keep.

“Nineteen years.”

“You asked me what I wanted,” he said. “At the ninth hour on the seventh of the month beginning two thousand nine hundred and fifty-four, standing, in the ordinary voice, in front of the thing that made me, and it was the first question you had asked me in nineteen years and I did not answer it for a whole day.”

“I asked you why you did not answer it.”
“I did not answer that either,” he said. “That is not the same as the first one. The first one is mine and I am answering it and I have answered it, and I am going to stop you putting them together, because what I am about to ask you has never once been asked of you by anybody in this world.”

She had her hands flat on the table and had not moved them.

“I am asking it now while you are standing in the only room you have, because if I wait until tomorrow you will have counted and counting is a kind of armour and I want you without it.”

She put her hands still on the table.

“Say it.”

“What do you want, Ning Yan.”

The room sat there with it.
She opened her mouth and there was nothing in it.

Nineteen years of a life built entirely out of things she was after, and every one of them had been a thing somebody else had put in front of her and then taken away, and she had wanted all of them with a completeness she had never once had cause to look at, and now a man who could hear the inside of her head had asked her a question with nothing in the question and she had no answer at all.

“I want,” she said, and stopped.

She stood there at the near end of a table with wear in it, in a lamp an eyelash, in a room that no one alive could find her in, and the whole of the channel was open and the whole of the keeping could have heard it, and she was nineteen years old, and she did not know.

“Tell me when it begins,” she said.

“That is not an answer.”

“No. It is the first question I have ever wanted to ask anybody,” she said, “and I have been putting it off for nine days because I did not want to spend the asking on a man who has already been given everything.”

He was quiet for a count of a hundred.

“When it begins,” he said, “I want you to be in a room I can be met in.”

“That is the whole of what you want.”

“That is the whole of what I have said I want since the eighth, and you already know the rest of it, and this is the first piece of it you have ever heard me say that is not about me.”

She stood at the near end of the table and understood that there was no answer to that in nineteen years of record, and that the not being able to meet him was the same cost as the not being able to be met, and that it had been the same cost for the whole nineteen years and neither of them had ever once said so out loud, and that was the first thing either of them had said that was about the future in six hundred years.
