# It Came About By Itself

There were people in the yard above Lowwater when she came back to it, and they were not in the yard for her.

That was the first thing and it took her about a count of six hundred to stand in a lane with a lamp in her left hand and work out that it was true.

There was a boy of about ten on an upturned bucket with a slate on his knee copying a line of writing very slowly with a stick in the wet. There were two men at the far wall doing something to a length of timber. A woman of about forty was beating a mat and two girls were carrying water in and out of the east side of it and going out by the west side without any arrangement about it, because they had been doing it long enough not to need one.

Not one of them looked up when she came up the lane. Not one of them stopped.

## The Arithmetic Of The Lane

She said the figures out loud on the first morning, standing, in the ordinary voice, at the top of the lane above Lowwater, before she went down into the yard, because there were seven hundred and sixty days between the last place she had stood in and this one and there is no way of crossing that in a head.

“Two thousand seven hundred days since the seventh of the month beginning two thousand nine hundred and fifty-four. That is seven years and a hundred and eighty days and I am twenty-six.

“Two thousand four hundred and ninety-seven days since the thirtieth of a month beginning three thousand and one hundred and thirty-four.

“Two thousand five hundred and seventy-six days out of a room. Three thousand three hundred and fifty-nine days in that room before it. Three thousand three hundred and sixty-six days with this arm open at the elbow.”

She said them in the order she had said them in since she was ten years old and she stopped at the fourth, and about nine people in a yard were going to hear the rest of it over the next four days, and not one of them knew yet that there was nothing after the sixth figure.

## What She Expected To Find

She had come back up the lane with a plan for it and the plan was the only plan she had ever made and it was four hundred words long.

She was going to stand in the yard at the ninth hour. She was going to say the three sentences to whoever came. She was going to put the count out loud, because she had given the count away in the seventh county and because saying it in her own mouth was the only thing she still had that was hers.

She got about thirty paces into the yard and a woman of about nineteen came past her with a yoke.

“You will want the far end,” she said. “The light is worse at this end after the fourth hour and the boy cannot see the slate.”

And that was the whole of the instruction and it was given to her by a stranger as a fact about the yard, and then she was gone through a door that had been a wall in about a foot of the day before.

Ning Yan stood in the middle of a yard that had a light in it and a bucket in it and a rule about the fourth hour in it, and none of the three of them was hers.

## Where It Came From

She spent two days finding out how it had happened, and the finding took her four conversations, and every one of them ended the same way.

She asked the woman of about nineteen who had told her about the light at the west end.

“The old woman with the slate,” said the woman. “About four years back. She was here first.”

She asked the old woman with the slate, who was nineteen years old and who had a piece of chalk she was still using.

“A woman on a road in the third county,” the old woman said. “She said it three and a half times and she got it wrong the way I got it wrong, and I stayed about nine days and taught it to anybody who stood still.”

She asked the woman on the road in the third county, by walking to her, which took five days.

“I was in her yard for a week,” the woman said. “She stood in it and she said a true thing out loud every morning at the ninth hour and I watched her do it for seven days and then I thought, somebody could do that here.”

And then Ning Yan asked the last question, standing, in the ordinary voice, in a yard above Lowwater on the fourth day, and it was the only question in four days that she had not already known the answer to.

“When you said *somebody could do that here*, who did you have in mind.”

The woman thought about it.

“Her,” she said. “You.”

And that was where it stopped.

She had stood in nine counties for two thousand seven hundred days and about a hundred and forty people had heard her say it and she had not made one decision about any of it, and she had put no word on it anywhere and told no one to do it, and she had taught two people a way of standing in front of somebody for about thirteen minutes, and one of them had stayed nine days somewhere and the other had watched for seven.

That was the whole of the cause.

## The Line Of Handwriting

The boy on the upturned bucket was copying the first leaf of a book of eight leaves, and she saw him doing it on the second afternoon and stood and looked at it for longer than she meant to.

The first leaf was Feng Lushi’s last fair handwriting. It had been the first thing in the book since a year she had been in a cell and could not get out of, and there had been two hundred words on it and she could have said the order of every one of them.

The boy was copying the third line. He was about ten and he had a good eye for a stroke and no idea at all what the words were, because not one person in the yard had ever told him and she had never told anybody, and there was not one person in that courtyard who knew whose hand was on the leaf.

She did not tell him.

She stood in the yard and let him go on copying, and she worked out standing, in the ordinary voice, that a hand could be the plainest thing in the world to learn from and that she had been carrying hers against her fourth rib for nineteen years and had never once seen it being used.

## What Was Built On The Number

The number had got there before she did.

They said four hundred and one in the yard. They said it the way you say a thing you have all agreed on without anybody ever producing it, and there were people there who had arrived four years ago and people who had arrived that morning and all of them had it.

“It is four hundred and one,” said a man of about thirty at the far wall, “because that is what the woman said at the ninth hour.”

“I said four hundred,” said Ning Yan, “and I said a woman of about thirty beside a man of about fifty said four hundred and one in a lane in the seventh county, and I told them I could not check it, and they have been saying her number ever since because mine was the one that sounded like an answer.”

The man said, “Is it the wrong one?”

“I do not know,” said Ning Yan, “and I am not going to stand in this yard and find out, because I have got a book with two leaves left in it and a rule that says a name goes on a leaf after it has been said standing in the ordinary voice in front of the person it belongs to, and there is not one version of that I can do for everybody who is standing in this yard this afternoon.”

She put the two figures out loud again, standing, in the ordinary voice, to about thirty people in a yard above Lowwater, and gave the whole of what she knew, which was a lane in the seventh county and a woman with a basket and no way on earth to find out.

## What She Said About It

And then she said the other thing, standing, in the ordinary voice, in front of about thirty people who had not asked her a question and were not waiting on one, and she said it because a thing said out loud standing goes into the air and cannot be taken back and because there was a woman here who had stood in a yard for seven days because she thought somebody could do that here.

“I did not build this,” she said. “I have been on the road two thousand seven hundred days and I have been in this yard four days and I found out about two days ago who started it, and the answer is a woman who watched me for seven days and a woman who watched that woman for nine days.

“Not one person decided this. I am not going to claim it, because I did not make it, and I am not going to stop it, because it is the only thing in nine counties that has happened without anybody having to be told to do it.

“And I am not going to call it anything. I have not got a name for it and I am not going to find one, and if one of you finds one then it will be yours and not mine.”

A man at the back said, “What do we call it then, when we are telling somebody in the fourth county where it is.”

“You will say a yard above Lowwater and a lane off the made road and a woman with one arm who used to stand at the ninth hour and who is not standing there now.”

That went about the yard the way everything she said goes, and by the next morning three of them had told people in the fourth county that there was a place and no name.

## What Was In The Yard On The Fifth Day

On the fifth day she was in the yard at the fourth hour because she had nowhere else to be, and the old woman with the slate came and stood in front of her and said, “You are in my way.”

“I am where I have always been.”

“You have not,” said the old woman. “You have always been where people can get to you. You have been in the middle of it since you arrived on the second morning and about nine people have gone round you and there is a boy who has not had his slate looked at in two days.”

And then she said the thing that turned the whole of the seven and a half years over, in the ordinary voice, in a yard above Lowwater, to a woman of about twenty-six who had come up a lane expecting a place to stand in.

“I would like you to look at his slate,” the old woman said, “and then I would like you to go and stand at the west end, because the light is worse at this end after the fourth hour and he cannot see it.”
