# Since He Was Nineteen

He had been not-silent all night, which was a thing she had only learned to hear in the past two months, and on the eighth of the month beginning two thousand nine hundred and fifty-four she woke to find that the not-silence had stopped.

She lay still with her hands flat on the stone and gave it a count of a hundred before she moved. A room with one person in it sounds different from a room with none. He was in there. His half was in there. It was not turned the way it had been turned for four days.

It was still.

The lamp stood on the stone beside her with the oil nearly out of it and the table in front of her had no lamp over it and no wear in it, and the register lay where it had lain with five mercies cut deep into it, and she put her hand flat on the register the way she put her hands flat on everything, which was to find out whether it was there.

## The Sentence That Came

It was there.

“Nineteen years,” he said. “I want it. I have wanted it since I was nineteen.”

She sat up.

There was no lamp over the table and the lamp beside her gave an eyelash and she read the room by the part of her hand that was in front of her, and none of that mattered, because the sentence came in the ordinary place his sentences came, low and level and plain, and it took her the way a step takes you when the stair is the same height it has always been.

Nine years of his voice in her head. Nine years of figures to the hour and costs to the day and a reach that told her what a body was doing while she was doing it. She had built nineteen years of herself on the fact that he was the most exact thing she had ever had.

He had just said the least exact sentence she had ever heard from him.

“Say it again,” she said.

“I want it,” he said. “I have wanted it since I was nineteen.”

## What He Asked For

The second one came out the same. Not flatter. Not kinder. The same, word for word, in the same voice, with the same weight on the word *it* and the same plainness on the whole of it.

She got herself up off the floor and onto the edge of the table because her back had gone the way it went after a night on stone, and she put her hands on her knees and breathed four counts sitting still, and then she said what was in her mouth.

“What do you want me to do with it.”

“Nothing,” he said.

The lamp moved a little where it stood. There was no draught in this room that she had ever found. The flame leaned and came back and stood up an eyelash above the wick, and she looked at it while she waited for what that cost him to say.

“Nothing,” she said.

“Nothing,” he said. “I am telling you what I want and the telling is the whole of it. I am not asking you for anything. I have not asked you for anything in nineteen years and I am not starting at the one thing I want most.”

She put her hands flat on the table edge on either side of the register and felt the stone under them, which was the same stone it had been every morning she had been in this room.

“Nineteen years,” she said, “and you have never asked me for one thing.”

“No,” he said.

“That is the first thing you have asked for.”

“That is the first thing I have asked for,” he said. “I have wanted a great many things and none of them were ever for me to say out loud, and I did not know that was true until you asked me what I wanted and I heard myself not answer for a whole day.”

She sat with that. The register under her hands had five mercies in it and no conditions in any of them and an offer standing open with no answering asked for it, and the man inside her head had wanted it since he was nineteen, which was a hundred years before her own life began, which was four hundred years before the keeping began, which was a length she could hold in her mouth and put nowhere.

“Since you were nineteen,” she said. “That is a figure.”

## The Check She Could Not Do

“No,” he said. “That is an age. An age is not a figure. A figure can be checked and an age cannot.”

She wanted to check it. That was the whole of what she did instead of sleeping, sitting on the edge of an unworn table in a room at the top of a tower with the lamp nearly out beside her, and she wanted to check a want the way she checked everything, which was by turning it over and over until it came out the same.

It did not come out the same. It came out the way a true thing comes out, which is that it does not improve on being looked at and it does not get worse either, and it is still there when you stop.

“Yuan,” she said.

“I am here.”

“You said you have wanted it since you were nineteen.”

“I did.”

“I am going to say a thing and you are going to let me say it.”

“I am not going to stop you,” he said. “I have never once stopped you. That is the whole of what I have and I have had it since I was ten years old and you were not in the world yet.”

## The Line With Nothing In It

She put her hand on the place behind her fourth rib without deciding to, the way she had been doing since the twentieth of the month beginning two thousand nine hundred and twenty-four, and it did not do anything at all.

“You have never once asked me for anything,” she said, sitting, in the ordinary voice, to the dark and the table and the man. “Not in nine years. Every want you have had, you have had in here where I could hear it, and you have let me hear it and you have never once put it in a sentence with my name on the other end of it.”

“That is true,” he said.

“I thought it was because you could not. I thought nineteen years of a voice in my head and not one asking was a limit and not a choice.”
“It was not a limit,” he said. “It was me. I did not want to be the thing in your life that wanted something and then got it. I have watched the whole shape of it for nineteen years and I know exactly what it does to a person, and I decided a long time ago that I would not be one, and I did not tell you that I had decided it, and that is the one thing I have kept from you that I kept on purpose.”

The dark past the last landing sat around her and did not change.

“That is a decision you made about me,” she said.

“Yes,” he said. “And you are asking me now what I want and I am telling you, so you know what the size of it is.”

She sat with the size of it. Six hundred years of a man inside the machinery that made her, and the thing he wanted was the thing on the table, and he had never once put it to her, and she had spent nineteen years guessing at him from the sound of his turning.

“What would you have done,” she said, “if I had asked you at the sixth year instead of this one.”

“I would have said yes,” he said, “and you would have been sixteen, and I would have spent the rest of your life being the reason.”

Her breath went nine counts sitting still, and then four, and then four again.

“That is the answer to that question,” she said. “That is the whole of it and you gave it to me in the first hour and it is not one word.”

## Nineteen Years of Not Asking

“It is one line,” he said. “I have been shortening them for four days and I cannot get it shorter.”

She got off the edge of the table and onto the stone floor with her back to the table leg, which was the way she sat when she meant to be there a while, and she put the lamp where she could reach it without looking and the register stayed where it lay.

“You have wanted it since you were nineteen,” she said, “and you have carried that for six hundred years, and you have never once said it to me, and in nineteen years of my guessing at you I got it wrong.”

“You guessed I wanted out,” he said. “And you guessed I wanted to be a person and not an entry, and both of those are half true and neither of them is what I want.”

“What do you want.”

“The thing on the table,” he said. “All five mercies and the parts of it you have not read yet. Walls up. Layer up. The mercy of a bounded world, which is real, and you have had the argument with it and it was had and it was right.”

“And you.”

“And me,” he said. “Addressed. Remembered. Given work. All of it. None of it is a cost to you and all of it is six hundred years of a man who died apologising to the men who were cutting him open.”

She sat in the dark and let that be on the table between them, because she was not going to be the one who took it up and did not have to be.

“You said one line,” she said. “No figure on it.”

“No figure on it,” he said.

“Nineteen years and it is the only sentence either of us has said that has nothing in it to check.”

“That is why it is true,” he said. “You can check every other thing I have ever said to you. That one you cannot check, and it is the only one I have ever been certain of.”
The lamp stood nearly out on the stone beside her. The register lay on the table with five lines cut deep into it and nothing worn in the table at all.

Eight flights and a stair below her a room was running on firsts and a boy was somewhere under her with oil on his hands and no idea that there was a record, and inside her head a man who had wanted one thing since before she was born had said it out loud for the first time in his life, in a single line, and had put no number in it at all.
