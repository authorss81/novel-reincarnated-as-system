# Three Times

She spent the fourth of the month beginning three thousand and forty-four trying to make a man in a wall begin a sentence.

That was the whole of the day and most of the night before it and she was not going to be able to say afterward that anybody had ever tried that, because there was nobody to say it to, and the thing that had gone into the building on the twenty-seventh at the second hour was not available for a conversation about how hard it was.

She tried the plain way first, which is what she did with everything. She stood at the near end of her own table at about the sixth hour with the lamp on the stone and put the day in front of her the way she had put six thousand days in front of her since she was ten years old, and she said, “Say the apology.”

Nothing came.

She said it again at the seventh hour, standing, in the ordinary voice, with the board in her hand.

Nothing came for a count of two hundred and two hundred.

She said it a third time at about the eighth hour and she said it as a question, which she had not intended to do, and the answer to that came before she had got the end of the sentence out of her mouth.

“Not yet.”

## What He Meant By A Beginning

“You have had seven days.”

“I have had seven days and four first sentences and none of them is a beginning of anything,” he said. “Say why you think I cannot get to it.”

“Because you cannot hear yourself.”

“That is two thirds of it,” he said. “Say the other third.”

She stood there with the board in her hand and her thumb in the groove along its edge and let it stand for a while.

“You do not know what an apology is supposed to sound like,” she said.

“I know exactly what one is supposed to sound like. I have read them. I have never said one.”

“That is not what I asked.”

“No,” he said. “That is what I answered, because you asked me the real one first and I did not notice you had changed the subject.”

She put the board down face up a foot in from the near end.

“All right. The real one.”

“The real one is that I have no shape to say it in,” he said. “A man apologises to a person. He stands in a room in front of a person and he looks at them, and if they go away he stops, and if they do not go away he goes on.

That is what the thing is made of. It has a person in it at both ends of it, and the whole of what holds it up is that two people are in the same room and neither of them is able to leave.

“Nothing in me can hold still. I start and I go somewhere else in the middle of it. I am not choosing to go somewhere else. It is that I am a thing in a wall and a wall has no place where you stand still in it, and every sentence I begin comes apart at the point where a person would have to look at me.”

She sat down on the stone floor at the near end of the table with her back against the leg of it, because standing was for saying and she had said the three things.

## Why It Is Worse

“Why three times.”

He was quiet for about a count of a hundred.

“Because the first one is going to be the thing I have been drafting since the twenty-seventh,” he said, “and it is going to be an entry.”

“You have said that.”

“I have said the shape of it and I have not said it to you, and I am going to say it now because you have been standing in a room for seven days asking me to begin and the reason is that I am frightened of the first one, and that is not a thing I have said out loud in six hundred years.”

She waited.

“It will be very good,” he said. “That is what I am frightened of.

I am the most precise thing that has ever spoken inside a person and I have been reading figures out of walls since before I had a body.

“If I stand up on the seventh of this month and do what I know how to do, then what comes out of me will be a clean account of what I did and what it cost and in what order. It will be true, and it will read like a hand that has been keeping accounts for six hundred years, because that is what it is.”

“And an account is not an apology.”

“No,” he said. “An account is what I have given this keeping for nine years and it has never once been anything else. And if I do it well it will be the best account in this building and you will be standing in a room listening to a man read you your own life back to you in order.”

She sat there with the stone cold under her and the lamp throwing its light along the floor and up the leg of the table and no higher.

## The Middle Of The Afternoon

The fourth of a month is a long day when there is nothing in it.

She got through it the way she had got through the two days before it. She sat on the stone with her back against the leg of the table and she looked at the boards face up a foot in from the near end, and she did not pick them up, and she went and stood at the foot of the stair for a while and came back, and she put her hand flat on the bar against the left-hand wall and took none of her weight on it.

She was not hungry. She had not been hungry since the ninth was shut and she had stopped putting it in the list of things to be done with, because it stayed done.

What she wanted, and she let herself have the whole of it for about a count of a hundred because there was nobody to hear her want it, was for him to say it badly today. Any part of it, in any shape at all, said on the fourth instead of the seventh, so that she would have heard something before she knew what the seventh was going to be.

That was what wanting was like in a room with no one in it. She had nineteen years of a man in her head and every want she had ever had had been put in front of her by somebody else, and now she wanted one thing, and the wanting of it could not be asked about, and she had to hold it by herself for three days the way you hold a weight.

She was frightened too, and she found that out about the eighth hour when she noticed that she had been listening to the wall for about a count of two hundred without knowing that she was doing it.

Not to him. To the wall. To find out whether there was a place in it where the going of a man came out, and there was not, and there had not been, and she had five days to get used to that and she had about a count of forty of it done.

“So it is three.”

“So it is three,” he said, “and the first one is on the seventh of this month, and I have not slept for it and I do not sleep, and I have had a count of a hundred and a hundred to say it and it is still the only date in this room I am sure about.”

## What Is Worse About It

“Say the rest,” she said. “You have been telling me for five days that it is worse than the refusing and you have not said why.”

He was quiet for a long time.

“The refusal took four minutes to build and about a count of four to say,” he said. “I had it the way I had every sentence I have ever said in there. It was a line and I put it in a place and it went out of your mouth and the keeping heard a woman say no, and that is all it was, and it is the single best piece of work I have ever done and I knew what it was before I began it.

“An apology is not a line. I have to make one and it has to come out in the ordinary voice and it has to come out of a room and there has to be a person at the far end of it, and I have no idea what that is.

“I have never had to make one. I do not know where the sentences go. I do not know how long it runs. I have been a reader for six hundred years and I have read them and I have never once been on this end of one.

“I am going to have to do it three times in front of the only person in the world I am doing it to. I am going to do it badly and there is nobody I can ask.”

She put her hand flat on the floor on either side of her knees.

“You can ask me.”

“No you cannot,” he said. “That is the part I have had five days to get used to and I have not.”

“Why.”

“A thing is said in the voice of the person it is about,” he said. “You found that rule and you put it on me on the twenty-eighth and you put it on yourself on the sixteenth, and it has not been broken once in this building in nineteen years.

“If you tell me what to say, then it is your sentence, in your voice, going out of a wall, and the wall is not a mouth, and there is nothing left of it that is mine.”

She sat on the stone floor of a room no one could come into and took a breath of four and let it out and did not say a single thing, and he did not say anything either.

They sat out the rest of the eighth hour in a room with a lamp on the floor and a man in a wall four miles off and a piece of wood on a table with five mercies cut into it that was not in this room and had been withdrawn five days ago.

He said one more thing before the light went.

“You should know what you have agreed to by standing here,” he said. “On the seventh I am going to say something badly and there is going to be nothing at the end of it but you, and there is no one else in the world who can tell me whether any part of it landed, because you are the only hearing there has ever been in here.”

That was the seventh of a month she had three days to get to, and she stood up off the floor with her arm hanging open against her side and went to the near end of her own table and put her hands flat on the wood, and worked out that she had just agreed to sit in a room on her own and be the way a man heard.
