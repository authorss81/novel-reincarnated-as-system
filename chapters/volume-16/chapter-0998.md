# Her Brother And The Work

She knew him in about a count of forty and she did not go any faster for it.

He was up the lane with a barrow and a length of timber on his shoulder and the light was off the top of the far wall. He was older. That was the first thing and it was the only thing she was ever going to be able to put in a column, and there is no column in it.

He had not been in the yard in about fifteen years and the lane had not been a lane when he last came up it.

He put the timber down against the far wall, took the barrow round the back, came back, and said, “The man at the gate said you wanted a wall done.”

“I wanted a wall done,” said Ning Yan.

“How much of it.”

“The whole of the east side. It has been leaning about two months and there is about a foot of it that has nothing behind it.”

He looked at it for about a count of two hundred and said, “That is four days with four of us and you are not one of them,” and then he went and found four of them and did not explain to any of them who he was.

## What He Had Been Doing

She asked him about it at about the sixth hour, standing, in the ordinary voice, at the west end of the yard, with the two of them and about four people in earshot who had no business hearing and could not be moved.

He kept the gate for about four years.

Then the gate was a gate and there were about sixty stalls on the near bank and carts on the far one, and a man on a stool two paces inside it was not doing anything at all, and there was not one person on either bank who wanted him there, and not one person came and said anything about it.

“I stopped,” Ning Wu said. “I did not go and be told. There was not one person to tell me and there was not one person to complain, and I had been sitting on a stool for four years doing a thing that had stopped existing, and I stopped doing it.”

“Was that the gate you were at when I came up in the fall.”

“It is.”

“And Du Wen.”

“Du Wen is at the gate,” said Ning Wu. “He was at the gate before me and he is at the gate now and he has been at it the whole time and we have never once discussed it, and I would rather keep it that way than not.”

She stood at the west end of a yard and worked out, standing, in the ordinary voice, that her brother had carried the gate for four years out of about fifteen and had put it down because the thing it was keeping had stopped being a thing, and that he had not told anybody and had not asked anybody for anything, and that she had heard about it eleven years after it happened.

“I did not know,” she said.

“I did not tell you,” said Ning Wu. “You could not be met where I am and I could not follow a name I was given and neither of us can be sent anywhere. There is not one instrument in this and there has not been one since the ceiling came off. You did not know because there is no way to know, and I have had about eleven years of not being able to tell you and I did not find it as hard as I thought I would.”

## The Thing That Is Not Between Them

A boy of about seventeen in the yard called him uncle.

It was a mistake. Ning Wu had never been anybody’s uncle in his life and he had a brother in the yard and no children, and at thirteen he had been taken off a furnace crew by a signature no hand at the furnace had ever put to.

The boy said, “Sorry. I did not know.”

“That is all right,” said Ning Wu, in an ordinary voice, and nothing else, and went back to the wall.

Ning Yan was about nine paces off and she heard all of it and she did not say one word.

What she heard was not unkindness. It was the opposite of unkindness, and that was worse, and she had had about fifteen years of being able to hear exactly how her brother felt about things and she could not hear this one at all.

She put it to herself standing, in the ordinary voice, at the west end, at about the seventh hour, and worked out that she had never once got one word out of him that settled anything, and that she had spent fifteen years not asking, and that not asking was not the same thing as him having let it go.

He had not said that he had let it go either. That was the thing she could stand on, and it was a thin thing, and it was hers, and she was not going to say it out loud standing in front of about four people at the ninth hour as though it were a figure.

## The Sentence She Carried And Did Not Say

She had it ready at about the seventh hour and she had had it ready for about fifteen years, and she worked out standing, in the ordinary voice, at the west end of the yard, that it was not a question about the gate and not a question about the wall and not a question about the boy who had called him uncle.

It was the winter she was seventeen, when she was the only one who knew what she was, and a man with a wet thumb had written it down because it was his day to write it down, and her brother had been moved up out of a furnace crew above the authority of the furnace, and had told her so on the tenth day of a month in a lane in the open with no wall on either side of it and had asked her for nothing.

She had the sentence. She had had it ready since she was about twenty-two years old and it had got no worse in twelve years of carrying it.

She did not say it.

She worked out why, standing, and the reason was not noble and she was not going to pretend it was. The reason was that she was going to say it because she wanted to hear what he would answer, and not because he had asked her or because she owed him, and she had spent about four hundred days in a yard learning what it looked like when a person stood in front of somebody for the sake of getting a thing done for themselves.

That was new and it had taken her twelve years and a wall.

She put her hand flat on the stone coping of the yard for about a count of two hundred and then took it off again and went and carried a bucket.

## What They Did With A Wall

They worked the wall.

That is the whole of what happened on the seventeenth of a month beginning eight thousand six hundred and eighty-four and it took about four hours and about nine people and it is the only thing either of them said he came for.

He dug. He was better at it than she was and he was better at it than anybody in the yard and he did it without talking about it, and twice he stopped and moved a stone that was in the wrong place and both times he moved it with his own hands rather than tell a man about it.

Ning Yan got the footings open with the left hand and the lamp stood on a bucket about nine paces off because there was still no reason in the world to set a lamp down, and she could not get her right arm above about a foot, and the arm had been open at the elbow for six thousand four hundred and six days and was not going to close.

At about the eighth hour a woman of about thirty came and stood about four paces off her brother and said, “You are the one she has not got on with.”

“I am,” said Ning Wu.

“You will want the far end of this.”

“I will want the far end of this.”

The woman went away. Ning Yan looked at him and he did not look at her.

## What She Said At The Ninth Hour

She did the figures out loud at the ninth hour, standing, in the ordinary voice, in the yard, in front of about nine people, because the rule was the rule and she had said on the twenty-seventh of a month that she was going to do it every morning.

“Five thousand seven hundred and forty days since the seventh of the month beginning two thousand nine hundred and fifty-four. That is fifteen years and three hundred and forty days and I am thirty-four.

“Five thousand five hundred and thirty-seven days since the thirtieth of a month beginning three thousand and one hundred and thirty-four.

“Five thousand six hundred and sixteen days out of a room. Six thousand three hundred and ninety-nine days in that room before it. Six thousand four hundred and six days with this arm open at the elbow, and not going to close.”

Then she said the part about the wall and the part about who had done it, out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice, to about nine people.

“The east wall of this yard was opened by a man of about thirty-four who came up a lane at the fourth hour with a timber on his shoulder and was let get on with it, and he dug the footings himself and moved two stones with his own hands and did not tell anybody about either of them, and he has not eaten here and did not offer to and I am not able to give him anything and he did not ask.”

The man himself said, “I had a piece of bread,” from about thirty paces off, and about nine people laughed, and he did not laugh, and she did not either.

He went at about the ninth hour with the barrow and said, at the lane, “I will be back on the third day and I know which end.”

“I know which end,” Ning Yan said.

That was the whole of it. He had come up a lane and worked a wall for four hours and said three true things about his own life that were not about her and had not settled one thing between them, and he had said which end, and she had said the same four words back to him, and he was gone down the lane before she had put the lamp down or picked anything up.
