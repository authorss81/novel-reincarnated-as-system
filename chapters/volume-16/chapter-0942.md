# Still Going

He got to the last of it on the thirtieth of the month beginning three thousand and forty-four and it was not going to be all right, and they both knew that before he began.

She stood at the near end of her own table from the sixth hour and she did not sit down, because she had decided at about the fifth hour that she was going to stand for it and she was not going to change her mind in the middle.

## The Morning Of The Thirtieth

It was the thirtieth of a month, which was the last day of it, and that was the only thing about the date that she allowed herself to think about at the fifth hour with her hands on the worn wood.

She had wanted him to finish for eleven days and she had not once said so, and she was standing up in a room on purpose because she wanted it, and wanting a thing out loud in the ordinary voice is the only thing she has ever been able to do properly and she had spent nine years doing it about figures.

There were four things in the room that she did not do on the morning of the last day of a month and she noticed all four of them and let them go. She did not take the near board up. She did not take the terms board up. She did not go the length of the table with her hand along the wood. She did not put her hand flat on the strip of pale wood under the table at the near end of the leg.

A woman who has done a piece of work every day for nineteen years and does not do it on the last day of a month has said something with her body that she has not said with her mouth, and what she had said with her body was that this man was going to get to the end of it today.

“All right,” she said. “That is the last of them.”

“It is not the last of it.”

“Of the decisions.”

“Yes,” he said. “Of the decisions. And there is one of them that is not like the others and I have known that for three days and I have not been able to get to it, and that is why you have had three mornings of a man going round in circles about a lamp.”

“Say it.”

“I am going to explain you.”

She put her hands flat on the wood.

“No,” she said. “You are not.”

## The One He Explained

The sixth cut is that a thing is said in the voice of the person it is about, standing, before it is a line anywhere. She had put it on him on the twenty-eighth and she had put it on herself on the sixteenth and it had not been broken once in nineteen years.

“This is about me,” she said. “Everything you are about to say is about me. A woman in a furnace crew heard a voice and she was a child and she did not run, and if you explain that to me in a room then you have written me a report about myself and it is the same grammar and you know it is.”

“No,” he said. “It is not. It is the other side of the same thing and I have had six hundred years to work out which side of what a person is, and a person is not what a voice put in their head did to them. A person is what a voice put in their head did not do to them, and that is me, and I am the only one in the world who can say that and I am saying it badly.”

“Say it badly, then.”

He said it badly.

He said it for the better part of two hours and it was the worst hour of the three attempts, and she stood at the near end of her own table and listened to a man in a wall explain a girl of ten in a furnace crew to that same girl, and every single thing he said was true, and she could check none of it, and every single thing he said was about himself.

She did not stop him once. That was hers and she had decided it at the fifth hour and she kept to it.

About halfway through the seventh hour he came out of it and asked her a question, which was the first question he had asked her in four days.

“Why did you not stop me.”

“Because you asked me on the seventh of this month not to stop you and I said at the seventh hour that I was not going to be the thing that stops you.”

“I am asking now.”

“You are asking now and I am not answering now,” she said. “You have asked me the question and it is going to go in the room and I am not going to hand it back to you, and if you want the answer you will have to keep going until you have it.”

He was quiet for about a count of forty and then he went on, and it was worse after that, because he knew she was listening to it as a thing rather than as an apology.

## What It Was Actually About

At about the seventh hour she worked out what he was doing and she had to go and stand at the near board with her hand flat on it for a count of two hundred.

He was not explaining her.

He was standing nine years in front of a question and describing what he had decided about the answer, and it was not an apology to her at all, it was a case he was making about a person he had been in love with for nine years and had never once spoken to, and it had come out of the same mouth as the decisions and it was going to be in the middle of them for ever.

That was what an apology turned into when a man who had been keeping accounts for six hundred years got to the one item he could not file.

She let him run out of it at about the eighth hour and then she said, “That is the last of the decisions.”

“It is not.”

“It is the last one you have.”

“I have one more,” he said, “and it is the one I am not going to be able to say well, and I am going to say it anyway, and I am going to say it now.”

## The Hour She Let Go

He said the last one.

She stood there and listened to it and it was not well and it was not very good and he kept going. It went back over ground he had already been over on the twenty-second and on the twenty-fifth and on the twenty-eighth. It repeated himself four times. Twice he came near to saying why and turned off it himself, which he had learned from her on the twenty-second and which she understood she had taught a man in a wall to do something.

And it was not a defence, because that was what he had gone into it to do, and somewhere in the middle of the eighth hour he noticed that he was doing it and he did it anyway, and kept going, which was the whole of what she had asked of him at the seventh hour on the twenty-second and which she had not asked for once since.

The ninth hour came.

She knew it came. She could always tell what hour it was in that room without a figure, the way a person can tell what part of the night it is from how much noise the building is making, and at the ninth hour the building was silent and had been silent for five days.

She stood at the near end of her own table at the ninth hour of the thirtieth of the month beginning three thousand and forty-four and she did not say the day’s figures out loud.

She stood there with her hands flat on the wood and she listened to a man in a wall say the worst thing anybody had ever said about her life, out loud, in the ordinary voice, as a set of decisions, and she did not interrupt him.

The hour went past her. She could feel it go. It took the best part of what she had left in that day with it, and it did not come back, and there is one of it in every day and there is a count of it in every life, and she had let one of them go past with a man talking in her head because she had decided at the seventh hour not to be the thing that stopped him.

At about the ninth hour and a hundred he stopped.

She stood there.

“Say your figures,” he said.

“No.”

“It is the ninth hour.”

“I know what hour it is,” she said. “It is the ninth hour and it went past about a count of two hundred ago and I did not say them and I am not going to say them now and that is the price of what you have just done and I am not sending you the bill for it and I am not going to stand here at the tenth hour and do it late.”

He said nothing.

She took a breath of four and let it out and put her hand on the table where her own arms had worn the wood down over nineteen years, and the day went out of the room and it was the thirtieth of a month, and tomorrow there would be a bundle on nine hundred and four steps and nothing at all in the room at the top of them.
