# Holding On To Nothing

On the second morning off the mountain she put her right hand out to take hold of a root and it did not take hold of it, and that was the first thing that had ever happened to her that nothing had told her about in advance.

She had known about the arm. She had carried it open at the elbow for seven hundred and ninety-two days and had said it out loud every morning at the ninth hour in her own voice before it was a line anywhere.

Knowing about a thing and finding out what it is on a wet hillside at the fourth hour of a morning are two entirely different pieces of information and she had only ever had the first of them.

## The Hand

The root was about the thickness of two fingers and it came up out of the scree and it went down again into ground that was not holding anything at all.

She put her right hand on it and pulled and her shoulder went forward and her elbow came to a stop about a hand’s width from where it had been, the way it came to a stop every time now, and the root came up out of the ground in her hand with a handful of wet earth on it and then slid out of her fingers and went back down.

Her right arm hung open against her side after that, the way it hangs, the way it had hung for seven hundred and ninety-two days without anything to do about.

She got down past it by putting her heels into the slope and her left hand wherever the left hand could find rock, and it took her about a count of three hundred, and she got down past it, and she was not going to be able to do that three hundred more times before dark.

Three fingers and a thumb on the left hand since she was sixteen years old.

Nine years she had had a keeping reading her figures back at her every morning of them, and one hand had done all of it, and the other one had done nothing at all since she was sixteen, and she had spent a great many years not thinking about that.

She thought about it for the whole of a morning on a hillside.

## Putting It Down

A lamp needs a hand.

She worked that out the hard way, on a slab of rock where the way down was a crack she could get her left arm into and her right arm would not go in at all, because the right arm went down about a hand’s width and then stopped being the shape of an arm.

So she put the lamp down on the rock above her, on a flat place, and she went down the crack with both hands, and at the bottom she stood in the wet for about a count of a hundred and looked back up at it.

It was a small lamp on a big slab and it had been burning since the second hour of the twenty-seventh of the month before last and there was nothing in the world to put oil in it with.

She went back up the crack for it.

That was the shape of the whole of the descent and it took her two more days to understand it. Anywhere the ground wanted two hands she had one. Anywhere the ground wanted a hand and a lamp she had neither, and she put the lamp down on the nearest flat rock above the worst of it, and went down, and came back up for it.

She put it down more times in three days than she picked it up, and she never once did that on purpose.

By the middle of the third day it was burning low enough that she could see the level in it through the glass, and by that night it was a piece of metal with a wick in it, and she carried it anyway for the rest of her life and was never once able to put anything in it.

## The Second Night

She made it as far as an overhang of rock on the second night and she got her back against the stone and put her right arm across her knees so it was out of the wind, and she sat there and worked out that she had not been cold in seven hundred and eighty-five days and had not now known that cold was a thing with a length to it.

It was not cold that was the problem. It was that she had been warm for a very long time and the whole apparatus of her had been built for a room with one wall in it.

She had not been able to keep a fire.

There was wood on that mountain that was dead and dry and had been dead and dry for a hundred years, and she had three fingers and a thumb on one hand and an arm on the other that would not go where she told it, and she got about four stones’ worth of heat out of a pile of it before the light went completely and then she gave up.

A fire that will not last the night is not a fire, and she had spent seven hundred and eighty-five days keeping a lamp in a room, and she knew exactly what a thing is that is not going to last the night.

She lay against the rock with her face to the overhang and she was colder at about the fifth hour than she had ever been in her life, and she was not hungry, and she had not one person to be cold in front of.

## The Rule She Broke

On the morning before the root she said out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice, that she would be under cover by the last light on the second day.

She had said it because it was a thing with a date on it, and she had not had a thing with a date on it since the first of a month went up nine hundred and four steps without her, and a rule with a day on it was something she could stand behind and hold.

On the second day there was no cover. She had said the word and meant something with a roof on it and there was scrub, and there was rock, and there was a place between two boulders about the length of her body that kept the wind off one side of her and one eye.

On the third day at about the seventh hour she went down a pitch of loose stone that she had gone down twice before that day without touching it, because it had been quiet about itself, and the third time it went all the way.

She went about forty of a count down it and then her right arm came round on the rock and took her weight sideways.

## The Four Counts

She lay on her back on a slope of stone with her knees up and her left hand over her mouth and waited for her breath to come back to four counts.

It had gone out of her completely and had come back wrong, three and a half and then five, and the wrong of it was the part that frightened her, because it was not a wound and she had had a great many of those and she knew what they did.

She lay there about a count of a hundred and thirty before she got four.

Then she lay there another count of two hundred before she got nine, which is what a body does standing still, and there was nothing on this mountain to stand still on.

The lamp was below her somewhere in the loose stone, and it was burning so low that there was nothing coming off it, because it had been burning down since the twelfth and it had nothing left in it that could make light, and she could see exactly nothing at all.

She had said on the first morning that she would be under cover by the last light on the second day.

It was the third day. She was on the side of a mountain with a dead lamp under a pile of stone, and there was not one room within reach of her, and she had not once in seven hundred and eighty-six days slept anywhere but in one.

## The Ninth Hour Of The Fourteenth

She said the six of them standing on a slope at about the ninth hour on the fourteenth, in the ordinary voice, with the wet coming down off the rock above her onto her shoulders, and she said them all and she got them all out in the order she had been saying them in for nineteen years.

Seven hundred and eighty-six days in that room, and she was saying it on a hillside, and there was no room.

She put them into the keeping standing, first in her own voice, before it was a line anywhere, because that was the rule and because there was not one ear to hear it and because she had never once in nineteen years let a day go by without the ninth hour being hers.

Nothing came back.

She had expected nothing. She had expected nothing since the twenty-fifth of a month and she had got nothing on the twenty-sixth and on the twenty-seventh and on every day since, and there was nothing different about saying six figures on a wet slope except that there was wind on them.

## What Was Under Her

She got herself up in three goes and she got her left hand on the stone and she went up about forty of a count to where she had put the lamp and it was where she had put it, on the flat, in the rain, dead.

She stood with it in her left hand for a while and looked at the slope.

There was a line going across it.

That was the whole of what she saw in the failing light and it took her until she had it properly before she understood it. It was not water and it was not a break in the rock. It was about the width of her two hands and it went across the hillside at a slight angle and the ground inside it was a different colour from the ground on either side of it, and there were places where a stone had come out of it.

A path.

Not a road. Not a made thing. A line that four hundred years of a keeping and nine hundred years of a world had never once been aware of, worn into the side of a mountain by feet that came this way and came back, generation on generation, and it was there under her hand in the dark.

She put her fingers in it.

Somebody had been walking here while she was standing at a table counting days, and then walking back, and then walking here again, and not one of them had ever known that there was a building above them in the mountain with a room in it that had a table in it with wear in it.

There was smoke somewhere under the path, and it was the first time in nineteen years that she had smelled anything at all that was not a lamp burning in a stone room.

She went down towards it.
