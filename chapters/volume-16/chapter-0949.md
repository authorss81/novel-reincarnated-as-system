# The First One Who Agreed

The stall at the place where the road came off the pass was boards on two upturned crates with a cloth over them, and it had four jars on it and a tray of wick, and it belonged to a woman who was about forty and who had watched Ning Yan come down the last of the path for the better part of a count of two hundred before she said anything at all.

There was wind coming round the corner of the stone wall and going through the cloth and the woman had put her arms inside her sleeves.

“You are the one who came off the mountain,” she said.

“No,” Ning Yan said. “I am not from the mountain.”

“You are wet through and you have been on the path above it for two hours and there is nothing up there.”

“There is a building.”

“There is a building,” the woman agreed, “and there has been one since before my mother was born, and none of us has ever seen anybody come out of it.”

She said that without any weight on it at all. It was the way people said a thing about weather.

## What She Had Come To Say

She had walked down a path to do a thing and she had got to the bottom of it and had not one word.

That was the first thing she put in front of herself, standing in the wind with a dead lamp in her hand. She had been nineteen years old and there had been a keeping telling her what to do every morning of the last nine years, and she had never once in her life had to produce a sentence out of herself for another person, because everything she had ever said had been said into a room that did not answer.

There was not one person in this to say it to.

She put the dead lamp down on the boards of the stall, which she had not meant to do, and then she stood there and started.

She started badly.

She said that there was a keeping in the mountain nine hundred years old that kept the walls of the world up on purpose, and that it ran on a thing called the Foundation tax, and that the tax was how it found one person at a time, and that it put a voice in that person’s head and made them less solid for exactly as long as it took to be useful, and then filed them.

She said that eleven women had gone through it before her, and that there was not one of their names anywhere, and that somebody was going to have to write them down.

The woman behind the stall had not moved once.

“You have been inside it,” she said.

“Yes.”

“For how long.”

“Seven hundred and ninety-two days in the room. Nine years since it began.”

The woman put her thumb on the edge of the boards and did not say anything for about a count of a hundred and twenty.

“Is that what you have come down here to tell me.”

“No,” Ning Yan said. “That is not what I came to tell you. That is what it is. I have not got the other thing yet and I have started at the wrong end and I am going to stop and do it again.”

## The Question That Cut It

“You are going to do it again.”

“I am.”

The woman looked at her for a while with her arms still inside her sleeves.

“Now I will tell you what I have been wanting to say since you started,” she said, “and then you can decide whether to say your thing or not.

“You have been talking about a machine. I have heard about four words of that in my life and all of them were from men who wanted something off me. I do not know what a Foundation tax is and I do not think you do either, because you have been saying it the way people say a thing they have heard of.

“If you have something to ask me then ask me. Do not tell me about the machine first.”

“All right.”

“And if you have not got anything to ask me then go and sleep, because there is a place with a roof at the end of the row and the woman in it will let you have the floor, and I will not ask you for anything for it.”

## Asking

She asked.

She stood up straight, because standing was for saying, and she put nothing in either hand because she had put the lamp on the boards without meaning to and could not think about it.

“My name is Ning Yan. I am nineteen years old. I am putting people into an arithmetic of mine and the keeping that runs this world is writing down what each of them costs, and the price is on the record, and I do not know what it costs me.

“No person has ever come out of this by wanting it less and I am not able to give anybody anything or be given anything, and if you agree to it you are not sent anywhere and nothing is done to you and no one else binds you.

“That is the whole of it and I have said it standing, in the ordinary voice, before it is a line anywhere, and I have said it to you before you have said yes to it, and you can say no and I will not ask you again.”

The woman did not say anything.

Then she said, “What did you want.”

“What do I want.”

“Yes. Out of all that. What do you want out of me.”

That was the question Ning Yan had not prepared for, and she stood in the wind at the foot of a mountain with a dead lamp on a stall board and found that she had no idea.

“I want somebody to help me carry a sentence,” she said. “And I do not know what it is going to cost either of us, and that is the honest of it.”

## What The Woman Wanted

The woman was quiet for a long time.

“Nine years,” she said. “I sell oil and wick at the place where the road comes off the pass, and for nine years there has been a thing in my head that tells me what a jar of it is worth, and it has never once been wrong, and I have got two children and a man who is not good at the work, and every week for nine years I have apologised to those children for the price of what I put on their table.

“Not out loud. You do not say a thing like that out loud. You just stop being able to look at them when you put the bowl down.”

She took her arms out of her sleeves.

“You said that only you bind people. I want to know if it comes off.”

“No,” Ning Yan said. “It does not come off.”

“Then I would rather have it than not have it,” the woman said, “and I want you to say the line. And I want you to know that I have not got the least idea what I have just agreed to, and that I am going to find out in about a week and come and shout at you.”

Kan Shou, she said. Her name was Kan Shou.

## Said First

Ning Yan stood in the wind with the wind going through everything she had on and said it out loud, in the ordinary voice, before it was a line anywhere.

“Kan Shou. I bind you into the Sisterhood, and you are the second of it, and the price of it is on the record. No name taken into the keeping is sent anywhere, and nothing is done to you, and I am the only one who binds you. I have told you what is on the record before you answered and you answered.”

Kan Shou stood with her hands flat on the boards of the stall and said nothing at all, which was what a person did when a line went past into a keeping a long way off in a mountain.

Then she said, “That is it?”

“That is it.”

“I did not feel anything.”

“You will not,” Ning Yan said. “It is not for feeling. It is for weighing.”

## Afterwards

She picked the dead lamp up off the boards.

A woman came down the road at about the seventh hour and put a jar on the boards and asked for oil in it, and Kan Shou took the jar and filled it and gave the woman back two small pieces of metal, and neither of them said one word to Ning Yan while it was happening, and the woman had not stopped in front of her on the way past.

That was the whole of what being in a place with people was like and she had not known it.

The count was two. One of them was a boy of sixteen in a building with oil he made and could not buy, and one of them was a woman of about forty at a stall at the foot of a pass.

She stood there afterwards in the wind and worked out, standing, what she had actually done, and she got to it in about a count of two hundred.

She had told a person about a machine for longer than the person had been listening and then asked the person a question.

She had not had a sentence. She had made the sentence up out of her own mouth in the order it came, standing, in the wind, with a dead lamp, and it had been the wrong sentence and it had been the right length, and it had got a woman to say yes to a thing she did not understand.

She could not do it again tomorrow.

That was the part she stood there with. She had been nineteen years old and she had nine years of a keeping that had put a figure in front of her every morning and she did not have one sentence of her own that she could say twice, and she was going to have to make one, out loud, out of order, in a county where she had not one acquaintance, and she had no board and she had four blank leaves against her fourth rib.
