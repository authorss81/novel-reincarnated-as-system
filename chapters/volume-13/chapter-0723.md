# Nine Hundred Steps

She went up the second stair at the fourth hour of the sixteenth of the month beginning 2,294, on a stair that is narrow and turns, and she went up it for four days, and on the fourth day she found out how far it goes as far as she is able to hold.

## The Stair That Comes Back Down

The first seven steps are visible from the floor of the room and after that the angle of the wall takes them, and she found out on the sixteenth that the stair does not go up out of the room and stay up.

It goes up for a while and then it turns along and then it comes back down on the far side, and the two halves are the same width of stair and the same kind of stone, and the whole of it comes back down to the floor of that room at the far end, past the end of the table, out of a place in the wall she had not looked at because it is behind the lamp’s shelf.

That is why seven steps are visible from the door and the rest of it is not. It is not that something takes the stair at the seventh step. It is that a person going up it turns out of sight after seven and does not come back into sight for nine hundred steps.

She said it out loud on the sixteenth at the foot of it, standing, in the ordinary voice:

“A stair that goes up and comes back down to the floor of the room is a stair with two ends in the same room, and I have been looking at the top end of it for a day and a half as if it went somewhere.”

## Four Counts And Seven On A Rise

Going up it is a rise and her breath goes to seven on anything that rises and she is at nine standing still at the top of eight flights and she had it at nine on the third landing of it on the seventeenth and held it there for a count of forty.

She counted on the seventeenth and the eighteenth and the nineteenth, out loud, one number at a time, the way she counts everything, because the alternative is carrying it in her head and her head is not big enough for a stair and she found that out on the nineteenth.

One hundred and forty on the first day, because she gave up at the second turn where her left hand could reach the wall on both sides at once.

Three hundred and ninety on the second day, on the third of the turns.

Six hundred and forty on the third day, and her breath was at nine from the fifth hundred and she spent a count of forty standing on a step doing nothing at all, and she is good for eight of eleven of those and she has not had to do the whole of them in seven days since she was seventeen.

## How She Counts A Stair That Turns

Counting a stair that turns is not counting a stair.

Counting a flat flight is arithmetic. Counting a stair that turns is counting the turns, and the turns are the only thing that goes wrong, and she found that out on the sixteenth and lost forty of them before she worked out what to do about it.

What she did on the sixteenth was count the going and the turning together: one, two, three, and turn, four, five, six, and turn.

And she got to the eighth turn and lost the number and started again at one, which she has not done since she was ten years old with a bundle of kindling on her back.

So on the seventeenth she stopped counting the steps and counted only the turns, and she came up out loud on each one, and she got sixty-four turns in a day, and sixty-four is a number she can hold.

On the eighteenth she counted the steps in the straight runs between the turns, and there were nine of those runs that were long enough to count, and the longest of them was a hundred and forty steps, and one hundred and forty is a number she can hold.

On the nineteenth she put the three counts together out loud on a step with her back to a wall, three fingers and a thumb on her knee, and she said:

Sixty-four turns. Nine runs. One hundred and forty steps in the long one and the other eight between thirty and ninety. Six hundred and forty and then the rest.

And the rest is what stopped being a thing she could hold.

## What She Is Carrying On A Stair

And she put the other thing out loud on the same step, because she was up there with it and she is not going to leave a thing unsaid on a stair.

She said, “I am going up a stair that turns with an arm that is open, at seven counts of breath on anything that rises, and I am going to go all the way up it.”

She said, “And if I get to the top of it I will not know what is up there. That is the plain truth of it and I have got no instrument for what is up there and there is no person up there to describe it and there is not one book in this building that says what is on the top of that stair.”

She said, “And the reason I am going up it anyway is not courage. It is that I have been at the bottom of stairs since the seventh of the month beginning 2,054 and every single thing that has ever happened to me has happened at the bottom of a stair, and there is one thing at the top of this one and I would like to know whether it is worth the seven counts.”


## Nine Hundred

And on the nineteenth of the month beginning 2,294, at the fourth hour, standing on a step on a stair that turns, she said nine hundred out loud and could not go on.

She said it again at the fifth hour from a step nine turns further up and it was still nine hundred, and then she did it again on the way down, on the steps, with her hand on the wall, saying it out loud in the ordinary voice the whole way, and nine hundred is nine hundred and it never went to nine hundred and one.

What stopped is not her legs. Her legs have gone up and down four miles of mountain road in nine years and they are not what stopped.

What stopped is that the number would not stay in her head.

She can hold four numbers. She can hold five hundred and forty-four words on a hand-made strip nine inches long and the same sum under it, and she can hold eighty-one lines and their total, and she has carried a bar nine feet nine on a left shoulder since she was nine years old without once putting it down and having to think about where it was.

She cannot hold nine hundred.

That has never happened to her about a figure before, and she stood on a step with a wall on both sides of her and her breath at nine and said it out loud, to a stair, in the ordinary voice:

“Nine hundred stops being a thing I can hold at about the nine hundredth and it does not get better the higher I get and I have just gone up nine turns to find out whether it does.”

## Where The Number Went

And then she did the thing she does, which is to put a thing somewhere the thing exists outside her, and for the first time in nineteen years she had nowhere.

There is no book in this room. There is no slate. There is no chalk and there is no frame and there is no ground and there is no person to say it to.

There are five blank leaves against her ribs and she has kept them blank for ten years and she put her hand flat over the shape of them through her shirt and said out loud, standing on a step, that nothing that comes out of this stair is going into them, and that this number is the first one in ten years that has wanted to go in there and the answer is the same answer it has been for ten years.

And there is a stub of a nib and a cake of ink in a bag at the bottom of the first four flights with a dish and a lump of clay and a ring the width of a finger and a key and a strip of hand-made paper, and she has not been down those eight hundred and eighty steps since the third of this month, and that is where a number like this belongs, because a number like this is not a thing to be remembered, it is a thing to be written down.

## The Decision On The Stair

She came down the nine turns standing still and her breath came off nine in a count of sixty and went to seven and stayed at seven, and she got to the floor of the room at the seventh hour of the nineteenth and did not go to the second stair again that day.

And she said the decision out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice, at the foot of a stair with a bar on the floor against the wall on the left and a person standing at the far end of a table who has not moved once in four days.

She said, “I am going to have to go down eight flights and get the nib and the ink, and then come back up eight hundred and eighty steps with an open arm and a bag in my hand, and put a number on a piece of paper that I have had against my ribs for ten years.”

She said, “I am not going to do it this month.”

She said, “I do not know yet whether that number is nine hundred or something past it, and I am not going to write down a number I have not finished counting, because a line on a thing is not a decision and it stops being a decision the second it is down, and I have learned that one the hard way.”

She sat down on the floor with her back against the wall on the left, opposite the bar, and did not touch it, and waited for a day she cannot see turn.
