# What The Box Was For

She worked it out over four days and the four days were not trying to be anything.

She opened Pei’s drain at the fourth hour on the seventeenth and she was in the cut by the fifth and out at the seventh with the light off the field, and the arithmetic did what arithmetic does when a woman finally stops putting it off, which is to arrive all at once in the middle of a day she is otherwise working in.

The mill was a mile and a half back west of the gate. The counter was two miles and four hundred yards back west of the mill.

A cart had gone out of that yard on the eleventh loaded to the height of a man, with trays roped twice under boards and sacking, on the east road.

The seventeenth came and nothing happened on it.

That is the whole of the seventeenth. It was a pay-day nine days of road behind her, in a country where pays fell on the seventh and the seventeenth and the twenty-seventh, and there was no plank on that ground for anybody and nothing came of it, and she noted the fact the way a person notes a fact and went back to the bank.

## The Four Days

They had all four been ordinary, and that was the part she could not get over afterwards.

On the fourteenth she had walked a mile east of a mill she had stood half a mile short of for five months, and had asked a man at a gate for a number, and had been given one out loud in a sentence.

On the fifteenth a team had gone past on the road and the woman at the head of it had told her what the ground did to a wheel in the wet, and had gone on east at the speed she had been going.

On the sixteenth she had taken eight coppers into her own hand at a gate and had spent most of it in the same afternoon in a lean-to past the mill, and had gone back to the shed and put a light in a ring of brick and clay and watched it come up.

On the seventeenth she had gone back down into the same cut at the fourth hour, because that is where the reach was.

Four ordinary days. A number, a road, a light, a drain.

And under all four of them, the whole time, sitting in her the way a stone sits in a boot, there had been a tray going somewhere she could not follow, and she had not once put the two things in one sentence.

She put them in one sentence on the seventeenth, at about the fifth hour, in eight feet of wet stone, with a bar in her left hand.

## The Arithmetic

Here is what she had and here is what she did with it.

The man with the rule had said five sentences on the eleventh of a month between two trays and had not looked up for any of them.

The clerk had written a line on the first of the month, with a date against it, and a rule at the side of it about a book that had gone out of a room.

She had a tray somewhere between those two things and she was not going to be able to find out which one it was in and it did not matter, because either way it went up.

And the whole of her — the column, and the word, and the name at the top of it — went up on the first cart after the thirtieth, which is what the thirtieth was.

She stood at the top of the bank with the bar in her left hand and said it out loud in the ordinary voice, to nobody, in about as long as it takes to say four sentences, the way a person says a thing when they have finally got it into the right order to be said in.

“Every book that closes clean on the thirtieth goes up a stair to a floor where somebody reads it.”

“Mine closed on the thirtieth. It went up on the first cart.”

“There is one name in it and a word above the hours, and I put the word there by taking a stage in the open where eleven men could see me and nowhere could hear it.”

“So somebody has read me. Not my hours. Me.”

## What A Reader Is For

She let the bank stand still for a minute and went on down into the cut, because the water was still moving at the bottom and there was another reach of it on this ground that would not keep if she left it.

Here was the whole of what she had worked out, standing in the water with the silt going past her boots.

A name in a book does not have to be looked at to be acted on. That is not true inside a bound, where the only thing that ever made anything real was the layer saying it out loud to every living person at once, and where a thing that was not said had not happened.

Past the last working point there was no saying. There was a hand with a brush and a room and a stack, and the hand did not have to know her, and did not have to be anywhere near her, and did not have to be told anything by anybody.

And it did not need to be near her, because a column is a column whether the woman is standing behind it or nine days of road away with her hands full.

## What She Had Wanted

That was the whole of it and it took about as long as saying it.

She had wanted to be checked.

She had wanted it for five months and she had said so out loud on a bank once and had not known until this moment that wanting it was the thing that had done this.

She stood at the top of the bank in the wind with the light going and worked through it and did not make a speech anywhere, because there was nobody there and because she was not a person who made speeches.

At the second hour of the seventeenth of a month she had stood in the middle of a reach at the last working point in front of eleven men and taken a stage where every one of them could see her doing it and not one person inside nine days of road could hear anything at all, and she had done it on purpose.

The reason she had done it on purpose was that it was the only instrument on that ground that could check a woman.

And it had checked her.

That is what the box was for.

## And What Else It Was For

A witness knows where a thing was done.

A witness was standing on a reach in front of eleven men, in daylight, with a date on it that eleven men could have said out loud and had not said.

A reader knows what it says.

And a reader can do something about what it says, and does not have to be near it, and does not have to be a man she has ever met, and does not have to have been in a room while she was in it.

The counter had been a witness. He had watched her go out of his room five times in five months and had never once asked her anything, and he had been standing four hundred yards away in a building with a stove in it the whole of the time she did everything that mattered.

The box was not him.

She stood at the top of that bank and understood the whole of what had happened to her on the seventeenth of a month in about the time it takes to put a bar down on flat ground, and it came out the wrong way round from the way she had wanted it, and there was not anybody to be angry at anywhere on that ground for doing it.

## The Question She Asked Herself

She asked herself, in the ordinary voice, standing in the cut with a bar in her hand and silt coming off it, whether a word can be taken out of a book once the book is on a floor above.

She answered herself in four words.

She did not believe any of the four.

## What She Would Not Have Given Back

Underneath all of it, quieter, and worse, and the thing she was actually carrying down off that bank:

She would not have given it back.

Not the stage. Not the word in the book. Not the eleven men and the daylight and the second hour of a night she had chosen to be seen in.

If somebody had come up that bank on the day and told her that the check would go up a stair to a floor she would never stand on, in a hand she would never see, and be read by a person who would never be near her — she would have said yes anyway.

That was the finding. Not that she had been had.

That she would have gone into it again, knowing the stair, because she had been the strongest body within nine days of road for four years and the only thing she had ever wanted out of that was somebody to say she was worth something.

She sat on the bank edge with her right hand open on her knee and let that sit where it was for about as long as a person lets a thing sit.

Then she got up and went back down into the cut and finished the reach she had started, because the gate pays for the reach and the reach was not finished.

She was not going and standing under the post that evening and she was not going and finding the board, and she did not go and stand in a room with a stove in it.

She went back into the water at about the seventh hour and did not come out of it until the light was off the field.

In the shed at the mill, on the boards, with the ring banked low behind her and her coat still on:

“It has been a week since the tray left that yard,” he said. “That is longer than the road from here to the post, and it is going up faster than the road goes.”

She did not answer him and she did not tell him to stop, and she put the oilcloth against her ribs and left it there.
