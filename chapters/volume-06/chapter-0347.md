# Nine Feet Off The Road

He was at nine feet off the cart track where he had been for five months, with the notebook shut under his arm and no other paper of any kind, and he was doing the only thing he ever did on that ground, which was standing there and not coming inside nine feet.

She had never once asked him a question.

That was not a thing she had decided on.

She had asked the man with the brush one question a month for three months and the man behind the counter one a month before that and the man who kept the list however many the asking took. In five months she had never opened her mouth to the man with the notebook, because he bought nothing and asked nothing and had never once given her a reason to think that a question of hers was going to be answered by him.

She stopped at eleven feet.

## How She Got There

She had walked out of the works at about the seventh hour and then stood at the gate of the yard for about ten minutes, which was nine minutes longer than standing at that gate ever took her.

A man who buys nothing and asks nothing is a man who cannot be managed. She had worked that out about nine days after she came past the mill and had not needed to look at it since, because it had been true every morning since and she had never wanted to break it.

She wanted to break it.

There was one thing on that ground that she could not get out of any other person, and it was not the road and it was not the room and it was not the fine hand. It was whether anybody on nine days of road could write a letter that would arrive somewhere.

## What He Does In A Day

She did not ask that one. She asked what she had walked out with.

He shifted the notebook under his arm.

“Four lines a day,” he said. “A certificate of something, a measurement of something, a note of two men I have watched, and a page at the end that never gets read. That has been the day for two years and four months and I have written about nine hundred pages of it going unread.”

“Then where does it go.”

“In with the district,” he said. “At the end of the month, in a bundle, with about nine other bundles, and I do not go with it and I have never once seen anybody who took one.”

He did not say what the district was or where it was or how many pages a year it took, and she did not ask again, because a man who gives a person something for nothing three times in five months is a man who is going to stop.

## The Question

“What is a room with a date on it.”

She said it in the ordinary voice, in daylight, in the middle of a flat field with the works two miles behind her and nothing in the world in front of her but nine feet of wet grass and a man.

He did not take the notebook out from under his arm.

“Yes,” he said. “There are four rooms on the ground I have been in and two of them shut, and both of them shut on the last day of a month, and neither of them shut because of anything I did or did not write.”

That was all of it, and he stopped there, and he did not add what the rooms were for.

## What She Wanted To Ask And Did Not

She had a second one. It was the one she had wanted since the seventeenth of a month, which is four weeks back, when a piece of cloth about as wide as a hand had come down a road with a room at the end of it and a date under the room.

She did not ask it, because asking a second one tells a man the first one mattered, and she had spent four years learning what that costs a person who has to write it down.

## The Other Thing

“You have not asked me anything since the third month,” he said. “I have been waiting to see whether you would.”

“No.”

“I am still here,” he said. “And I will not come inside nine feet again unless you put me there. And my paper is still in with the district and a writ is not issued to a field agent, and you know both of those things because you have heard one of them off me and worked the other one out.”

That was two sentences more than she had got and she had not asked for either of them, and she stood there in the wet grass and did not know what to do with a man who gives a person something for nothing twice.

## What She Wanted From Him

She wanted him to be angry with her.

She had a strip folded in three against her ribs with two columns on it and a fine hand at the end of one of them.

She had walked nine days of road in her head every day for four weeks trying to work out what she was going to do about it, and she had wanted for about six days to say one true thing out loud to another person and have them be angry, because anger is a thing that can be put down and a fear is not.

He was not angry. He had never once been angry and he was not going to be now, and there was no handle on him anywhere, and he had said the whole of what he had in six sentences and shut his mouth again.

What she had instead, standing in eleven feet of wet grass at about the fourth hour, was the plain fact of what she had walked out there to get. She had wanted a person to be angry and she had got the ordinary shape of the only thing that might be able to carry a paper nine days of road, and it was a bundle a man put in at the end of a month that he had never seen anybody take.

## The Part About The Car

A carter came past at about the fifth hour with a load of dressed stone on the cart for the works and slowed for the rut, and the man with the notebook did not buy anything and did not ask the carter anything and the carter did not look at him either, and they have been passing each other on that track at intervals for five months without one word between them.

She stood and watched the cart go and understood that this was the whole of his life out here and that she was standing in it asking him for pieces of it.

He turned back down the track after it had gone about a hundred yards, at the pace of a man who has nine pages to write and nowhere to write them except a bench about four miles off.

## On The Way Back

She walked back east with the bar on her shoulder and about fourteen feet of ground between them the whole way, and she got one thing out of the two she had wanted.

She had wanted to know what a room with a date on it was, and she had got four rooms and two that shut and a reason that was not her.

The rest of it she walked three miles with and had still not got when she got to the works, and what she had instead was the ordinary fact that she was the only person on nine days of road who was going to have to stand in one, and that the ground she stood in to reach it was nine days long and she had four fingers on one hand and a gate that paid a third of a reach.
