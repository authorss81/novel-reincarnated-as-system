# The Fire She Did Not Need

She lit it on the nineteenth of a month at about the ninth hour at the end of a full day, and it was the first time in five months she had put a whole heap of that material into that ring and had enough of it left over to build a bank around and close over the top.

It came up in about four minutes and it went on coming up for another nine and it was a fire.

She stood in the shed at about the tenth hour with her face about a foot from it and it put the heat on her the way a fire does, and the shed was warm from the corner to the door for the first time since the eighth of the last month, and she could feel it on her face and her neck and the back of her hands.

Her hands did not tell her anything about it.

That was the whole of the first ten minutes and she stood there and let about nine of them go by before she admitted it to herself.

## The Arithmetic Of A Heat

A ring is not for heat. She had said that out loud on the fifteenth of a month in this shed and it had been true then and it was a colder truth now than it had been.

The heat is as far as a hand goes.

And on the nineteenth of a month she could put a hand anywhere on her body and it was warm already, and it had been warm for eighteen days, and it was going to be warm for the rest of her life, and she had bought eight days of it with a week of her own wall and a cart that went when it had no business going and a hole in a yard’s account about eleven days away.

She had bought the one thing on nine days of road that she no longer needed, and she had bought it at the most expensive rate anything had ever been bought on that ground, and the fire was doing exactly what she had paid it to do.

## What It Answered

She put her hand flat on the brick at about the eleventh hour with the ring burning behind her and waited about as long as she had waited on the twenty-eighth of the last month, which was about nine minutes.

Nothing came.

Not slowly. There was no cold coming up through the base and no place in her where anything went out. She stood in a shed with a hand on a burning ring and there was no temperature in the room that was not in her hands already, and the connection that had gone out of her on the twenty-fourth of the last month at the top of a ditch had not come back because there was a heap of dressed stone in the corner of a shed.

She took her hand off and banked it down to about a third and sat on the boards with her back to the wall.

That is what a clearing paid in materials bought her.

Eight days of a fire, in a shed, three miles east of a yard, and a woman sitting on the boards beside it at about the eleventh hour of the twentieth of a month, eighteen years old, with the best fire within nine days of road.

## What The Clearing Asked Of A Body

It took her seven days, and it took her about a hundred and ten bricks under one arm on three of them and twenty feet of footing on four of them, and it took her a block into a ditch.

It took her about eight loads she did not make because a cart went on the wrong day, and twenty-three pieces broken down one at a time on a flat stone by a door at about eleven minutes a piece, which is about four hours of her hands that no book has anywhere and never will have.

And it took her this, which she had not thought about before the second day and had thought about every hour since:

It took the reach.

Not the furnace. Her foundation. The part of her that a clearing settles out of.

She had felt it come up twice off the lane crossing and twice off the retaining wall, and it had gone out through the base both times, and the first time it had taken the fire and the second time it had taken the answering, and she had known what the second one was going to cost about nine minutes after she had agreed to it at a ditch.

She had known.

That was the thing. She had sat on a sill at the seventh hour on the twenty-fourth of the last month and had been told the price in about four sentences and had said yes to it in one word, and she had known then exactly what the fire and the answering were and had known they were the two things she had in the world.

She said yes.

## Half A Reach

She went to Pei’s gate on the twentieth and the twenty-first and worked half a reach both days, because two and a half a day is bread and bread is not a box and does not settle anything.

Half a reach at five coppers a reach is two and a half, and half a reach is what her body can open on a day, and her body had got stronger twice in the last two months and can open half a reach instead of a reach and a half, and will open a quarter of one at whatever she takes next.

That is the gate.

The gate has never once in five months not been exactly her, and it will be exactly her again in about nine months, and it is not cruel about it and nobody at that gate has ever said a word about it.

And two and a half a day is bread and bread is most of what she spends, and everything else she has had in the last two months has come out of a yard in pieces about the size of two fists, and the fire in the corner of this shed is burning at about a third because that is what it was worth, and she is sitting on the boards beside it.

## The Nineteenth Night

She sat there until about the second hour with the ring banked to a third and the shed warm for about nine feet and the wind going at the corner of the door, and she made herself do the whole of it in the ordinary order, the way she had made herself do it in a bank under a wet clay a month ago, because the only two ways she had of finding out what a night was were to lie in it and to lie in it without knowing.

The bank. Two feet of standing clay, four minutes, and a person in that position makes no sound at all.

The shed. Boards, a banked fire, a door shut against the wind, and about nine feet of a room that is warm, and a woman in it who has put a hand on the brick and been told nothing.

Both of them had been the whole of what she had.

She got up at about the fourth hour and put her hand on the flat stone by the door to see whether it was frost, and it was not, and she could not have told.

## The Sentence

She was eighteen years old, and she had been the strongest body within nine days of road for four years, and she had a heap of dressed stone in a corner and a fire going and a woman’s name at the top of a column in a book two miles and four hundred yards west and a blank on a board in a yard three miles east with about two days to go on it.

And there was nothing at all in this shed with her in it that was hers except the fire, and the fire only answered when she told it to.
