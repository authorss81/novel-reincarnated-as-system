# The Room With Two Things In It

She wrote the answer on the back of the strip on the ninth of a month at about the fourth hour sitting on the boards with the ring nine feet away, because a refusal that is only spoken is a thing that stops at the person it was spoken to, and a man who keeps a board is not the person it has to stop at.

Eleven minutes a line with the flat of her second finger, and every line she wrote anywhere cost its hour twice over since the twenty-fifth of a month about six months back, and she wrote three lines.

She put the flat of the second finger on the boards and pressed and it came away and she wrote the first of them.

“Ning Yan, of Lowwater. A second holder is not named and will not be named. The holder named is the only one.”

It came out of the flat of her finger and not out of a pen, and the letters were the size of a child’s and the spacing was wrong on about one line in three, and she could read all of it, because she had made all of it.

That was three lines and it took her about half an hour and by the end of the second one her right arm had been open for most of it.

## What It Cost To Write

Nothing came out of the ground she was standing on.

That was the arrangement, and it was the arrangement at every stage and every spending and every time she had put her hand on a bar and asked for something in four years. Whatever it was came out of the thing under her base and never out of her hands.

The three lines went back up the road on the next bundle on the twelfth of a month with about nine others, folded in two because they fold in two.

## The Two Lines Left

The ditch line came off the board on the tenth and she had it in four days and paid in a box of the clay that had come out of it.

The head line came off on the fourteenth and she had that in three, against the board’s three, and it was a cut at the near side of a head where the footing was clay under the clay and she took it with a bar in one hand and a hand on the out-side and it was five days of her hands and nothing else.

And after the head the board was bare. Not thin. Bare, with the ruled space at the left and four columns and no line running into any of them.

## The Morning Of The Fourteenth

She went in on the fourteenth at the seventh hour and had the head in before the ninth, and she was at the gateway at the tenth with the box off and the boards on and the man behind the list counting it, and then she stood in the gateway and looked at the board with the last line gone off it.

Four columns. The ruled space at the left with her name in it and nothing running into any of them.

That was the whole of what she had cleared in seven weeks and there had been four lines on the wall on the eleventh of the month last, and there were now none.

## What She Was Living On

One copper a day at the gate.

Two boxes of clay off two lines in five days, and a box is four days of the ring, and about two days of the box was clay you could put into a fire and the rest of it was rubble.

Bread at two a day and a store five miles off, and she went to it on the way to the gate twice a week and she had not had clay and nothing in about five days.

She stood in the gateway at the works on the fifteenth and looked at the bare board and understood, standing there, that she was the reason it was bare, and that it had been going to be bare in about six weeks whatever she did, and that a person who could take a stone line would have had four lines and two days of it in each and would still be standing there in six weeks.

The man who kept the list came out with the bench cover over the board and put two battens across it.

“Season turns,” he said. “It comes back then and it comes back full, because it is the same board and the same men and the same clay. It has come back every year since I came here.”

“How long.”

“Until the season turns,” he said, and put the key in his pocket, and he was right and she could not get another word out of him about the date.

## The Night Before

She went out to the far side of the elms on the twenty-third and came back at about the eighth hour with nothing in her hands because there had been nothing to fetch, and she lay on the boards for about two hours with nothing to do, which was the second time since she came past the mill.

The first one was on the sixth of the month when she had banked the ring and then sat and looked at the two doors of the shed, and she had found out then that a body with two spare hours on boards in a shed cleans a bar.

This time she had cleaned the bar on the second hour and had about an hour and a half left, and she spent it on the thing she had been carrying since the seventeenth of a month, which was that she had no idea what a holding was.

She had one now, or the name of one, or the refusal of one. She had not got the thing.

She asked him one question at the second hour on the twenty-third and it was not about the second holder and it was not about the doors.

“Can you write.”

“No,” he said. “Not since before you were born, and not since the fourth year, and I have not looked for it since about the third year because a thing you cannot do is not a thing you go looking for.”

He did not say anything else about it and she did not ask again.

## The Counter, The Twenty-Fourth

She went up the mill road to the counting house at the second hour of the twenty-fourth with her one question a month in her, and she had had it ready since the eleventh, and it was the question she had stood at a counter a month before and not asked.

She got as far as the door and the man with the brush was coming down the stair with his hands full of sheets and stopped on the third step from the bottom.

“You have not come up this month,” he said.

“I have not.”

He looked at the sheets in his hands and then at her, and then he went back up four steps and put them down on the top one, and came down again.

“You have had your question,” he said, “and you have had it since the eleventh, and I am going to let you stand there with it.”

## What Was On The Top Step

There were two things on the top step and not one of them was the sheet he had been carrying.

One of them was a sheet with a single column on it, days down the margin and hours against them, and a word at the top she could read from nine feet away in a room with a stove in it.

“Ning Yan. Of Lowwater.”

The other one was a sheet with two columns on it, side by side, ruled, with a margin at the left, and the left-hand column was days, and the right-hand column was something else entirely.

And the right-hand column and the two lines at the foot of it were in a hand she had never seen, and it was finer than the hand he wrote in, and she had been looking at that hand since the seventeenth of a month, on strips and in books she could not get to.

He had not gone to the sheet. He had left both of them on the step.

She stood at the bottom of that stair at about four feet off with the stove behind her and nine men at their cups and read two lines of her own name in a hand she could read and then, about a foot further along, could not read one word of what the second of those sheets was saying about her.
