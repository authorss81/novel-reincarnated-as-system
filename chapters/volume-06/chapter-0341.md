# Four Counts And A Load

The cart went up the mill road at about the sixth hour on the first of a month with a cord round the tray and her in it, and she stood in the yard and watched it get past the elms and then she went east at the seventh hour and took the next line off the board.

## What Was On The Board

A head at the second gully, one day and one box.

One day is a day the ground wants and not a day she can manage, and there had been six lines on that board on the seventh of a month and there were five on it now, and a gully head is about seventy bricks and a foot of footing on the low side and about two feet of it is clay under the clay.

She put the line inside her coat and read the back of it, which had her own two days on the eleventh of a month in a hand she could recognise at four feet, and she went down off the flat at about the eighth hour with the bar.

## The Load

She made about nine loads of it and it was not going to be nine loads, because a day is a day.

So she made three.

Four dressed pieces under one arm, taken off a stack against a wall in a size that was meant to be carried two at a time by a man with two arms, and the bar in her left hand, and a bank of four feet between her and the top of the gully.

She had done it about nine hundred times in four years with eleven holds in that hand, and she had done it about four hundred times in five months with one of them gone.

## Five

Her breath went to five on the second load and she noticed it at the top of the bank with the pieces on her arm and the bar in her hand, because the third count is where she always counts and she had been counting four since she was nine years old.

She set the pieces down on the top of that bank and stood still over them and counted.

Four.

She picked them up and went down the far side and up the near side again and it went to five about halfway up, at the third count, the same place, and it came back to four when she put them down on the gully floor.

She did it three more times, because a woman who has been counting a thing since she was nine does not believe it once.

Five on the way up. Four on the way down.

## What She Took From It

That is the measure of the edge and it is not a figure about a furnace and it is not a stage.

It is the place on a four-foot bank at which a body with four fingers and a thumb on one hand and eight holds on the other stops being able to decide things and starts being a thing that is being carried. Everything below that place is work and everything above it is a fall.

She went up it four more times that day with three pieces and it did not go to five, and she went up it twice with five and it went to five at the second count and stayed there and she put five down on a gully floor at about the seventh hour and did not go up with five again.

That was the whole of what she learned and she learned it in one day on a bank of four feet at the second gully, and she wrote none of it down anywhere, because there is nothing to write it in.

## The Day

She got the head in on the second evening at about the seventh hour with the bar laid down on the bank and her right hand open on the last piece, and the last piece went in and she sat down on the bank with her back to nothing.

She had cleared a listing in a day that the board had said was a day.

The man who kept the list was not there and did not come out and she was on her own with a bar and four hours of light, and she had not told anybody that she was doing it in one day with three loads, and there was nobody on nine days of road she could have told.

## What She Wanted And Did Not Get

She wanted to put the bar down for about nine minutes.

That is the whole of what a body asks for after a day like that and it is not a stage and it is not a settlement and nothing comes up through the base. It is an arm that has been open half the time for four years, and a right hand with four fingers and a thumb on it, and eight holds where there were eleven, and a bank of four feet that she had gone up eleven times.

She sat on the bank for about nine minutes and then she got up and carried the bar three miles west and lit the ring, which was already lit, and lay on the boards in the shed.

## The Fourteen Days

There were fourteen days between the second of a month and the sixteenth, and she worked eleven of them.

She worked Pei’s gate two and a half a day on six of them and she worked the gate and the gully on four, and she did not work on one.

The ring stood lit in a shed with nobody in it from the fourth hour to the fourth hour every night of those fourteen days. She went in once a day for a third of a sack and once a day to bank it. After the second week she stopped going in at night at all and only went for the clay.

She counted the breath on a loaded reach every morning of those fourteen days because a stage takes something out of a body and there is no way on nine days of road to ask anybody what it takes next.

Four.

Four on a loaded reach, at the third count, going up a bank of four feet, with four pieces under one arm and the bar in her left hand, on the second of a month, on the seventh, on the ninth, on the twelfth and on the fourteenth.

Four every one of those five days.

There was one day in the fourteen that she did not work, and she spent it at the mill.

She broke up a sack on the flat stone by the door, four lumps at about eleven minutes a piece, and banked the ring with three handfuls of ash at about the ninth hour, and then she sat on the boards with her back to the wall for about two hours with nothing to do, which is a thing she had not done since she came out of the bound.

It was the first time in five months that the fire had been lit and she had nothing to put into it. She had bought a sack of clay and then had bought nine days of fire that cost nothing, and the two had come out of the same four fingers, and in the five months before this she had gone in every fourth night with three coppers and gone out about two hours later with an empty corner.

She found out on the third night that there is a thing a body does with two spare hours on boards in a shed, and it is to clean the bar, and then to sit and look at the two doors.

And she wrote none of it down, and she told nobody, and she did not sleep well on about four of the fourteen nights, and on the sixteenth of a month she carried the bar out past the last hedge before the light and laid it down on the grass, in a place where nothing within about half a mile had ever heard anything.
