# What The Quiet Is For

The board had four lines on it on the twenty-first of a month and two of them had come down since the eleventh, and she stood in the gateway at about the seventh hour and counted them twice and knew both times.

The man who kept the list had the board off the bench and his ink out and was doing a job on it that took him about four minutes, which was two minutes longer than he took.

## The Four On The Board

She read them standing up and she had read a board in a gateway about nine hundred times in seven weeks and had never once read it the way she read it that morning, which was from the bottom up, the way a woman reads what is left on a shelf.

Footing at the near ditch, two days, one box.

A head at the low end of the same ditch, three days, one box, and the footing on it was clay under the clay.

A cut at the long bank with nothing in it but length.

And a fourth that said ditch and said one day, which was a day the ground wanted and not a day a person could manage, and she had taken that one off the board on the fourth day of the month before and had done it in a day with three loads.

Four lines and two months, and then nothing until the season turned.

## What He Said About The Two Months

“When those four are done there is nothing on that board until the season turns,” he said. “Not because the yard has nothing to do. Because nobody has put anything down. It is not a season here the way it is at the yard nine miles west. It is a board and a man puts lines on it when he has them.”

He squared the board up on the bench.

“There are two men on this yard’s books who are on nothing after those four. They go to the low work at the far end and the low work is cut and put back and it is not footing.”

He put the ink down.

“That is what the quiet is for. It is for the two men. It is not for you and it is not against you and I am not going to pretend to you that I can put a line on that board that is not a line there is work in.”

## The Four Sentences

He wiped the nib on the rag and looked up at the wall and not at her.

“There is a line out west of this gate about nine miles,” he said. “It is kept. There are men on it and they are paid by the day and their families are on it, and it has been there longer than my board.”

He put the cap on the ink.

“Nobody on this ground has ever needed one thing out of it.”

That was four sentences and he had said them the way a man says where he was born, and then he asked her whether she was taking the ditch line or the head.

## What She Said

“The ditch,” she said, and then, because she had been carrying it since the eleventh and it came out of her before she could hold it in, “And when the four are done, what is on this ground on the day after the last one.”

He looked at the board.

“A gate,” he said. “And whatever anybody wants to pay for out of a field.”

“That is not an answer.”

“That is the whole of what there is,” he said. “I have been in this gateway eleven years and I have never once had a person come to me and say they could not use a gate, and I have never once had a person come to me and say they wanted something the yard did not have. You are the first of either and I do not know which you are.”

## The Line She Took

She took the ditch line off the nails and folded it in two and put it inside her coat with the footing line that had been there since the eleventh, and the man behind the list put the board back on the wall and left the ruled space empty because the name in it was hers and a name does not go on a line that has been taken off.

That was the arrangement and it had been the arrangement for seven weeks.

She stood in the gateway for a moment after that and worked out the whole of the next ten days standing up: a ditch and a head, five days of cutting and five days of putting back, two boxes, and a box is four days of the ring.

And after the ditch and the head there would be one line and it would be the one-day cut at the ditch end, and she could do that in a day with three loads, and after that she would be standing in that gateway on a morning with nothing on the wall at all.

## The Anger

She was angry at him for about a minute and a quarter on the walk east, and it was the first time in five months she had been angry at anybody on that ground who had not done anything at all, and there was nothing wrong with him.

He was right. The board was right. The gate was right.

A yard that put a line on a board for a woman with four fingers on one hand and eight holds on the other, when it had nine men on its books who could take the line, would be doing a thing that a yard does not do. Every one of the men who worked in it would have known it was a favour and she would have had to live inside a favour for as long as she was there.

Everything on nine days of road that had ever touched her had been right.

That was not the new thing. The new thing was that being right had a price list and she was the only person paying any of it, and she had spent five months finding out that the list was written in a hand she could not read.

## The Walk East

She did not go straight back. She went up the made road past the second gully and stopped at the top of the bank she had been putting a footing back into for two days and stood on the crest of it in the last of the light.

Eleven feet of bank, stone under clay, put back a foot at a time in an order that would stand. She had done it and it had taken her two days against the board’s four and a man behind a stack had called it the way it wants to go back in, and there was not one other person on nine days of road who had any idea that she had done it.

She stood on the crest of it and counted what it had been worth. Two days of board pay. Two boxes of stone and clay at four days of the ring each.

And what it had cost was the whole of the reason she had gone to that bank, which was that the thing she could do was the thing the board could not pay her for, and she had gone there to find out whether it could.

It could not. The board paid by the length and the length was the only thing on it that was not stone.

She came down off the crest at about the eighth hour and went west with the bar on her shoulder and the ditch line against her ribs, and the two of them lay there together and neither of them was worth anything to anybody.

## The Last Of The Arithmetic

She stopped at the ditch on the low side of Pei’s field at about the eighth hour and did it standing up with the bar across her knees.

Four lines on the board. Two months before the season turned and the board bare. A gate at five coppers a reach and a third of a reach and a third of a reach is one and a half and it goes down at whatever she takes next.

The strip against her ribs with a date in it, thirteen days — no, seven now.

Seven days to the twenty-eighth. Nine days of road. A bar that needed two hands she did not have by the time she got there.

She wanted to put the bar down.

That was the whole of what she wanted on the bank at the eighth hour on the twenty-first of a month, and she had wanted it for about three weeks, and there was nowhere on nine days of road to put a bar down where it would still be in the morning.
