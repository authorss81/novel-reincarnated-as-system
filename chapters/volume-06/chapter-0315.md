# The Lane Crossing

The lane went over a ditch on two stones that had been laid by somebody who had not been in a hurry, and the ditch under it had been taking the foot of the far side for about nine years and had finally taken enough of it.

She went down into it on the fourth of a month at the fourth hour with the bar and about nine days of her own hands in it, and she came up out of it on the sixth at the seventh hour with a low head and an apron done in brick and clay that was not any worse than the work of two men.

## The Work

It is a head and an apron. The head is the wall you look at and the apron is the floor you look at, and the two of them meet at a corner, and the whole of it is about nine feet by four and it is deeper on the far side than it is on the near side because the far side is the side the water comes out on.

She could not hire a hod and did not have one. She carried every brick under one arm from the stack at the ditch head, one at a time, four fingers on it, and put it down on the bed she was laying, and the bed was a line and a level and a piece of string and a stone.

On the first day she laid about forty.

On the second day she worked out that forty was not the number and that the number was about a hundred and ten, and that a hundred and ten bricks under one arm up a bank of about four feet was a thing she could do in a day if she did nothing else at all, and she was not doing nothing else at all.

## The Second Day

She got up the bank with a brick under her arm at about the seventh hour and her right hand went open on it instead of closing, and the brick went off the bank into the ditch and she heard it go down and land.

She stood at the top of the bank and listened to it going down for about as long as it took.

It landed on stone and stopped.

That was the whole of the accident. Nothing hurt her, nothing broke in her that she has not had back once, and the right hand closed again about a second and a half later, which is faster than it used to do and she noticed that too.

## The Ditch

She went down for it at about the seventh hour with a rope off the hedge, because a brick is easier to carry up than to find in the dark at the bottom of nine feet of wet ditch.

She found it. She could not get a purchase on it in the water and the clay, with four fingers and a bar and about half an hour to try, and she went down that bank three times and got nothing off the wall.

The far side of that ditch is the ground that is gone. He had said so at the gate and he had said it in the ordinary voice and he had been right, and the far side went down under water and clay at about the second hour of that day and would not hold a foot anywhere a person could stand.

It is not coming out.

There was a brick down there that was worth about a ninth of a box and it was going to stay down there for as long as the ditch was a ditch, and she had put it there.

## Three Days

The board had said four days and she finished on the sixth at the seventh hour, which was three, and she went back and put the line back up on the board with the four nails in it and the three days written on the back of the piece of board in her own hand, because a line that has been on a board and taken off and put back is a different thing from a line that was never on it.

The man at the gate looked at the back of it for a moment.

“Three,” he said.

“Three.”

“The work is done or it is not. It is done.” He turned the board round and hung it up. “The day you did not use goes back on the board. I cannot give it to you and I am not going to try.”

He was right about that and she knew he was right about it while he was saying it, and it was the fourth time in five months that a man on that ground had been right about something, and for the first time in five months being right had cost her nothing at all.

## The Box

The box is a box. There is no name on it and no mark on it and it is a wooden tray with four sides and no lid, and it is about as big as a man can put both arms around.

He counted it out for her over the afternoon on the seventh into the box at the open end of the building: brick and clay, broken to a carrying size, the clay in lumps about the size of two fists and the brick in pieces that would go into the ring without being cut again.

It took him about as long to put it in the box as she had taken to lay a hundred and ten bricks.

That was the thing she stood in the yard and looked at for a while.

Five months of a season at five coppers a day had bought her the ring as it was and had not once bought her a piece of clay that a man had broken to a carrying size for her.

She had broken every piece of it herself, one lump at a time, on a flat stone, at the dark, with the flat of a second finger and about eleven minutes a lump, and she had done it perhaps nine times.

## What It Is Worth

One box, and the box was worth more than four days at a gate.

Four days at a gate is ten. One box is four days of fire, and she had been buying four days of fire with four days of her legs and her back and two and a half coppers at a time, and there had been a day four in every week that the ring was cold, and there had been a good many more than that.

And the fire was not the whole of it either, and she had got that out on the walk back across the flat with the bar on her shoulder and her right arm hanging the way it hangs.

She had laid a hundred and ten bricks under one arm and carried them up a bank of four feet, three days running, and the thing that had come out of the ground at the ditch head was standing square and clean and would be there in the spring.

## At The Fourth Hour

She was on the flat at the fourth hour of the seventh of a month with the bar over her left shoulder and nothing on her at all, about a mile east of the yard, and the cold came up through the base.

It came the way it had come at the fourth hour in a yard four hundred yards past the post nine days of road behind her, and not into her but through her, the way a thing comes up through a floor out of a room below.

It sat in the four counts of her breath for the length of about four and it went away.

She stood on the flat and waited for it to not come back, and it did come back, at about nine minutes after, and it was bigger the second time, and she had put a hand on a post by the road and had not gone down.

Her right hand was warm. She looked at it — four fingers and a thumb, the flat of the second one longer than it had ever been — and it was warm from the inside, and it was not the brick.

She had laid the last of it at about the seventh hour and it was the fourth hour, and nothing had touched her hand in nine hours.
