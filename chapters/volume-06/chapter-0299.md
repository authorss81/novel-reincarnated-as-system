# The First Cart

The eighth of the month came clear and she went east, which was a thing she had not done once in five months, and she went at the fourth hour because a cart came in at the fourth hour and she wanted to see a box come out of it.

The bar was on her right shoulder, which was the wrong shoulder, and she moved it to the left at the cart turn without breaking stride, and that was the whole of the way her body argued with her.

Past the cart turn the ground changed. The hedgerow thinned the way it had thinned the two times she had walked this stretch, and the wheel came up out of the dark ahead of her standing still, in the way a thing stands when it has been standing a long time.

She had looked at that wheel four times in five months and never gone near it. On the seventh she had stood about half a mile short of it in the dark and turned round and walked back up the track to her own boards.

Today she had a reason, and the reason was in a box.

## The Yard

The mill was a working building. That was the first thing and she had five months of not knowing it, because she had never been close enough to find out what it was for.

It was a long low shed with a loading door standing open on the yard side, and a wheel on the far side of it with a race round it that had gone the colour of the boards, and the boards of the wheel itself had warped enough in five months that a gap had opened at one of the joins. Under the wheel there was a channel cut in stone and the channel was dry and there was weed in it.

She stood in the yard and looked at the wheel for about as long as it took her to work out that the wheel was not what the place was for.

There was straw on the ground where a horse had stood and not moved for a day, and a bench inside the door with a blanket folded on the end of it, and a paste-pot on a shelf with a brush stood in it and the brush had gone hard in the pot and nobody had washed it out.

The yard was about half a stone’s throw across and it had been swept, and a man was standing in the middle of it with a flat rule in one hand.

He was somewhere past forty. He had the shoulders of a man who has carried trays for thirty years and does not know that that is a thing that gives a man shoulders. He was doing the thing he did every morning and he had been doing it since before she came to this ground.

## The Cord

The cart came in off the track at about the fourth hour, one animal and a low flat bed with the shafts up.

It was not loaded on the way in. That was the thing she had not expected and could not afterwards explain to herself. She had walked two miles on the strength of a sentence a man at a plank had said in the ordinary voice, and the cart had come in empty except for a canvas bag on the seat and a boy of about sixteen sitting with his legs hanging.

The boy put the bag down on the bench and went to stand by the animal, and the man with the rule came over it.

She watched him take the bag up.

She knew the bag. That was the thing that took her at the throat and did not let go. It was a bag the man at the counter had used to send books out of his room for five months, canvas with a leather patch at one corner, and the patch had been sewn on with a shoe lace because somebody had once run a hook through it and had not wanted to pay for a new one.

He untied the mouth of it. He went through it one book at a time, the way a man goes through a bag he has emptied a thousand times, and he did not read the spine of any of them and he did not turn any of them to see what was on the page.

There were four.

He put each one into a tray. A tray was a wooden thing with a low rim and a lid that did not quite close at two corners because the wood had warped, and he turned it once as he set it down, the way a man turns a thing to find the side that will sit flat.

Then he tied the cord.

The cord was a length of twisted stuff and he passed it twice round the tray and through the holes in the rim and pulled it and turned the whole thing over once in his hand to set the knot on the underside, where it would not chafe against the next tray.

He did not look at what was in it.

She stood about ten feet away with the bar on her shoulder and watched a man tie a cord round a tray, and the whole of her five months came down to that.

## What She Wanted

She wanted to be handed the box.

Not the book. She had not wanted the book since the seventh and she had not expected it and it was four hundred yards behind her in a room with a stove in it, under a cloth on a shelf, which is where a shut book goes in an office that is not going to need it again until the first.

She wanted the tray.

She wanted a man to have carried it out of that bag and put it into her hands at the loading door, and to have said nothing at all while he did it, and for her to have stood there holding four or five months of herself in a wooden thing with a warped lid, and for the walk back to be two miles with a weight in it.

There was no version of that morning in which it happened. The tray went onto a stack under a lean-to at the side of the yard, and the stack went under a cover of boards and sacking, and a second tray went on the top of it, and the man did not lock the lean-to because there is nothing in a country like this that anybody has ever come back for.

She had been the strongest body within nine days of road for four years.

She stood in a yard and could not get to a tray with a cord on it.

## The Round

The cart went out again at about the sixth hour and it was not the same cart.

It came in off the east road instead of the west track and it was loaded, and the loading was about eleven trays high and roped twice, and the animal pulling it was not the animal that had come in.

That was the rule, and she worked it out standing in the yard by herself with nothing to go on but a cart that had come in light and a cart that went out heavy.

The cart belongs to a round. It goes out and it comes back and on the way back it is whatever the round is for that day.

She had got here on the strength of a box that had not come on that cart and would not go on it either, because that cart had gone out loaded and up the east road, and she was not going with it.

## What Was In It

She could put the whole of it in order without looking at it, and she did, standing in the yard, because there was nothing else to do with the inside of her head.

The column ran from the second of a month four months back to the thirtieth, with twelve gaps in it and every one of them entered by her own hand at the plank, and the far date at the top of the last entry was in the small hand of a clerk with a slate who had put his own mark beside it five months earlier and had not asked her a single question about either end of it.

Above the column, where a book on a working reach keeps the wage-holder’s standing, there was a word she had put there herself, at the second hour of the seventeenth of a month, in the open, in front of eleven men who could see her and nobody at all who could hear anything.

The man with the rule turned a tray over to set the flat side down and tied a cord on it and put it on a stack under a lean-to.

She did not get to see whether that tray was the one her column was in, and she was not going to ask, because a man who ties cords does not open them and a question about which tray is which is the kind of question that makes a man look at you.

## The Night

The man in the shed asked her nothing.

That was the whole of the first day.

She put the bar down inside the door by the bench, and she sat on the bench with her back to the wall, and she took out what she had carried out of the yard that morning — bread and a piece of the dry cake she had been carrying for eleven days — and she ate it.

He went on with what he was doing at the far end of the floor, which was a broom and then a bench and then a look out of the door.

He did not ask her who she was, or where she had come from, or where she was going, or whether she had a place to be at the dark.

He did not ask her anything and he did not offer her anything, and neither of those was an insult, and she had been on a line of work for five months where men had asked her nothing and paid her by the day and neither of them had ever been an insult either.

That was the shape of the ground.

The floor of that shed was boards an inch off the ground and it was dry and it was a great deal better than the ground outside, and she was two miles east of the last thing she owned and she slept on it.

Her ring was four hundred yards east of the post at the yard behind her, opened and cold in the dark, with the ash of the last firing spread out on the flat stone and not put back in.

She lay on the boards and put her right hand out flat on her coat, four fingers and a thumb, and the arm was open and she let it be open, and she thought about a tray with a cord on it going up an east road in the dark without her.

The eighth of a month closed two miles east of the last working point of a line of work, on a bench in a shed, in a country that had asked her nothing at all.
