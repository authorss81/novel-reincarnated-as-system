# He Does Not Want It Answered

Bai Zhu was at nine feet off the cart track on the fifteenth of a month at about the fourth hour with his notebook shut under his arm and no other paper of any kind, the way he had been at nine feet off a track for two years and had been since before she came out of the bound.

She had seen the cart go down that road on the evening of the thirteenth and it had not gone past him, because the cart turns at the elms and the track he stands on is a different one.

He did not ask her a question. He had never asked her a question in any room in any county and he was not going to start on a track two miles east of a mill.

“There is a new paper,” he said, “and it is not yours.”

## What He Was Doing There

He was attending.

That was the whole of the reason he was on that ground and it had been the whole of the reason for two years, and it is what a man does when he has an order that does not come to him as a thing he can carry. He came. He looked at the ground. He wrote four lines a day in a notebook that was shut under his arm and none of them had her name in it and none of them was going to.

He bought nothing. He had not bought anything from her in four years and had not bought anything for her in four years, and there was nothing in that man that wanted anything, and a person who wants nothing is the only kind of person on nine days of road that she had been able to stand next to without counting what it would cost.

## What He Said About The Paper

He turned the notebook over once in his hand and did not open it.

“A column and a holding under it,” he said. “That is what is on it. I have seen the shape of it twice in a notebook that I am not permitted to write in and I have not been told what it says and I did not ask.”

“You have seen it.”

“I have seen the shape of it,” he said. “It is a new shape. The last shape was a line and a line is a thing that gets found. This has a holding under it.”

That was all he said about it and he said it in the ordinary voice and it was not a warning and it was not advice and it was not anything at all except a man with a notebook stating the shape of a thing he had seen twice.

## The One He Said Afterwards

He was quiet for about a minute with the wind going along the track, and then he said the second of the two things, in the same voice, without weight in it and without a threat in it.

“I do not want it answered either,” he said.

She did not ask him what it meant and he did not explain it.

“There is a difference between a thing being answered and a thing being found out,” he said. “An answered thing goes up and somebody above me has to reckon with the answer, and I have never believed the reckoning would be the right one, and I have said that once already about a claim and I am saying it again about a paper because it is the same sentence.”

He did not raise his voice and he did not put anything in it.

“That is the whole of it. I have not asked you anything and I am not going to.”

## The Line, In Short Sentences

He shifted the notebook and looked west, past her, at about a quarter of a mile of nothing with a hedge on it.

“There is a line out west of here.”

“Yes.”

“It is kept,” he said. “It has been kept for longer than I have been alive and it is kept by men. Not by an office. By men who go out on it and come back on it. They are paid by the day and their families are on it and every one of them has somebody who is waiting for him to come back.”

He said it in short sentences, the way a man recites a thing he has checked.

“Nobody out here has ever needed one thing out of that. That is not a complaint. That is how it has been for nineteen years and I have stood on the end of it and I cannot tell you why it is there. I have not been told why it is there. I have met four men in nineteen years who worked it and not one of them could tell me what was at the other side of it.”

That was all of it. He stopped.

“Your brother is on it.”

He said it flatly, once, and then he shut the notebook up properly under his arm and did not say anything else about it, and she understood that he had said it because it was true and because he said things that were true and nothing else, and that he had no idea at all what it was going to do to her.

## What Was Still Pending

“And your paper.”

“My paper has been in with the district for two years,” he said, “and a paper does not become a writ because it has been in with a district for two years. A writ is not issued to a field agent. I attend and I certify and I write down what is on the ground, and on nine days of road there is one more man at a gate than there was when I came, and none of them is me.”

He said it without bitterness and without any weight at all, and she had heard the shape of it before and it had not changed since the last time and it was not going to change.

“You are still here,” she said.

“I am still here,” he said. “I have been on this side of the post for the rest of the run and I will not come inside nine feet of you again unless you put me there.”

## What She Wanted

She stood on that track at about the fourth hour and twenty minutes of the fifteenth of a month and she wanted, very badly and for about nine seconds, to ask him one question.

She wanted to ask him who had read her column and set it across from a second book, because he had been at nine feet off a track in this country for two years and he saw the carts go and he saw what came down the road and there was not one other person on nine days of road who had both of those and no reason to tell her about either.

She did not ask him. He had not asked her anything and she was not going to start, and because he did not ask her anything he kept standing there and going on being a man who did not buy anything, and that was the price of it and she had known the price of it since she was fourteen.

He went back past the post at about the speed he always went at, and she watched him to the top of the rise, and then she went west with the bar on her shoulder.

## What She Took Off That Track

A paper that was not hers. A column and a holding under it. And a holding under a column is a thing that can be asked for.

He had said the shape of it and nothing else, and he had been right about the shape of it, and she was not going to thank him for it, because he had not said it for her.

She got about two hundred yards down the track before she stopped in a gateway she knew and put her hand flat on the gate post and stood there.

A line is a thing that gets found.

A holding is a thing that can be asked for.

And the asking is done by somebody in a building, and the answer has to be given by somebody standing in a room, and she was going to have about a fortnight to work out what a person standing in a room has to do with a man who has no hands and no road and no way of ever being anywhere she is not.
