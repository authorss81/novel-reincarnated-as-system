# The Board Is For That

The thirtieth came and she was in the room with the counter at about the second hour with the bar over her shoulder and two and a half in the fold of the inside of the door frame, because it was the thirtieth and there was a thing that happened on a thirtieth that she had now watched twice from about nine feet off.

The seasonal book was open on the counter with a stone on it.

There were about seven men in the room and a stove at the back and a man behind the counter, and up the stair at the back of it there was a second stair going on out of the light, and nobody came down the first one for about four minutes after she got there.

## The Man With The Brush Came Down

He came down with his hands full of sheets and stopped on the third step from the bottom when he saw her standing there.

“All right,” he said. “You have seen it twice.”

“I have.”

“You have not seen it from this side of the counter.”

“No,” she said. “I have not.”

## What He Did On The Thirtieth

He put the sheets down on the end of the counter and came and stood at the book and put his thumb down the margin of the left-hand page and went down it the way a man goes down a page he has gone down a hundred times before.

He got to about two thirds of the way down and stopped.

He took his thumb off and put it back about half an inch higher and went down the same two lines again, and then he stood there.

“That is not right,” he said, to the book.

The man behind the counter did not look up.

“That is not right,” the man with the brush said again, and he said it in the ordinary voice, and then he said, “Come and look at this.”

She came and looked at it.

## What Was On The Page

Her name was at the top of it.

NING YAN. OF LOWWATER. And under it five months and a bit of days with dates down the margin and hours against them, and the word above the hours that said good in a hand she had never seen, and it went down the page further than she had ever been able to prove she had been anywhere.

She had eighteen counterfoils in four marks in an oilcloth against her ribs. She had had eighteen counterfoils in four marks since she was a child and they were the only proof of her life she had ever had, and they had dates she had put in herself with the flat of her second finger at a plank, eleven minutes a line.

There were five months of dates on that page and about four gaps in it, and one of them was four fingers wide where a bank had come in on her under a field.

And at the foot of the column, ruled under the thirtieth, there were two lines.

## The Two Lines

The first of them was in the counter’s hand and it was a materials account. Her name was at the left of it. The word material was under her name and the mill was against it and there was a rule under the whole of it, and there was nothing at the other end of the rule.

Under it there were figures. Stone. Clay. Loads. A box and a box and a box and eleven boxes against a yard’s line, and against each of them a day, and against the days a place, and the place was the mill.

The second of the two lines was in a finer hand than anything else on the page.

There was a mark at the head of it that was not a name. There were four figures under the mark. There was a rule underneath them.

And there was nothing at the other end of that rule either, and no date at either end of the line, and no season marked, and no thirtieth.

She put her finger on it without touching the page and she could not read it, because it is a line in a hand that is not the counter’s hand and there are four figures on it that she had already seen once on a strip about as wide as a hand in a gateway nine miles east.

## The Sentence About A Book

A book that shuts on the thirtieth has a beginning and an end on everything in it.

That is what shutting is. It is not the cord and it is not the tray and it is not the man with the paste-pot. It is that everything in the book has started somewhere and stopped somewhere, and when the thirtieth comes the man can close it because there is nothing in it that is still going.

He had put his hand flat on the open page, not on her name, about four inches below it, over the two lines.

“These two have not got an end on them,” he said, “and it is not me and it is not the counter and it is not the man who wrote either of them. I have been copying books in this building for eleven years and I have had a book not shut twice, and both times it was a book that somebody in a building had told us to put a line in.”

## What Nobody Broke

That was the whole of what happened, and it took about a minute and a half, and nothing in it was a decision.

He did not take the lines out. He could not take the lines out, because a man who copies does not decide what is in a book, and because taking them out would be a false book and he had never once in eleven years made a false book.

He did not rule a line across under them and start again on the top of the next page, which is what a man does with a thing that has gone wrong on a board.

He wrote the day at the left of the page, in the margin, against both of the lines, and he put his thumb on the bottom corner and squared the page up against the one underneath it, and then he said:

“I am going to have to ask. A book that does not shut still goes up on the first cart, and it goes as it is, and I am not going to shut it with a knot.”

## Asking

He went up the stair and there was nobody on the second stair going on out of the light, and after about four hours a man came down the whole way carrying a strip of the same stuff as the board at the works, about as wide as a hand, with four words on it in the same fine hand that was on the second of the two lines in the book.

He did not read it out. He put it on the counter, squared it with two fingers, and went back up.

The man behind the counter read it.

## Four Words

It goes as it is.

That is all of it. She looked at it from about two feet off with the man behind the counter not holding it and not turning it, and it was four words and the strip was a strip.

“A book that does not shut is still a book,” the man behind the counter said, “and a book that does not shut goes up on the first cart in the morning, and it goes as it is. That is not a rule anybody has made. That is what a book is.”

He put the strip down on the counter.

“And if it will not shut, then there is a line in it that has to be answered by somebody, and it will not be answered by me.”

## The First Cart

The tray was in the yard at the fourth hour of the first of a month with the cord in the man’s hand and the paste-pot on the bench and about nine boxes standing against the long wall in two rows.

He put the book in and he put the cord round it and tied it, and the cord went over the top of a book with a page fanned open in it about two inches from the back, and he tied it twice, and he did not put a stone under the tray to hold it, and nobody watching said one word about any of it.

She stood about nine feet off and watched a tray go up that did not shut.

## The Road

She walked out of the yard with the cart at about the sixth hour and went up the mill road on the near side of it, and about a mile along that road there is a post with a board on it and a ladder against it and a pot of paste on the ground at the foot of the ladder.

The cart stopped.

## What Comes Down The Road

The keeper came down off the ladder. He was about fifty-five and he had kept that board nine years and he had a paste pot in one hand and a rag in the other, and he got a strip out of the tray with the book open in it and stood on the ground at the foot of his own ladder looking at it.

Nine days east, a woman is walking. She is nine feet off on the near side of a cart and she cannot see a mark on it.

That is the arrangement. It was the same arrangement five months ago on a yard nine days west, with a different keeper and a different board, and he has been in nine days of road on the far side of it since before she could read.

He read it for about as long as it takes a man to lift a box. Then he got the paste up off the ground with the toe of his boot, and worked it round the pot, and put the strip on the board, and pressed it down with the flat of his hand from the top down and from the left across and from the right back, the way a man does a thing he has done every nine or ten days for nine years.

He did not ask her a question. He has never asked her a question in his life and he does not know that there is a woman standing nine feet off the near side of his cart.

## And This Is Why

He pasted the strip in because that is what the board is for.
