# The Man Who Keeps The List

The man who kept the board was not the man behind the stack.

He was about the same age and thinner and he had ink on the side of his right hand, and she found that out at about the ninth hour on the ninth of a month by standing in the gateway until somebody came out who was not the one who had come out before.

He looked at the arm and then at the box against the wall and then at her face.

“You have taken two off that board,” he said.

“Yes.”

“Twenty-three,” he said, and he did not look at the box to say it.

## The First Question

She asked it in daylight, in the ordinary voice, at the gateway, with her bar over her left shoulder, and she asked it exactly once.

“I have twenty-three in that box and I have three miles to put it in a fire. What does it cost to have it carried.”

He did not answer straight away and she understood that he was not thinking about whether to answer. He was thinking about the answer.

“The cart goes when the cart goes,” he said. “It is not the yard’s round alone. The yard’s round goes out to about eleven places and the mill is one of them, because there is stone cut at that mill and there has been since before I came, and the cart has gone there eleven times a year for as long as I have been keeping this.”

“And if it went this week.”

“It will not go this week.”

## What Is Not His

“I am not able to make it go,” he said, “and I am not able to stop you carrying it, and there is nothing at that gate that would stop you and nothing at this gate either. It is lawful work. You have taken the line down and you have finished the thing and the thing is yours and it is sitting where you left it.”

“That is what I have worked out.”

“You have worked out most of it,” he said. “You have not worked out the part where it stops at my gate.”

## Where It Stops

It stopped at the gate because a yard that lets material out on a woman’s own two arms is a yard that cannot tell anybody whose a load was if the load does not arrive.

That was the rule and he said it in the ordinary voice to a woman standing in his gateway with a bar on her shoulder, and it was not said to make a point.

“If a cart goes out of here and a thing is not on it, the yard wants a name to put against the loss,” he said. “And if a thing goes out on two arms and does not arrive, the yard has the same problem and has to write the same name on the same line. So the yard keeps an account of what leaves it and what leaves it goes on that account. And there is no account on this ground with your name on it.”

“I have a name in a book at the counter two miles west.”

“You have a name in a book that is about your days,” he said. “That is a different book and it is not mine and it is not this yard’s, and I could not take a piece of stone off this yard against a column of days in a building with a stove in it.”

## The Way There Is

He said it after about a minute, and he said it once, and he did not enjoy saying it.

“There is an office that keeps the list this board comes from.”

She had not known that. She had known for four days that there was a board and a yard and a man with a counting frame, and she had not once asked where the lines came from.

“The lines are set somewhere else and they come down and I put them up,” he said. “And the office that sets them keeps a book of what the ground owes and what has been paid on it. Everything that goes out of this yard against a name goes into that book, and it does not stop at this yard.”

“And if my name were in it.”

“Then the yard could put twenty-three against your name and deliver it and write the delivery on the same line, and if it went wrong there would be a line with your name on it that said so.”

## What It Costs

He looked at the arm. He had been looking at it for about three days by then and she was used to it in the way a person gets used to a gate.

“It is not this board,” he said. “I want to be exact, because you will remember it and I will not. This board is a board. That book is a different building and a different hand and it is not kept by anybody in this yard. It is copied, and I do not know how far, and everybody on this ground would tell you the same thing and every one of them would be right.”

“That is the cost.”

“The cost is that it is copied,” he said. “What you say here about a box of stone does not stay a thing about a box of stone. And nobody has to do anything to you for that to happen. It happens because the book is a book and it is kept up, and there is no way in it to say no.”

## The Third Thing He Said

He turned and went and got the board down off the wall and squared it on the bench under the lean-to, which was the ordinary end of his afternoon and not a thing he was doing for her.

“The board pays in what the work was made of and you take it off the wall yourself,” he said, “and the work is four days and it is however much brick and stone that job is worth, and there is not a name on it anywhere. That is what you have been doing for a month.”

“Yes.”

“The office keeps a list as well,” he said. “The one I get is the yard’s. There is another one and it is not the same, and it comes down about four times a year, and it is not put up on a wall where a woman can take it off. It is offered.”

He said the word offered and left it there, and she understood in about nine seconds what the difference was between a board and an offer, and it took her the whole walk back west to put a name on it.

## Three Miles West

She walked back with the bar on her shoulder and the light going and got about two miles and stopped in a gateway she knew and stood there.

A board is taken off a wall and it is the same board tomorrow and the day after, and it goes to whoever comes, and there is nothing on it and nobody’s name on it and that is why she can take one off it without anybody knowing.

An offer is different in every way and one of them is the important one.

An offer is made to a person.

She had been doing listed work off a board for a month and nobody had made her anything and nobody had written her name anywhere, and she had told herself twice in that month that she did not want to be hired, and she had said the word to herself as a thing other people had.

She stood in a gateway at about two miles and understood that the whole of the last month had been a thing she went and took off a wall, and that the thing on the other side of it came to a woman and had her name at the top of it, and that she was going to have to decide about it in daylight in front of a person and not on her own on a bank.

She had three days of board on it — the thirteenth, the fourteenth, the fifteenth — and the cart was not going anywhere and she had twenty-three pieces of dressed stone against a wall.

She went back east on the ninth of a month and did not sleep, and on the tenth and the eleventh she worked half a reach at Pei’s gate and carried nothing, and on the twelfth of a month she went and stood in a gateway three miles east and asked a man who kept a list whether a woman who is not on the books of that ground is allowed to ask to be put on one.

He said yes, and he said what it was called, and he said it out loud in daylight in the ordinary voice.
