# The Cart Comes Back

The twelfth of a month she worked half a reach at Pei’s gate, came out at the seventh hour and took two and a half out of the fold in the inside of the door frame, and the reach was half a reach because it had been half a reach for two months and eight holds had not moved it.

That was the part she had expected to be moved and was not.

## The Round

The round came on the thirteenth at the fourth hour and she was in the gateway at about the seventh because she had been going east every second day for a fortnight and she wanted to see it.

The cart was against the wall with its shafts down and a board across them and a stone under each wheel, and about eleven boxes standing against the long wall in two rows, and the listing man at the bench with his ink.

He wrote a line at the left of the board and then he wrote her name at the left of that line and then he wrote a place under it, and the place was the mill.

Then he got the shafts up.

She stood about nine feet off in the gateway and watched a cart that had been standing against a wall since the thirteenth of a month before this one go out of the yard at the seventh hour in the evening.

## What It Cost Him And What It Cost Her

He came out with the board in his hand and squared it up on the bench and then turned round and looked at the cart going down the made road, and he did not say anything for about as long as it takes a man to walk nine feet.

“I said in the month before last that I was going to lose it,” he said. “A man with a yard was going to want to know whether my board could be taken out from under me and the ordinary thing he would do about it was take the cart off my round until somebody put something on the line.”

“You did not lose it.”

“I did not lose it,” he said. “You did. It went out on the thirteenth of the month before last with twenty-three pieces on it against a line with nothing at the end of it and it cost me two boxes of stone and about forty miles of hauling and it went on the yard’s side of my month signed by me, and there has not been a word about it from anybody since.”

He looked at the gate and not at her.

“And now it goes again because there is a name at the left of the line and a place under the name, and a cart that goes against a name is a cart a man can answer for. So I have my round back and it is because of you, and I am telling you it is because of you because you asked me a question about the cart in the month before last and I said no and you believed me.”

She had believed him. It had been true when he said it.

## What She Said

That was all there was to say and she said it in daylight in a gateway about nine feet off a bench, in the ordinary voice.

“All right.”

That was not enough and she knew it was not enough before she had finished saying it, and there was no arrangement between them that could be finished with words, and she had been in that yard about six weeks and every single person in it had been right every single time.

“I know what it cost you,” she said. “I have known what it cost you since the round came in the month before last.”

“You have,” he said. “I have not had anybody say so and I am not going to make a thing of it.”

## The Load

Eleven boxes went on the cart in about nine minutes and were roped twice by the man behind the stack, who did not speak during any of it.

It went down the made road to the left and west, past the flat ground and the elm line, and it was going to the mill, and everything it was carrying was going into a building that had a book in it that shut on the thirtieth.

She stood in the gateway and let it go and then she went after it.

## Two Miles West With The Bar

She caught it about half a mile on, at the elm line, and she walked the last two miles on the near side of the shafts, and the driver did not ask her to and did not offer her a seat and did not say one word for about an hour and a half.

A cart that goes against a name is a cart a man can answer for. She had heard that sentence three times in twenty minutes from two people who did not know they were saying the same thing, because they had both got it from the same office nine miles east and neither of them had ever been in that building.

The load was dressed stone and clay, broken to a carrying size by men with a bar and a hopper and a whole morning to do it in, and it was going to a book, and the book was going up a road on the first cart of every month.

That was what she had done at the eighth hour on the twenty-eighth of a month in a gateway nine miles east. She had written a fire on the side of a book.

## What She Wanted On The Road

She wanted to be angry at something and there was nothing on that road to be angry at.

The cart went at the round because a book said the cart went. The yard had material and a name and a place and a line with four marks in it, and the mill had a ring in it and a book behind the ring, and both of those were true and neither of them had anything to do with her except that she had stood in a gateway and said a place out loud.

She walked two miles and a half with her hand on the near shaft and worked out the whole of it in the ordinary voice, to nobody, with the bar on her back.

She had wanted her name on a wall.

There is no version of a name on a wall that does not also go into a book.

## The Mill, At About The Eighth Hour

They put the boxes in against the far wall of the shed under boards and sacking, and the tray man with the rule and the paste-pot came out of the shed for the first time in five weeks, and he looked at the stack and looked at her and went back in.

She put her hand on the ring and it was warm and she could not have told you whether it was warm or cold, and she stood in a shed two miles east of the works with a load of dressed stone behind her and a book a hundred yards off.

What she wanted on that ground, at that hour, with the wind going at the corner of the door, was to see the page.

That was the whole of it. She wanted to see what her name looked like written down in a hand she had never seen, in a book that shut on the thirtieth, on a page that was about to be corded into a tray and read in a building with a counter and a stove in it against every other book that went up in the same month.

She had been in this country five months and she had never seen one page of it.

She did not go and look. She had asked once for a line in a book in this country and got a line, and the line had cost her the truth of her own days, and she was not going to stand in a yard at the eighth hour and ask a man to show her the page.
