# What The Paper Asks

She went east on the twenty-first of a month with the strip in her coat and stood in the gateway at the works and asked one question, and she asked it in daylight in the ordinary voice and she asked it exactly once.

“What does presented in person mean.”

The man who kept the list had the strip in his hand, because it was on his board and he had taken it down off the nails to show her a thing in it, and he turned it round twice while she watched.

“It means a person goes into a room and does not come out of it again,” he said. “It is not a paper and it is not a message. Everything else that comes off that road is a paper. You paste it on a board and you look at it and it is finished. That one has a room at the end of it and somebody has to stand in the room.”

“Which person.”

“That is not on it,” he said. “I have been looking at it for two days with a glass and there is nothing on it about which person, and there is nothing on it about where the room is, because the room is not on the strip. The room is on the copy and the copy is in the building the strip came out of.”

## What He Had Seen Of It

He put the strip down on the bench and squared it with his thumb.

“I have kept this board eleven years,” he said. “Strips come off that bundle every nine or ten days. I have had about four hundred of them and I have read the ones that are about my yard and I have not read the rest of them and neither does anybody on this ground.”

He looked at the right-hand column without touching it.

“Two of those four hundred have had two columns on them. The first one came down about two years back and it had a column on the left and nothing at all on the right, and I read the left one and the right one was blank and it went back up with the bundle. This is the first one I have had with anything in the right column.”

“So you do not know what happens to it.”

“I know what happens to everything that comes off that road,” he said. “It gets read. That is what the road is for. There is a building at the top of it with a counter in it and there is a floor over the counter, and things get read there against other things, and I have never been up and I am not going to.”

## The Line West

He turned the strip over and squared the board up on the bench and said the rest of it without being asked, which was the way of the yard and she had known that for six weeks.

“There is a road west of this gate about nine miles and there is a line on it,” he said. “Men go out on it and come back. They are paid by the day. I had an uncle on it. It has been there longer than the yard and longer than this road and longer than my board.”

He said it in four short sentences and not as a speech.

“I have never needed one thing out of it,” he said. “Nobody on this ground has. There are yards out there and buildings out there and I do not know what is at the top of any of them and it has not once mattered to me, and it has not once mattered to me because I have never had anything that anybody west of here wanted.”

Then he looked at the strip on the bench between them.

“And now there is,” he said.

## What She Said About That

That was the moment the fortnight she had been carrying turned over.

She stood in the gateway at about the ninth hour of the twenty-first of a month and had in her hand a piece of cloth about as wide as a hand that said a column of hers was kept and copied and that a holding under it was to be presented in person in a room, and the reason it existed at all was that she had gone into a yard six weeks earlier and asked to be put in a materials account.

She had wanted the name on the wall.

That was all she had wanted. She had wanted it the way a person wants to be able to prove they were somewhere, and it had been given to her in daylight by a man who was right, and six weeks later a piece of paper had come down a road with her name on it and a room at the end of it.

The only person on nine days of road who wanted anything out of that line was her, and she had wanted it out loud.

## The Night, And The Telling

She was at the shed at the mill at about the second hour on the twenty-second with the ring banked to a third and the strip in her hand and the wind at the corner of the door.

She read the third line of it four times and then she put it down on the boards with the flat of her right hand on it, four fingers and a thumb, and she told him what it was going to cost her to ask, in the ordinary voice, with the price named before the word, which was the arrangement and had never once been broken.

“I am going to ask you for something,” she said. “I am going to ask first. Whatever it is, it comes out of me, and I would rather you had it before than after.”

“The paper wants a holding presented in a room,” she said. “It does not say where. And I cannot take a room to a man who has not got a road, so the paper is going to have to be answered by me standing in it, and that is not the thing I am asking you about.”

## What It Costs Him, And What He Wants For It

She had the strip flat under her hand and the fire behind her at a third, and she gave him the price the way she had given it to him every time for four years, in the ordinary voice, before the word.

“There is one thing left in you that an office cannot put a name to,” she said, “and you have been standing behind it for nine days, since the seventeenth of a month five months back. You have been readable since then and you have not been addressable. Readable means they can find you. Addressable means they can send for you.”

Then she shut up and let him have the rest of it, and he took about a minute and said it once.

“Then it goes when I am held, and I will not be nowhere afterwards. That is the whole of the price and there is nothing else in me they can write. What you get for it is the fire: it holds without you, and you never have to be at a shed at the fourth hour again for as long as you are walking. I have wanted that since the second month and I have never had a way to buy it and I am not going to pretend to you that I am buying it for anything else.”

She had known the price. She had known it for nine days and had not wanted it and had not said no, and there was no part of her that was going to say no.

He did not say one word about her hours or her column or the eight hundred jin or the brother or the four fingers, and she noticed that, and it was the first time in four years he had wanted something that was only for her.

## What She Said

She looked at the strip under her hand for about as long as it takes a man to write four words.

“All right,” she said.

Not yes. She had said yes to a man with a brush on the seventeenth of a month and yes to a man with a bench on the twenty-sixth, and this was not either of those and she wanted him to know that it was not.

“All right. You will tell me when it has gone.”

She would not be able to tell, and she knew that before she had finished the sentence, because there had been nothing in her that told her anything since the twenty-fourth of the last month. She would find out the other way, from the fire.

She put the strip down on the boards and got up and went to bed on the boards, because that was where she slept, and she lay in the dark at the second hour of the twenty-second of a month with a ring burning nine feet away that she had put a third of a sack into on the nineteenth with her own hands.

## The Last Thing She Did That Night

She got up at about the third hour and put her palm flat on the flat stone by the door.

The stone was cold. Her hand was warm. She had put her hand on that stone four thousand times in five months and it had told her things, and for the second time in a month she put it on a cold thing and it told her that it was her own hand.

That was the whole of the night and she did not sleep the rest of it.

In the morning she worked half a reach at Pei’s gate and took two and a half out of the fold in the inside of the door frame.

The bar was on her shoulder and the strip was against her ribs in three folds, and she had said *all right* to a man in her own head at the second hour of a night in a shed two miles west of a yard, and at the fourth hour she was three miles east of it and the ring at the mill was lit, and she was not there.
