# Three Days On It

She had three days to do it in and she did it in three days, and there was not one other living thing on that ground that knew there was anything to do.

The twenty-third of a month she worked half a reach at Pei’s gate and came back and banked the ring and did not think about it.

The twenty-fourth she went east and stood at the yard gate at about the seventh hour.

## The Round

The round had come that morning.

She knew it because the cart was out on the made road with its shafts down and a board across them and about nine boxes against the wall behind it and a count going on at the open end of the building, and there were two men on the bench who had not been on the bench the three times she had been there in a month.

The listing man was at the board with his ink and he was writing, and she stood in the gateway about nine feet off and watched him do it, and it took him about a minute and a half.

He wrote the line off the thirteenth out again with the day on it and the two boxes against it and nothing at the other end, and then he ruled a line across under it and started again on the top of the next, and he did it the way a man does a thing he has done once before and would rather not do again.

She waited until he had put the chalk down.

“You are early,” he said. “It goes at the fourth hour.”

“I have seen it go.”

## What He Was Going To Do About It

He looked at the board for a while and then he looked at her arm, which he had been looking at for three weeks.

“What I am going to do about the thirteenth,” he said, “is charge it to the yard. Not to you. The yard put out a cart on a day the yard had not put it out and the yard is short two boxes of stone and forty miles of hauling, and that is the yard’s account and it goes in on the yard’s side on the last line and it is signed for by a man whose name is on the end of this board.”

He put his thumb on the board where there was a mark and no writing.

“It is not you. You are not on here. There is nothing on here with your name on it and there is not going to be.”

“All right.”

“I want to say the other half of it,” he said, “because you came and asked me for it and I would rather you had it from me than work it out.”

## The Other Half

“The hole is on my board,” he said. “It is the first in eleven years. When I have to put it in a month and put my mark on the end of it I am going to have to write that a box went out on a cart that was not going out, and I am not going to be able to write a name against it, because there is no name on here for it to be.”

He was not saying it to be fair to her. He was saying it because she had asked and because he was a man who gave an answer all the way down.

“That is going to be asked about,” he said. “Not by anybody who is against you. By a man with a yard who wants to know whether his board can be taken out from under him. And I am going to have to say I cannot say, and then he is going to do the ordinary thing, which is take the cart off his own round until somebody puts something on the line.”

He squared the board up on the bench.

“I have had this cart every round for eleven years and I am going to lose it for a while. I am not saying that to put it on you. I am saying it because you asked me and I would rather you had it all.”

## What She Said To That

She stood in the gateway with the bar on her shoulder and looked at about nine feet of flat ground and a cart with its shafts down and did not say anything for about as long as it takes for a man to lift a box.

Then she said: “I am sorry about the cart.”

And then, because he was right and she had been in that yard about three weeks and everybody in it had been right every single time:

“Do not lose it on account of me.”

That was all. There was no arrangement between them that could be finished with the words and she had been in that yard about three weeks and she knew that.

## What She Weighed For Two Days

She weighed it on the flat at about the second hour of two nights with the fire banked down and the shed warm for as far as a ring makes a shed warm, and there was nobody in it to weigh it with.

What she would get.

A price for work that did not fall every time her body got stronger. A listing that came to a person instead of a board that anybody could take off. Material paid to her own account that would still be hers on ground three miles east of a yard, or nine, or on the other side of a country she had not walked to yet.

That last one was the whole of it and she got it at about nine minutes past the second hour on the twenty-fifth and it was the reason and it was not a noble one.

Half a reach at five coppers a reach is two and a half a day, and it has been the price of her for four years, and it goes down every time she takes something and it will go down again for whatever she takes next and again for whatever she takes after that.

She was eighteen years old and she had five stages in her and there was a furnace of her own and a fire in a shed and a day was going to be worth less at the end of the year than it was at the beginning, every year, for ever, and that was not the country doing it to her. That was arithmetic.

## What It Costs

A second office. Not the counter, not the floor above the counter, not the man with the brush who found her name off a column and never asked her for it.

Another one, nine miles east, in a hand she had never seen, in a book that is copied.

She had one now. She had had one since the fourth hour of the twenty-third of the last month and it was the only thing in the world she had ever been put in by anybody and she had gone into a room and said three words and had got it, and it had cost her the truth of her own days and there was no way of giving it back.

And this one would be entered against material. It would not be a column of days and it would not have a rate on it and it would not be a wage and she would not be hired by it.

And it would be copied, and a materials book gets copied further than a day-book, because material goes everywhere the ground goes and a day only goes where somebody counts days.

And it cannot be taken back. Not by her, not by anybody, not by walking a season’s road in the other direction.

## The Plain Part

That is the price. It is not a figure and it is not on her body.

If she says yes on the twenty-sixth, then there will be two books on nine days of road with a person in them that is the same person, and they will not be copied to the same place and she will never see either of them, and from that hour there will not be one hour of the rest of her life that she gets to keep.

And the only person she can be angry at is a man with ink on the side of his hand who kept a board for eleven years and told her the truth twice and was right both times and is going to lose his cart over a week of her wall.

## The Twenty-Sixth

She worked half a reach at Pei’s gate on the twenty-sixth of a month and took two and a half out of the fold in the inside of the door frame and went east at about the seventh hour with the bar over her left shoulder.

She had not written anything down and she had not asked anybody and nobody had persuaded her and nothing in about three weeks had been argued at her, and that was not a thing she had arranged. It was a country where every single person she had met had done their own work correctly and had told her the price of it in the ordinary voice and had gone back to it.

She came up the made road about nine minutes before the eighth hour and the yard gate was open and lit and the board was up on the wall inside it with nothing on it, and the man who kept the list was standing at the bench with his hands on it.

She stopped about nine feet off and she did not go any closer and she said her name out loud, in daylight, at a gate, the way she had said it at every trestle she had ever stood at.

He did not ask her to say it a second time.
