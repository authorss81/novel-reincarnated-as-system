# Past The Last Farm

The ground east of the mill was flat and farmed and had a house on it with a woman in the doorway, and at about the fourth hour of the fourteenth she was standing at a gate with a bar over her shoulder looking at a drain that had stopped taking water.

It was a stone drain. It ran along the bottom of a bank about as deep as a man’s chest and it was silted nearly to the top of the stones, and the field above it had gone the colour that ground goes when water has been standing on it.

There was no hiring and there had never been any hiring and she had not been looking for any.

She found that out on the road and it took her about a mile.

There was no office to go into. There was no book and no plank and no line of anybody’s wage, and no season, and the man at the counter two miles and four hundred yards behind her had told her on the twelfth that he wrote one line a month about his room and no more.

What there was, half a mile east of a mill, was a gate, and a drain that had stopped, and a man who wanted it open.

## Seven

He was about fifty. He had a house behind him with washing up and a boy of nine at the edge of it doing nothing he had been told not to do from a distance of about a foot.

“Off that bank, by the yard,” she said. “What is a yard.”

“A reach,” he said, and then he said it again in the way of a man who has said a thing to a stranger and wants the stranger to have the number as well as the word. “Seven coppers a reach.”

He said it once, out loud, in the ordinary voice, standing at his own gate at the fourth hour of the fourteenth of a month, and then he went and got a bar out of the shed and came back and gave it to her and went to stand on the bank where he could see the whole of it.

That was the whole of the hiring and it took about as long as the gate took to open.

Seven coppers a reach was not a wage. It was not five coppers a day for a day’s work at a reach a woman had spent five months learning to work. It was a unit of the thing done, and the thing done was counted, and a man who counts a thing has to have said the number out loud so that the person doing it knows what the number is.

She stood at the top of the bank and looked at the reach of drain below her and understood that her body had a price again for the first time since she had crossed, and that the price was a yard and not a day, and that she had no idea which of the two was her.

## The First Yard

She went down into it at the sixth hour and she worked it out herself.

That was the part she would think about afterwards. A man stood on a bank four feet above her and watched a field and did not watch her, and a woman went down into eight feet of wet stone with a bar and four fingers and an arm at half and got a reach of drain open in a day.

Four counts of breath everywhere.

Nine holds out of eleven.

Not one of the things she had lost stopped her doing it.

She got the silt out in loads, up onto the bank edge, and scraped the bottom of the stones with the flat of the bar until the water moved and then moved with a sound.

By the time the light was off the field she had a reach open and the water going down it in a proper channel, and she came up out of the cut at the top of the bank with her left arm shaking.

She put her right hand flat on the gate to stand still for a moment.

The man came over the top of the bank and looked down the run of it and then at her.

“There is more of it,” he said.

“I know how much of it there is.”

“You will not take it on tomorrow. It is not worth taking on.”

“All right.”

She was paid at the gate at the end of the day in her own hand and she did not count it standing there and she did not count it on the road, and it was the first money she had had since the twenty-ninth of a month five months ago and it came out of a hand of a man at a gate and not out of a plank.

There was no stub. There was no name written anywhere. There was no booking and no term and no season and no way at all of going back to that gate in a year and asking whether she had been there.

That was not employment and she did not want it to be.

## The Fifteenth

She was back at the bank at the fourth hour on the fifteenth and had most of a second reach open by the light when the team came down the road.

Three of them and a loaded cart, going east, and the one at the head of it was twenty years old and had a whinny whip folded twice and going past at the speed of a loaded cart goes past.

Wei Ran did not stop the cart.

She slowed it about as much as a cart slows when a person is standing at a bank with a bar in her hand, and she looked across, and she took about as long to work out who was in the cut as a person takes to look at the sun.

“You are on Pei’s ground,” she said.

“Off his field. At the gate.”

“Seven a yard on that gate. It is what the other gate pays and it is not worth the walk.”

“I have walked it.”

She looked at the drain for about two seconds and then at her own right arm and then at nothing in particular, in the way of a woman doing arithmetic she has already done.

“The ground turns to soup about two hundred yards past that hedge and it will hold a wheel about half a season,” she said. “Every year. Not one year. Every year.”

“Right.”

“There is a cut in it about a mile on where a cart went in at the second hour in the wet last year and they got it out again and the one who drove it did not walk off the road for a month.”

That was a road, a rate and an opinion, and she gave all three of them in about as many sentences as it takes a person to say them, and she did not give anything else.

She did not offer to take her anywhere. She did not offer the cart, or the team, or a word to anybody, or a face to anybody. She said what she had said and she went east at the speed she had been going, and the three of them went with her, and the road under the wheels was the road she had walked in on.

## What She Felt About It

She stood at the top of the bank after the cart was out of sight and found that she was ashamed.

Not of the work. She had been ashamed of exactly one thing in five months and it was not that.

She was ashamed of how fast she had gone to find out what a reach of drain cost.

She had walked a mile to find out. She had asked a man at his own gate at the fourth hour of his own afternoon for a number, and she had got it in under a sentence, and she had taken the bar he gave her and gone down into his bank without any of the ordinary business of a person who does not know what a thing is worth until somebody tells her.

She had been five months without a number of any kind and she had gone to a gate and asked for one the way a hungry person asks for bread.

It was not dignity that had kept her on the yard for five months. It was that nobody had offered her a gate.

She went back down into the cut at about the seventh hour and worked until the light was off the field, and the water went down the reach she had opened and kept going, out through the bottom of the ground where a person cannot follow it.

On the fifteenth she had opened a yard of a drain she had found a mile from anything, in a country that had not heard of her, at a rate a man had said out loud once.

She was going to be back at that gate on the sixteenth, and the bar was going with her, and the gate would be open.

In the shed at the mill, on the boards, with the light off the wheel:

“You got a number for your body out loud today,” he said. “At a gate. In front of a man with a house.”
