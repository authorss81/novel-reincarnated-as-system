# The Block In The Ditch

She got nine feet of the old footing out on the first day and about four feet of the new one in, and she found the course at about a foot down where it had stopped being stone and started being clay, which is where a wall that is going to go goes from.

A retaining wall is not a wall. It is a foot, and a wall on top of the foot, and the foot is under the ditch and the ditch is where the water comes out on, and about twenty feet of the foot was gone and about nine feet of the wall above it were standing on nothing and doing it very well.

## The Second Day

The block was a dressed one, about as long as her arm and as thick as her two hands, and it came out of the foot whole, and she had it up off the clay and turned on its edge and was walking it round to the stack when her right hand opened.

Not the arm. The hand.

Four fingers and a thumb on a wet dressed block at the edge of a nine-foot ditch, and the hand opened the way it opens about a third of the time, and the block came about a foot and a half and met the bar.

It did not hit her. It hit the bar, side on, at the end where the end had a lip on it from a day in a drain a month ago, and the sound went out across that flat ground about as far as the mill.

She had heard stone split once in her life, in a hall with no roof, and this was the same sound and about half the size.

## About Four Men

There were four men on that ground within two hundred yards and every one of them stopped, and not one of them made a sound.

That was the part she had not been ready for and she stood in the ditch with her hand open and her back to four men and about two hundred yards of flat ground and nothing at all coming across it.

One of them picked his bar up off the ground where he had put it down. Another one went back to the foot and started taking clay out of the course with the flat of his own bar, on the other side of the ditch, without hurrying about it and without saying anything.

The third one looked at the bar and then at her hand and then at the bar.

Then he went back to work, and the fourth one had not stopped at all.

## Nine Minutes On The Sill

She got out of the ditch at about nine minutes past and stood on the sill with the bar across her shoulder and looked at what was left of it on the ground.

About a third of a block, in four or five pieces, and one piece with a corner off it that was the size of a man’s hand, and the rest of it small enough that a man would sweep it up with a broom and think nothing more about it.

A dressed block does not mend. There is no way of putting one back together and there is no man in four miles who will take a broken block into a head and a course and build with it, because a wall that has one bad stone in the bottom course is a wall that will be at the wrong angle in nine years.

It was out of the yard’s stock. That was all it was.

## The Rest Of It

She was aware, standing on that sill, that she had about two and a half days of work left in her and that the footing was about three and a half, and that the number she had to hit was four days and there was nothing in it about a block.

She got back down in at about the third hour of the second day and worked until the light went, and she came up on the third and the fourth and finished the course at about the seventh hour of the fourth, which was the twenty-second, with the bar’s end and her right knee and a wedge she made out of a piece of the broken block.

She laid the last of the new foot at about two feet below where the old one was and got the line right, because she had not had to guess at a line in four years and she was not going to start on the last four feet of a job that was going to be looked at.

## What It Cost

About a fifth of a box.

That is what a dressed block is out of two boxes and the man at the yard had a number for it without being asked, standing in the gateway on the twenty-second at about the eighth hour with the counting frame and the tally board and no particular expression at all.

“Twenty-three,” he said. “You are owed twenty-three and not twenty-five.”

“You said four days.”

“You took four days,” he said. “The block is the yard’s and not yours, because you are not hired here and nothing you have done on this ground has ever been on the yard’s account.”

## The Thing She Wanted

That was the fifth time in five months that a man on that ground had been right about something, and she took the twenty-three and put it in the front of her coat and stood in the gateway and heard it land the way a thing lands when it does not come out of your own pocket.

He had not taken the two blocks out of her pay.

He was right to. She was not hired. There was no line in any book on that ground with her name on it and a day against her name and a thing owed at the end of it, and the yard’s loss was the yard’s loss because the yard had chosen to put work on a board and let whoever came clear it, and a broken block in a ditch between two farms is what happens.

She had wanted to owe something.

That was all. She had wanted, for about four minutes in a gateway, to be a woman with two blocks of a wall of her own against a yard that could come and take them off her, and instead she was a woman with twenty-three in her hand and a bar over her shoulder and no account anywhere on nine days of road that anybody could open and see her in.

## At The Seventh Hour

The work was closed at about the seventh hour of the twenty-second of a month and she stood at the top of the ditch in the last of the light with about five feet of new foot and nine feet of new wall on it and a course that was true.

There were two boxes’ worth of brick and clay and stone in the yard behind her, twenty-three of the twenty-five, and she had not been paid in anything at all yet, because the yard paid in what was stacked and the stacking was done at the open end of the building and the yard did not stack for a woman with no line on the yard’s own board.

And she knew, standing on that sill, exactly what was going to come up through her at the fourth hour of the next day, and that it would be twice the size of the last one, because the last one had come off a head and an apron at a lane crossing and this one had come off twenty feet of a retaining wall.

She had bought two boxes of fire with it.

She had a ring at the mill that had been out for six days and she had three coppers and about two days of bread in her hand, and she had not been let off anything at all.
