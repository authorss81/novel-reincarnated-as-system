# The Lines On The Wall

The board was on the wall inside the gate at the works and she read it at about the seventh hour on the eleventh of a month with the bar across her knees and the gateway behind her still wet through from the night.

Four lines.

That was all of it. Four lines and five more to come down out of the same place over the next two months, and the man who kept it had given her the whole of that arithmetic in a gateway about a month before, in about a minute and a half, and she had written none of it down because there is nowhere on nine days of road to write a thing down.

Six lines to take and five lines to come. Eleven. And then the yard goes quiet until the season turns, and the season was somewhere past a date she had not been told and could not ask for, because the man who kept the board did not have the date and had never had it.

## The Morning Before

She had been at Pei’s gate at the seventh hour for five months and a half and had never once gone in without working out first what the day was going to be worth.

A third of a reach was one and a half coppers and a third of a sack of clay was three coppers, and that was six days of the gate for one load of clay into the ring, and the ring had been burning by itself for eleven days and had not needed her to walk to it at the fourth hour for any of them.

She went in at the seventh and came out about four feet down with about nine feet of drain opened and it took her until about the ninth hour to get what she used to get by the eighth, and the count went to five about nine feet in and she worked the rest of it at five.

Pei came out at the eighth with his own bar and his boy on the fence with a stick and did not say one word about it, and he had never once said one word about it.

She took one and a half out of the fold in the inside of the door frame and the fold had been thinner since the eighteenth of a month and was thinner again since the twenty-eighth.

## The Name Space

There was a ruled space at the left of the board about two fingers wide, and every line ran into it, and in that space there was one name written in her own hand when she could get a hand to it, and a word under it that said material, and a place against that said the mill.

That was hers and it had been hers since the twenty-sixth of a month three months back and it was the reason a line she takes off that board leaves the yard against a mill instead of against nothing.

She read it twice, which was not a thing she did, and then she took her eyes off it.

## The Man Behind The Stack

He was carrying a box of dressed stone out to the cart with the bar on his shoulder and he put the box down on the cart bed and squared it against the side before he looked at her.

“There is a line out west of this yard,” he said. “About nine miles. It is walked and it is kept and there are men on it and they are paid by the day and their families are on it. Nobody east of here has ever needed one thing out of it.”

He picked the bar up off the cart and leaned it against the side.

“That is all I have got about it and I have never had reason to look at it.”

Then he went back in behind the stack, and that was the whole of it, four sentences in the ordinary voice, and he had not once looked at the board.

## The Fourth Line

Footing at the far gully, two days, one box. A cut about four feet deep with a hard floor under it and about eleven feet of bank on the low side, and the bank was stone and not clay, and the stone at the top of it was the heavy stone they had cut off the top of somebody’s field a year before and had never wanted.

She read the line twice and put her finger under the word that said footing and left it there.

That word was the only word on that board that weighed anything.

Everything else on it was length. A ditch, a cut, a length of bank, a head, a gully — a thing is measured by how far you have to go to do it and not by what comes out of it. Footing is the one the men at the yard do not take, because footing is the bank you put back, and the bank at the far gully is not clay.

She took the line off the nails and folded it in two and put it inside her coat.

## What She Wanted And What There Was

She stood in the gateway for about two minutes after he had gone back in and worked out the whole of the next ten weeks standing up, because that was the only place on nine days of road where anybody had ever done that arithmetic out loud and got an answer.

Ten weeks of gate at a third of a reach is seventy coppers. Bread at two a day is a hundred and forty. And in about ten weeks the gate and the board between them are going to be worth less than half of what she eats.

She wanted a line on that board her hand could not do since the fifth day of a listing in a gateway about seven weeks back, and she had wanted it the way a person wants a door that is locked on purpose, which is to say she wanted the wanting and not the getting.

There was no such line. The board did not know what her hand was. It was a board with a word on it about taking it down and a word on it about putting it back, and there were nine men in that yard who would take the next line off the wall beside her and not one of them would look at her arm.

And in about two months the board would be bare and there would be nothing on it for her or for anybody, and the only money on that ground would be a gate that paid by the reach, and the reach was a third.

## What She Took To The Shed

She walked back west in the last of the light with the bar over her shoulder and the line inside her coat and the gate three miles off in the other direction.

A third of a reach is one and a half coppers and a third of a sack of clay is three coppers and the ring in the shed at the mill was lit and had been lit for eleven days by itself, and she had been buying the clay with her own hand out of a reach she could no longer open the whole of, and in four days there would be nothing left in the corner of that shed but the shape of what she had carried in.

She wanted one thing out of the two months, and she told herself it in the ordinary voice at the fourth hour with her palm flat on the flat stone by the door.

“I am going to do the footing at the far gully,” she said, “and it is two days on the board and it is going to take me every one of the hours I have between a gate and this shed, and it is the last thing anybody on nine days of road is going to pay me by the weight of.”

The stone was cold under her palm and her hand was warm and the two of them told her nothing at all.
