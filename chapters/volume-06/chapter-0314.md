# Four Nails

The third of a month came up clear behind her and she turned back west along the track with the bar over her left shoulder, because there was nothing east of the last hedge and two and a half coppers a day west of it.

She was at Pei’s bank at the fifth hour and out of it at the seventh with half a reach open and running and the money in the front of her coat.

Pei gave her the bar out of his shed the way he gave it to her every morning, and the boy was on the fence with a stick, and neither of them said anything that was not about a field.

## The Arithmetic

She did the sum at the gate on the way out, sitting on the stone kerb with the bar across her knees, because she had four years of a habit that had not switched off when the thing it was counting had stopped existing.

Two and a half a day. Nine for a charge.

Four days of work for it.

She had done that sum eleven times since the twenty-sixth of the last month and it came out the same every time, and on the eleventh she noticed what the sum was actually for, which was not the ring.

It was counting to something.

For four years the count had run to one of three days, and the seventh and the seventeenth and the twenty-seventh had each meant a walk of two miles and four hundred yards and a line of a man’s other days, and the shape of her head had been built out of that and had held her upright in more ways than one.

Now the count ran to four days of work to a charge, and the four days meant nothing at all except that the fire in a corner of a shed two miles west would be cold on the fifth.

That is the whole of what was left of it. The shape survived. The thing inside the shape did not.

She had been marking days in the inside of her head for four years and she was going to go on doing it, and there was not one person on that ground who would ever know that she had.

## The Wall

She went east at about the second hour with nothing to carry and nothing in mind, and at the third hour the track turned off the flat onto a made road, and at the fourth hour there was a wall.

It was mortared and about as high as her chest and it ran about four hundred yards each way from a gate made of two piers and a beam, and inside it was a yard and a long low building with an open end, and eight or nine men at the open end in the ordinary way of men who have done the same thing ten thousand times.

There were boxes.

That was the thing that stopped her on the road, and it was not the men and it was not the wall.

There were about nine wooden boxes standing against the long wall in two rows, and a tenth open against the open end of the building with brick and clay broken down into it in pieces about the size of two fists.

A thing in a size one arm could carry, broken down by men who had a bar and a hopper and a whole morning to do it in, and not broken down by one woman alone on a flat stone at the dark with a bar’s end and eleven minutes a piece.

She stood in the road a long way off and counted them.

## The Board

There was a board on the wall inside the gate, made of the same stuff as the keeper’s board at the post two miles behind her, and there was writing on it, and she stood in front of it and read it because it was at eye height and there was nothing a person can do about a thing that is put up at eye height.

It said, in the ordinary hand of a man who paints a board once and leaves it:

“Listed work. Paid in what the work was made of. No hire. No season. No name on the line. Take it down and it is yours to finish. Put it back and it waits for the next one.”

And under that, in a smaller hand, one line:

“Low head and apron at the lane crossing. Four days. One box.”

She read it twice.

The second time she read *no hire*, and the word had been in her own head for five months as a thing that other people had and she had not, and she had said it once, to herself, as a sentence she was content with.

## Taking It Down

She reached up and put the flat of her second finger under the corner of the line and lifted it off the nails, and the flat of that finger was the longest it had ever been and the board came away in one piece with four nails in it and no difficulty at all.

The board had her name on it nowhere. The line had her name on it nowhere.

There is a difference between those two things and she stood in a gateway on the third of a month and had it in her hand as a piece of board with four nails in it, and it is the first document of any kind that has ever had nothing written on it anywhere and belonged to her.

She turned it over twice and put it inside her coat against the oilcloth, and the whole of what she had ever been able to prove about herself was eighteen counterfoils in four marks against her ribs and a piece of board with four nails in it.

## The Man At The Gate

He was about fifty and he had been sorting bricks since before she was born and he came out from behind the stack when she had been standing there long enough to be worth coming out to.

He looked at the coat where the board was and then at her arm and then at the road.

“Four days,” he said. “It is a low head and an apron and the ditch is deep on the far side. If you do not finish it in four days you can put it back on and the next one can have it.”

“I will finish it in four days.”

“You might not. It is a listed one and it does not care.”

That was all he said about that, and then he asked her the only other thing there was.

“When do you want the box.”

## The Round

She had not thought about the box. That was the whole of what the question did to her, standing in a gateway at about the fifth hour on the third of a month with a piece of board inside her coat.

She had thought about earning the box. She had not once thought about the box going anywhere.

“The cart,” she said. “When does the cart go.”

“The cart goes when the cart goes,” he said. “It went out before you came here. The next one goes when the yard is full or when the round comes, and the round is about three weeks, and I would not build anything on it.”

“And if I carry it myself.”

He looked at her arm for a second time, which he had done twice already without any harm in it.

“Four fingers and one arm that is open half the time,” he said. “And it is three miles to where the fire is.”

“Yes.”

“I am not going to tell a woman she cannot do a thing she has not tried,” he said. “There is a bar at the end of the shed if you want it. The lane crossing is two miles east and the ground on the far side of that ditch is the ground that is gone. Take the bar and take your time about it.”

Then he went back behind the stack and did not come out again.

## What She Carried West

She went west at about the sixth hour with the bar over her left shoulder and a piece of board with four nails in it inside her coat and four days of work in front of her and one box behind her at a yard, and it was sitting against a wall in pieces about the size of two fists.

It was four days of work, and she had done four days of work in five months that nothing had ever come back to her from, and it had cost her an afternoon in a gateway and four nails.

And it was also three miles from the only thing in the world that had ever answered her, and she had a ring at the mill with a third of a charge in it that had not been hot since the twenty-first of the last month.

She had been working two and a half a day at a gate a man and a boy lived behind, and she had a piece of board inside her coat, and she walked the three miles back past a wheel that had not turned since before she came and did not look at it.
