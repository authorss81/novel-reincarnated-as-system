# The Ring Went Out

She did not light it on the eleventh and she did not light it on the twelfth, and on the thirteenth she went to the material shed past the mill with what was in her hand and asked for a third of a sack.

Three coppers.

The man behind the stack said it the way he said everything, without any hurry and without any unkindness, and he put a third of a sack out on the flat stone by the door because she had asked for it and because that is what a shed does.

She had two and a half.

She did the sum on the flat stone with her hand flat on the sack, and the sum was the sum she had been doing for about nine days and it had not changed and it was not going to.

## What A Third Is

A third of a sack is three coppers and it is about what she could put under one arm and lift off the ground without putting her shoulder through it, and it is a third of a charge.

Two and a half a day at a gate. Three coppers every four days. Bread, which she did not price and which came out of the same hand.

That is the ring.

Not nine. Nine is a full charge and a full charge is a thing she had bought once in five months. Three coppers every four days is the ring, and it is what a furnace of her own has actually cost her every fourth day since the eighth of the first month, and it comes out of the gate.

And the ring goes out on the fifth day anyway.

It has gone out on the fifth day every time. She had lit it six times in five months and it had been hot for about a day and a half of each of those six times and cold for the other two and a half, and she had never once written that down anywhere because there was nothing to write it in.

A thing that answers her, out of two and a half a day, half of the time.

## The Two Days

On the twelfth she worked half a reach at Pei’s gate and came back to a shed with a cold ring in the corner of it and slept on the boards with her coat on.

On the thirteenth she went back to that shed at the eighth hour with what was in her hand, which was two and a half, and the sack was three, and she stood on the flat stone by the door with her hand flat on the sacking and did not buy it.

The man behind the stack did not say anything about that either. He had put the sack out because she had asked for it and he went on with what he was doing, and she took two and a half coppers back east and worked half a reach at a gate for it.

That was the whole of the twelfth and the thirteenth and it was the arrangement and she was not going to call it anything else.

She was out on flat ground at about the second hour of the fourteenth with nothing lit anywhere within three miles and the cold coming up through her boots, and she had been out on flat ground for a night and a half of it and had not been frightened once and had not slept about four hours of it.

## What She Felt About It

It was not anger. She had had the anger on the twenty-fourth of the last month at a gate and it had been the flat kind and there was not one person on that ground she could have spent it on.

What she felt on the fourteenth of a month at about the second hour, on flat ground three miles east of a mill, was that she had been *spent*.

There was a word for it in a yard four hundred yards past the post nine days of road behind her and it had been on a list she had never seen and it had been four years ago, and she had been on that list and had got off it and had got on another one in its place, and the new one had a column with her name at the top of it and a word above the hours that said she was good.

She had laid a low head and an apron at a lane crossing with one hand and it had been worth a line in a record an office reads, and the line had cost her the only fire she had.

There was nobody on that ground to be cross with about it and there was not going to be one, and she had gone through the whole of the arrangement in her own head on the fourteenth, in the ordinary voice, out loud, to nobody, and it came out at about four sentences and it was the same four sentences every time.

## Four Sentences

She clears a listed thing.

A listed thing, when it is finished, settles something out of the ground it was done on, and the settling is worth something, and what it is worth comes out of her.

The fire and the settling are the same ground.

So every piece of fire she has ever got into that ring she got by spending herself, and she was doing it about every fourth day, and she could not stop it by working harder at the gate because the gate is not listed and a gate does not settle anything at all.

That is not a discovery. That is the arrangement, worked out on flat ground at the second hour, and it took about nine minutes and she had known most of it since the eleventh.

What she had not had before the fourteenth was the other half of it.

## The Other Half

The board was at the yard with three lines on it and she stood in front of it at the fourth hour on the fourteenth with her arm hanging and read them.

The low head and the apron at the lane crossing had gone off it on the seventh and had not gone back.

Under that there were two more, and one of them said:

FOOT OF RETAINING WALL GONE UNDER THE DITCH BETWEEN THE TWO FARMS. FOUR DAYS. TWO BOXES.

Four days. Two boxes.

She read it the way she had read the first one, and she read *no hire* and *no name on the line* and she took it down off the nails with the flat of a second finger and put it inside her coat against a piece of board with four nails in it.

Two boxes is about eight days of fire.

It would also cost her what the first one cost, at about twice the size, because it was twice the work and the settling comes off the size of the thing that was listed and not off the time she put into it.

She stood in the gateway with the line in her coat and worked out that she would be buying eight days of fire with one more week of herself, and that the fire she bought with it would arrive in a yard three miles from the thing it was for, and that the yard’s cart would not go for about three weeks.

And she took it anyway, and she went east at about the fifth hour to look at a ditch.

## Three Coppers And Two And A Half

That is what a third of a sack costs and she had two and a half of it, and it is the whole of the arithmetic of those ten days in one line.

Three coppers is a day and a fifth. A day and a fifth is four days at two and a half.

And four days at two and a half is the ring, going out on the fifth, every time, for five months, with a fire in the corner of a shed behind a third of a sack that had gone out on the seventh of the last month.

She had been standing at a material shed about a mile east of that shed, with a gate half a mile the other way and a yard three miles the other way again, and the distance between the money and the fire was the whole of the ground she had walked on since the seventh of the last month.

She had five at the seventh hour on the thirteenth and enough again by the fifteenth, and she stood at a gate on the fourteenth with her hand shut over them and understood that she was two days away from being able to afford a third of a charge, and that everything else on that ground was going to go on costing exactly what it had always cost.

Five months of a season had taught her the first half of this. The works three miles east had taught her the second half on the third of a month, standing in a gateway, looking at boxes with brick and clay already broken into them in a size one arm could carry.
