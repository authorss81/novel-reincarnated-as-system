# Nothing Came Down The Road

She got up at the fourth hour on the twenty-eighth of a month out of the habit of a woman who had been doing it for five months, and she was in the yard with the bar in her left hand in the dark, and the ring was up against the brick in the corner of the shed with no one in it and no one near it.

She stood in the middle of that yard and let the cold come off the flat and go through her coat.

There were nine days of road between her and a room she could not name, and on that ground the doors of it shut at some hour of that day on people she had never met.

## What She Did All Day

She went in at the seventh hour and opened the drain on the low side of Pei’s field and took a fifth of a reach out of it and got one copper out of the fold in the inside of the door frame, and she did it four feet deep with the bar in one hand and the arm that was open doing nothing on the out-side, and the ground came up to the lip of the bank every time.

At the eighth hour she stood at the gate post and did the sum.

It was the twenty-eighth.

Then she carried the bar back and put the clay in the ring and went out to the far side of the elms.

## What She Did With The Rest Of The Day

She sat down on the bank where the wind comes over and did not go east.

She sat there for about an hour and a half with her hands open on her knees, four fingers and a thumb on one of them, and she did not think about the twenty-eighth at all, because there was nothing to think about it with, and the hour and a half went the way an hour and a half goes when there is nothing in it.

Then she got up and went and looked at the road.

## The Nothing

Nothing came down the mill road. That was not a thing anybody could see from a bank four feet high on the far side of the elms, and she went and looked at about the eighth hour in the evening anyway, and the made road was there going west with nothing on it, and there had been nothing on it at any hour of that day.

That was the whole of what the twenty-eighth was.

A date on a strip about as wide as a hand, in a hand she could not read, in a room nine days of road east of a post, and not one person in the world had to tell her anything about it, and nothing had come down a road to tell her anything about it, and she was the only person on nine days of road for whom that day had been anything at all.

## The Two Days After

The twenty-ninth and the thirtieth were ordinary and she made them ordinary on purpose.

She went in at the seventh and worked a fifth of a reach and got one copper and came out at the ninth and carried the bar three miles west and put clay in the ring and went to the works and stood in the gateway and read a board with two lines on it and went east and read it again at the end of the day in case one of the men had put something back.

There was a thing she had started doing a week before, which was to walk three miles to look at a board, and she had never once noticed herself doing it until the second of those two days, and she noticed it on the second, and she did not stop it, because a woman with no wages and a board three weeks from bare has a right to walk three miles.

She had a right to walk nine days of road. She had worked that out eight days ago on a bank and had not liked it.

## What She Worked Out Sitting On A Bank

If she had been in that room she would have known by the eighth hour.

If she had not been in that room she would have found out in about six weeks, because a strip comes down off a bundle every nine or ten days and one of them in three carries words at the bottom of it, and it would take two of those to say anything, and in six weeks there would not be anything left to say it about.

And if she went and stood at the bottom of that road in the morning to watch, it would be the same nothing she had got tonight, because a road does not report on a room.

She had been carrying a date for four weeks and she had thought, somewhere without ever having put it in words, that carrying it was the same as being on time.

It was not the same at all. She was on time in exactly one place, which was on this ground, and the date was not about this ground.

She got up at the fourth hour out of the habit of a woman who had been doing it for five months and a half, and there was a fire in the corner of the shed that had been lit by itself for a fortnight, and she lay on the boards for about twenty minutes looking at it and then got up and went out into the dark in a coat over nothing.

That was the twenty-eighth, and she had wanted the day to be something, and it turned out to be the same shape as every other day on nine days of road with one copper in the fold in the inside of a door frame.

## The Twenty-Ninth And The Thirtieth

She worked the gate on the twenty-ninth and the thirtieth at a fifth of a reach and one copper a day, and the thirtieth came and went, and the book in the mill yard did not shut on her account and did not shut because of her either, and she was in the gateway at the works on the thirtieth afternoon and there were two lines left on the board.

The cart went up the mill road on the first at the sixth hour with a cord round the tray and a book in it that had a page fanned open about two inches from the back, and she stood in the yard and watched it get past the elms, and that was the sixth tray she had been in.

## The Want

She wanted somebody to come and tell her.

That was all of it. Not the answer — the telling.

She was eighteen years old and she had gone out of a bound nine days behind her with nothing, and she had built five months of a life out of a gate and a board and a furnace that lit itself. On the twenty-ninth of a month she found out that there is a kind of thing you can be the only person alive who cares about, and that being the only person alive who cares about it does not make it matter.

And underneath that, on the boards, at the ninth hour, there was a second want and it was a worse one and she let it up.

She wanted to be wrong about the seventh stage. She wanted to go out past the last hedge at the fourth hour of the twenty-third with the bar on the grass and be told, by the same thing that said it in the far-side house form, that it had not taken the bar.

There was not one person on that ground who could say it and there was nothing to say it with, and she had asked for the stage herself on open ground in daylight at her own order, and she lay there on the boards with the ring burning nine feet off her and understood that the whole of what she had bought in four years was the right to stand on a piece of ground and be the one who decides.

She did not use it on anything else for a week and a half.

Twenty-three days to the next one.
