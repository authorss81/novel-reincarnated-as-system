# A Book Under A Ladder

The twenty-eighth of a month came and went and nothing came down the mill road, and she worked the gate at a fifth of a reach for one copper and went up the made road in the evening and stood in it and looked at it going west with nothing on it, and the twenty-eighth was a day that had cost her a hold she had already spent and a name she had given twice and nothing else that anybody could see.

The first of the month came and the cart went up the mill road at about the sixth hour with a cord round the tray, and the cord went over the top of a book with a page fanned open about two inches from the back, and she stood in the yard and watched it get past the elms.

It was the seventh tray.

## The Book That Came Down

It came down on the ninth at about the eleventh hour on the back of the cart that takes the empty trays up, and the cart stopped at the post the way the cart has stopped at that post nine or ten times a month for as long as there has been a post there.

There was a tray in it with a cord still round it and a book in the tray and the book would not shut.

She was nine feet off on the near side of the track with the bar on her shoulder because she had come up the road to see whether anything came down it and it had, on the ninth day of a month, nine days after the first cart had gone up.

## The Man With The Paste Pot

He came down off the ladder. He was about fifty-five and he had kept that board nine years and he had a paste pot in one hand and a rag in the other, and he got the tray off the cart with a man from the cart and set it down on the ground at the foot of his own ladder and stood and looked at it.

“Trays go up,” he said. “Trays come down empty. That is nine years.”

He did not open it.

## What Was In It

It was not shut and it could not be shut and the cord was still round it from a building a mile up the road where a man with a pen had tied it and squared the page and put it on the cart at the sixth hour, and there was no page fanned open in this one. It was closed at the back and it was open in the middle, at the same place, and the middle was where her name was.

He looked at it the way a man looks at a thing that is in his way on a road.

“What do I do with a book,” he said.

## A Room, And A Person

There was a low building at the back of that board with a door standing open and a bench inside it and nothing else in it that mattered, and he picked the tray up off the ground one-handed — he had done it for nine years without a bar, because it is a tray and it does not weigh what a person weighs — and carried it inside and put it down on the bench.

She came in and stood at the other end of the bench with the bar against the door frame.

“There is a man in a building a mile up that road,” she said, “who has a counter and a book, and the book has your friend’s name on it and my name on it and five months of days, and it will not shut, and he has sent it back down because he cannot keep it and cannot send it up again.”

She put her hand flat on the cover.

“It does not shut because there are three things in it that have nothing at the other end of the rule. One of them is a furnace. There is a fire in a shed at this mill that you have never seen and that no office on nine days of road built, and the account of the clay in it is in that book, and the account has no end on it.”

She took her hand off.

“The other one is a holder. There is a name under a line in that book that nobody in the world can produce, and the man who wrote it is not going to be able to produce it, because he refused in a shed at the second hour of the fourth of a month and he was not asked for anything for it.”

## What He Said

He stood at the other end of that bench and looked at the tray for a while.

“I have had about three hundred strips on that board,” he said, “and four hundred books up that road, and I have never once had one come back down.”

He put his thumb on the corner of the book and stopped.

“Then that is yours,” he said. “I cannot send it up and I cannot send it on and I cannot shut it and I cannot read a word in it, and I am not going to try with a glass, because I have never in nine years had to.”

He picked the strip up off the bench where the man from the cart had put it with the tray.

## The Board Is For The Strips

He went out and got the paste up off the ground with the toe of his boot and worked it round the pot and put the strip on the board and pressed it down with the flat of his hand from the top down and from the left across and from the right back, the way he had done it nine or ten times a month for nine years.

That is what the board is for. Not for the book.

He came back in and stood in the doorway and looked at it on the bench.

“There is a stone under the ladder,” he said. “It is the driest thing I have got and the roof does not come in on it, and I have had nine years of nothing on that board that wanted keeping.”

He got the cord off the tray and set the tray down on the ground and stood the book up on the stone under the ladder with his hand flat on the spine of it while the other one held the tray.

It stood there.

## The Plain Part About One Person

There is a woman in a shed a mile up that road who cleared some work off a board in a gateway because she wanted clay, and then she asked a man with a bench to put her in an account against material in daylight in her own name, and he was right to do it.

And there is a book at a post on a road that holds a furnace she lit with the hand of her own that is left, and a holding she cannot produce, and a refusal she gave at a counter nine days ago with a day against it and her name at the left of it.

And the whole of it is standing up on a stone under a ladder at a post nine years old, and there is nobody within nine days of road who knows that it is there, and the man who has it cannot read it and will not tell anybody, because there is nobody to tell.

## What She Wanted

She wanted to see the end of that fine hand before anybody else did, and that had not changed in two months.

And on the ninth of a month she walked up to a post on the mill road and looked at her own book standing on a stone under a ladder in the rain coming through a roof that does not leak much, and no office that reads that fine hand was ever going to know that it was under a ladder a mile from their door.

She had wanted to see the end of it first and instead she had found out where the beginning of it was, and it was nine feet off a road, in the open, under a thing a man had used for nine years for paste.
