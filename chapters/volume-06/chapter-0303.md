# Seven Coppers At The Gate

She was at Pei’s bank at the fourth hour on the sixteenth and in the cut by the fifth and out of it by the seventh hour with the light off the field, and a reach of drain open and running.

The ring was at the mill.

It had come out of the field behind her own boards at the yard on the thirteenth, on her back and under the bar, tied into the bottom of her coat, and she had carried it two miles and four hundred yards west and the same east again with no help at all, and it stood at the back of the shed against the wall where he had let her put it on the ninth without either of them making a thing of it.

A furnace of her own is not a thing a person leaves in a field.

## Eight

At the gate he paid her the way he had paid her on the fourteenth and the fifteenth, which was out of a fold of cloth in the inside of the door frame, in coppers, into her own hand, with the count done by him out loud and not by her.

Eight.

That was the whole of the money she had taken by hand since the twenty-ninth of a month five months ago, and it went into the front of her coat where she kept it, which was where it had been kept since the twenty-ninth and which was not a safe place and had never been a safe place and was the only place she had.

He did not ask where she was going. He did not ask her name. He had not asked her name on the fourteenth either, and there was a stretch of about nine seconds on the fifteenth where she had got as far as the first syllable of it before he went back inside for something, and neither of them had made anything of that either.

## What Was Left Of It

Bread first, because there was a shop at a gate two hundred yards back toward the mill and she had not been inside it since the eighth.

Then the material shed past the mill, which was not a shop and had no counter and was a lean-to with about nine different things in it standing on the ground, and a man who did not come out from behind the stack for a woman with eight coppers.

Then the rest.

She did not do the sum twice.

That was the whole of it and she was particular about it, standing in the yard of the shed with what was left in her fist, because a woman who does a sum twice is a woman who has begun to want it to come out differently, and she had done that once in her life at a counter about four months ago and had not enjoyed any part of the hour after.

## Eighteen In Four Marks

In the shed, at the back, with the door shut and the broom leaning where the broom leaned, she took out the oilcloth.

That was the one thing she had that had been hers since before she crossed, and it was a square of oiled cloth about as big as two hands and it had been folded in eight in five months and the folds had gone soft as cloth goes.

Eighteen counterfoils in four marks.

A counterfoil is what a man tears off a plank when a day is worked and paid, and there is a date at the left of it and hours against the date and a rate, and a contractor’s mark at the bottom, and a painted mark in one corner. There were eighteen of them in the cloth in four small bunches held together by the fold, and there had been eighteen of them since the first of the season, and the dates came out in order.

She did not unfold them. She did not need to. She could put the whole sequence in the order it went in and she had done it in the dark more times than she had done it in the light.

There was no plank here.

There was no plank on that ground at all, anywhere, for any woman, and there was not going to be one, and the eighteenth was the last of them and there was no nineteenth that any living thing on this side of nine days of road could ever produce.

She folded the cloth and put it back against her ribs, above the three dead weight tickets, under the thing she never described and never took off and had surrendered to nobody.

## The Flat Stone

The clay came in a broken piece about the size of two fists and she put it on the flat stone by the door and stood over it with the bar’s end in her right hand.

A bar does not cut brick. A bar does not cut anything that has been fired. She had found that out in a hall with no roof with an arm gone at a shoulder, and had bought the rest of what she knew about it out of a counter at five coppers a day, a day at a time, for five months.

Breaking a piece of bought clay down to a size one arm can carry meant putting the flat of a second finger against it and putting the heel of her hand behind that and leaning on it until the clay gave.

It took eleven minutes.

It had taken eleven minutes the first time she ever broke a piece of it, in a yard four hundred yards past the post, and the flat of a second finger had gone from eleven minutes to longer, permanently, and nothing since then had put a minute back into it.

She did not hurry. She was in the cold and the shed was dry and there was no light on the ground and nothing anywhere within nine days of road that had any reason to be where a woman was standing over a stone with her hand on a lump of clay at the dark.

The clay gave at the eleventh minute, the way it had given every time she had broken a piece, and she picked up the pieces and turned them over on the stone and chose the ones that would go in the ring without being cut again and put those in the sacking and put the rest in the pile at the edge of the yard where the man would clear it out in the morning without knowing what it was.

## The Light

She banked the ring that night in the corner of the shed floor with a ring of brick and clay round it and the pieces she had broken going in where the last firing had been dug out.

It was not a firing.

She put in what was left after bread and material and nothing else, because there was nothing else, and she lit it at the low end the way she had lit it about six times in five months, with a strip of the dry cake and about four minutes of her own breath at the second hour of a morning she had not slept much of.

It came up and it held and it gave her back about as much as a thing of that size gives back, and the ground under it did what the ground under it does, and it had done it twice before and she had never once said it out loud and she did not say it out loud on the sixteenth either.

She sat down on the boards with her back against the wall and her right hand open on her knee, four fingers and a thumb, and looked at the ring for a while.

## What Answered

It was the first thing she had had in five months that answered her.

That was the whole of it and it went through her sitting on the boards the way a cold goes through a person, and it was not a comfort and it was not a small thing.

A furnace of her own, made out of sixty-one bricks on edge out of two days out of her own column and twenty-nine coppers on a promise, does not care whether there is a wage. It answers when it is lit and it does not answer when it is not, and it has never once been answered by anybody else.

Everything else on that ground answered other people. The mill answered a man with a rule. The gate answered a farmer with a field. The counter answered a clerk with a book.

A thing that answers a person is a thing a person can be found at.

She sat on the boards in the dark at the mill two miles east of the last working point and understood that she had just made herself the easiest thing on nine days of road to find, and that she had done it with what was left in her fist after bread and material, and that she would do it again in the morning.

The sixteenth closed with eight coppers spent down to one arm and a light in a corner of a shed that had not been warm since before she came.
