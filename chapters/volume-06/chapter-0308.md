# One Stair Up

She went west at the second hour on the twenty-third because the twenty-second was gone and there was not one other room on that ground where three words on a piece of cloth could be answered.

She knew what the answer was before she had walked a mile.

She had known it on the twentieth and she had known it on the twenty-first and she had gone down into a drain both days instead of going west, and the twenty-second had happened underneath her, and there was not going to be a twenty-fourth on which to do it.

She walked in with silt still on her coat from a field and put her hand flat on the counter on the side she could reach it with.

“The strip,” she said. “Is there more.”

## What The Counter Is For

The man behind the counter did not move for a moment, and then he put his pen down flat.

“That goes up,” he said.

“I know it goes up.”

“It is not mine to carry.”

“All right.”

That was the whole of the conversation. He did not refuse her, because she had not asked him to do anything. She had asked a question and the answer to it was in a room one flight above him, and a counter keeps books about a room.

She stood there about as long as it takes to look at a door.

At the back of that room there were two doors, and one of them had the stair behind it and the other one had a bench and a broom against it, and above the head of the stair where it went out of the room there was another stair going on up out of the light.

She looked at it for as long as she looked at anything in that building.

There was no writing on any door in that room. There was no name on the stair and nothing on the wall beside it and nobody came down it while she was there, and after about nine seconds she had understood everything about that stair that she was going to be able to understand standing there, which is that it went up, and that it was not the stair she had come for.

Then she went round the end of the counter, because the stair was at the back of the room and it was not locked and nobody came out of the top of it, and she went up.

That is the whole of what she did to get herself into it. There was no man to persuade and no form and no fee and nothing at all between her and the stair except that she had not come up it before.

## The Floor

It was a long room with tables down both sides and a window at each end with the shutters open, and the floor was boards and it was clean.

There were four people in it. Three at one table and one at another. There was a stack of sheets at the near end about as high as a man’s chest and it had a string round it, and there was a paste-pot on each table, and the whole of the room smelled of paste and warm paper and ink.

She had expected a room. She had not expected it to be a place where people worked.

The one at the second table looked up. He had a brush in his right hand and about nine sheets squared up in front of him, and he did not stand and he did not say good afternoon.

“Sit down,” he said. “If you are answering one, sit down, because the answer goes on the sheet with the strip and not in the room below.”

She sat down.

## What The Room Found Out

She did not give her name.

That was not a decision she made. It was that it had not occurred to her to make one, in the way that a thing does not occur to a person who has spent four years being announced and has never in her life had to say her own name into a room to be written down.

The man with the brush took a sheet from the stack and turned it round and put it in front of her.

He wrote at the top of it, in a hand she could see, and she watched him do it.

He wrote the name.

He did not ask her. He read it off a column that was on the left-hand side of a page underneath, one sheet down in a second pile, and he matched it against the woman sitting in front of him the way a man matches a thing he has been given against the thing in front of him, and then he wrote the name at the top of a sheet about two inches down from the top of it, in the ordinary hand.

She walked into a room and an office read her.

## The Gap

Then he said, “There is a gap here.”

He put the flat of his own hand on the sheet about a third of the way down.

“There is a day and a part of a day,” he said. “Twenty-second, and twenty-third to the fourth hour. Is that right.”

That was an ordinary question. It was the question a man asks about a thing he is checking out loud so that two people will not disagree about it later, and he asked it in the ordinary voice with the brush in his hand.

And she had a gap in it.

The twenty-second had gone into a bank of clay on the low side of a field ninety yards below where the drain ran open, and she had come out of it at the seventh hour and gone eight days west in her head and had not been anywhere else.

“I was under a bank,” she said.

He wrote that down.

She looked at him do it and understood that she was confirming it.

She had never once in four years put a true day in as a false one. There were twelve gaps in five months of her own column and she had entered every one of them herself at a plank and explained none of them to anybody, and this was the fifteenth, and she was sitting in a room giving it to a man with a brush who was not going to be told anything else.

“All right,” he said.

## What She Said

“There is more.”

That is all she said. Three words, at about the second hour of the twenty-third, in a room with four people in it and a stack of sheets against the wall, in the ordinary voice, in the daylight, with nobody persuading her to say it.

She did not explain herself.

She did not explain the drain, or the gate, or the reach, or the ring, or the eleven minutes it took to break a piece of clay, or the four months of a season at five coppers a day. She did not say her brother’s name and she did not say what was in the oilcloth against her ribs and she did not say anything at all about the inside of nine days of road.

She said there was more, and she said the twenty-second was under a bank, and the man with the brush wrote both of them down in the same hand on the same sheet.

Then he turned the sheet round, put it on the stack, and put the stack against the wall with the other one.

## What It Cost

Her column was the only thing in the world she could prove about herself.

Five months of days, entered by her own hand, with twelve gaps in it that she had entered herself and explained to nobody, and eighteen counterfoils in four marks in an oiled cloth against her ribs that no instrument in nine days of road could check and no living person had ever held.

That was it. That was the whole of what she had.

And from the fourth hour of the twenty-third it was a thing somebody else could read.

She gave up the truth of her own days in order to be a running column in somebody else’s book. She was right to. There was a piece of cloth with three words on it and a square of nothing beside it and she was the only person on that ground who could fill that square with something true, and the price of filling it was that she would never again be the only reader of a fact about herself.

That is the price. It is not a figure and it is not a stage and it is not on her body.

She cannot be un-answered. The answer went up the stair with the answer.

## What She Felt In The Room

She was not frightened in the room.

She noticed that about halfway through, sitting at a table with her hands flat on it, and it was the second thing that had turned up out of the ordinary order in six days.

The first had been under the bank.

What she felt instead was a kind of relief that is not relief, and she had felt it exactly once before in her life, at the second hour of the seventeenth of a month, in the middle of a reach at the last working point where eleven men were two hundred yards behind her, and she had not thought about that once until this second and did not think about it now.

She thought about it for about the length of a road between the mill and the post, on the way back, with the bar on her shoulder and the light off the field on her right.

Then she stopped thinking about it and went to the gate and opened a reach and a half and took the money at the gate in her own hand, and the strip was on a stack against a wall one flight above a counter four hundred yards behind her, with her name at the top of it and fifteen gaps in the days under it.
