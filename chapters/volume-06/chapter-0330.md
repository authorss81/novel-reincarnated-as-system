# The Name Against Material

The twenty-eighth of a month came up and she went east at about the seventh hour with the bar over her left shoulder, and the board on the wall inside the gate had a column at the left of it that had not been there on the twenty-sixth.

About as wide as two fingers, ruled down the length of the board, and every line on it running into the top of that column.

Not her name. Space for one.

She stood in the gateway and looked at it while a man counted out nine boxes against the long wall inside.

## Why It Was There

The man who kept the list had a board off the bench under the lean-to and a rule in his hand and he did not come out from behind it.

“Because since the twenty-sixth a line does not go out of this yard against nothing,” he said. “I write the name at the left of it or I do not write the line. That is all of it. I did not make it and I cannot do anything about it.”

She had heard something like that four times in five months and every time it had been true and every time it had cost her something she had not seen coming.

“That is what a name is for,” she said.

“That is what a name is for,” he said.

## The Part He Had Not Written

A materials account is not paid to a person. That was the next of it and he said it without being asked, because she had been in his gateway a month and she had learned that a man in this yard says the rest of a thing or he says none of it.

“It goes to a place,” he said. “There is a place to write on this and it has been blank since I came here and I have never once written in it. Tell me where your material goes.”

She did not have to think about it. The ring was in a shed at the mill two miles west and there was nothing else in nine days of road that a fire of her own stood inside.

“The mill,” she said.

He wrote it. He wrote it the way he wrote everything, in ink, standing up, without looking at the board while he did it.

Then he put the chalk down and looked at what he had written, and that was new. In a month he had not once looked at a thing he had finished writing.

## What He Said About The Mill

“There is a book at the mill,” he said.

She did not say anything.

“Not a book of wages and not a book anybody’s name is in. A book that shuts at the end of every month and goes on a cart up the road to the counting house, and it is read there against the other books that went up that month. I know that because the cart comes out of this yard on the round and it passes the post, and the keeper at the post has a board and puts up what comes down the road.”

“A material that goes to the mill goes into that.”

“Everything that goes to the mill goes into that,” he said. “I have not had a reason to care about it before today. You have handed me a reason to care about it today.”

“You wanted your name on this ground,” he said. “It is on it. You had better know that putting a column on that board has cost me exactly nothing, because I do the same work with a line on it that I did without one.”

## What She Had Wanted

That was the whole of what she had wanted for a month.

She wanted a line on a wall with her at the left of it. She had said her own name out loud in daylight at a gate two days ago and had been handed nothing for it, and she had walked west at the sixth hour with a piece of board with four nails in it inside her coat, and she had been sure that what she wanted was to be findable.

She got what she wanted.

Her name was a ruled space about two fingers wide at the left of a board in a gateway nine miles east, and it would be filled every time she took a line off that board, and the yard would know whose a load was, and the office that keeps the materials book would know, and it would be copied, and there would not be one hour of the rest of her life that she got to keep.

And the load was going to a shed two miles west with a book in the mill behind it that shut on the thirtieth and went up a road.

## Where She Stopped

She walked out to the road and stood at the gate post and worked it out in the ordinary voice, in daylight, with the bar over her shoulder.

A materials account is a place material goes. She had given the mill because the mill was the only building in nine days of road with a fire of her own in it, and she had said it in the time it takes a man to write four words, in a gateway, without asking where a thing that goes to the mill stops.

The mill takes the stone and the mill puts it in the ring.

And the mill has a book that shuts on the thirtieth and goes up a road to a counting house, and in that house there is a counter, and on the sixth of a month last there had been a strip of cloth about as wide as a hand lying on that counter with three words in it, and a room one flight above the counter with four people in it and a man with a brush.

Her name was not going to stop at the shed.

## What She Did Next

She took the second line off the board.

It was a head at the low drain on the far side of the elms. Two days and one box, and the far side of the elms is about three miles east of the gate, and there is nothing on that ground between them but open ground and a hedge.

Behind her the man with the ink on the side of his right hand wrote her name at the left of the line she had taken down, and squared the board up on the bench, and did not look up.

## The Twenty-Ninth

She came back into that gateway on the twenty-ninth at about the eighth hour with the bar over her left shoulder and nothing on it, because a box is four days of the ring and she had the work in front of her and not the stone behind her yet.

She stopped at the bench before she went out again.

There were six lines on the board. The one she had taken down was written at the bottom with a day against it and one box, and her name was at the left of it in the yard’s hand.

The other five had nothing at the left of them at all.

She stood and looked at that for about a minute, and it is the whole of what a name against material looks like on a board in a yard nine miles east of a mill: one line out of six, and a woman’s name at the left of that one, and five empty spaces the width of two fingers where the next five women will be.

“I will be back for the third,” she said.

“When the third comes down,” he said, and he did not say one word about her coming back, because a man who keeps a board does not promise a woman when the next line comes off the same place.

She went east at about the eighth hour with a piece of board about as wide as a hand inside her coat against an oilcloth with eighteen counterfoils in four marks in it, and there was about a hundred and ten bricks in a bank three miles off that she had not touched yet.
