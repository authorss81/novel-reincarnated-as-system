# It Does Not Close Clean

She went up the mill road on the tenth day with a box of dressed stone against her chest and the bar over her left shoulder, and she stopped at the post the way she had stopped at about eleven posts in five months, because there was a strip on the board that was not lying flat.

## The Strip

Every strip she had ever seen on that board lay flat.

She knew that without knowing it, the way she knew a bar came up off the ground right way up. A strip comes off a cart and it has been folded in the middle in two and it goes up wet and it dries flat against the board, and the board at the foot of that ladder was nine feet of stuff with about nine years of strips on it in rows, and every one of them was lying against the board like a page in a closed book.

This one was not.

It was up past the edge of the board on the right-hand side by about the width of a finger, and it had a crease in it a third of the way down where the fold had been put in and had come back out again, and the two halves of it stood out from the board like the leaves of a thing that has been opened and not shut.

She stood about four feet off and looked at it for about as long as it takes to go round a lamp twice.

## The Man With The Paste Pot

He came down the ladder when she had been standing there long enough to be worth coming down for.

“The cart came down on the first,” he said. “I have had a strip off that bundle every nine or ten days since the road opened and I have put it up wet and it has gone flat every time.”

“Yes.”

“This one did not go flat.”

“No,” she said. “It is longer than the board is wide.”

He nodded at it the way a man nods at weather.

“It is about a hand longer than it was,” he said. “And it has a crease in it a third of the way down instead of in the middle. That is a strip that has not been folded in two.”

He got the paste up off the ground with his toe and worked it round the pot.

“I did not fold it,” he said. “It came out of the book that way. A man puts a crease in a strip in the middle when the thing it is a strip of has been shut.”

He looked at the board and not at her.

“And it is what the board is for,” he said, “so it is up.”

## What She Worked Out Standing There

A strip is a reading. A reading is a book that shut on the thirtieth, written out so that people who cannot read a book can look at it.

A strip is made in two, in the middle, and it goes up flat.

And there was a strip on that board with the crease a third of the way down and the end of it standing out past the edge of the board by the width of a finger, and it said on it, in a hand she could not read and had never been able to read, a line that had no end on it.

## The Plain Part

She put her hand on the edge of the board.

From the hour that strip went up, nothing went up that road shut.

That is not a thing an office decides. A tray-man ties a cord and a keeper pastes a strip, and a book shuts if everything in it has an end on it.

And a cord goes round a tray whether the book in it is shut or not, because a cord goes round a tray, and a tray goes on the first cart, and a tray goes on the first cart whether there is anything in it or not.

She broke it by working.

That was the whole of what she had done. She had cleared five listed things off a board in a gateway in seven weeks, and she had asked a man with a bench to put her in an account against material, in daylight, in her own name, and he had done it.

And every box she had carried three miles had gone into a book that shuts on the thirtieth. And a book that shuts on the thirtieth cannot carry a line with no end on it and shut.

She was eighteen years old and she had never in her life refused anybody anything and she had not refused one thing.

She had cleared some work.

## What The Board Is For

Nine years.

He said it with the paste pot in his hand and not looking at her, in the ordinary voice, the way men say a figure they have been keeping.

“In nine years I have put about three hundred strips on this board and I have had four hundred books up that road and I have never once had one come back down.”

He stopped.

“That is not because anybody is hiding anything. That is because there has never been anything on one that needed answering. Everything that goes up that road is a reading of a thing that has been dealt with, and a reading of a thing that has been dealt with does not get answered, because it is not a question.”

He put the paste pot down on the ground at the foot of the ladder.

“There is a strip in the bundle behind me,” he said, “and it came down with the cart this morning, and it is not a reading.”

## The Thing That Came Down

She looked at it on the ground where he had put it, face up, wet at one end where it had been in with the wet in the cart.

She could read the first line of it, because the first line is always a reading and readings are in the ordinary hand.

It was her name.

It said NING YAN, OF LOWWATER, and under that a column of days, dates down the margin and hours against them, and a season marked at each end, and the word good above the hours.

And then, about a third of the way down, in the fine hand that she had never once been able to read, four or five lines of something with a rule drawn under the last of them and nothing at the other end of the rule.

She stood four feet off that board with a box of dressed stone against her chest and read her own name in a hand she had never seen and could not finish reading.

She has stood on that road for five months and there is a building nine days of road east of it where every mark on that strip can be read, including the fine hand, and nobody in the building is ever going to come down it.

## The Keeper’s Ordinary Voice

He looked down at it.

“I cannot read that,” he said, “and I have never read a fine hand in my life, and I have had about four hundred books up that road and I never had one come back down and I have never once had to ask anybody a question.”

He picked it up and folded it in two, in the middle, the way he folded four hundred strips, and it folded.

“That one folds,” he said. “That one is an ordinary strip. That one is a question, and it has got to go back up the road with the next bundle, and there is not one thing I can do about it and I would not do anything about it if I could.”

He put it in his coat.

## What Has To Happen To It

She stood at the foot of that ladder with a box against her chest and worked it out, and it took about nine seconds and she did not do it out loud.

It has to go back up.

It goes back up on the bundle with the next strip that comes off a book, and it goes into a building with a counter in it and a floor over the counter, and a room above that which she has looked at for nine seconds from the bottom of a stair and has never been in, and the men in it read what goes up that road against everything else that went up it.

And the thing they have to read is her days, and a material account against the mill with nothing at the end of the rule, and a holding with nothing at the end of the rule.

And the fine hand is not going to be read in a building by a man who has never been able to read it.

Somebody is going to have to stand in that building and answer it.

## The Simple Part About One Person

There is a woman three miles east of that post with eight holds in her left hand and four fingers and a thumb on her right and five counts of breath on a loaded reach.

She is eighteen years old. She came out of a bound nine days of road behind her with nothing. She has a line with a name at the top of it in a book that shuts on the thirtieth, and a piece of cloth folded in three against her ribs that says a holding under it is to be presented in person in a room she has never been in.

And a strip on a board nine feet below her hand, standing out past the edge of the board by the width of a finger, with a crease in it a third of the way down, is going to make somebody ask her a question in a building with a floor over the counter.

The strip had been on the board ten days and it had not gone flat and it was drying, and it would be there on the twentieth, and on the thirtieth of that month there would be a tray going up that road with a book in it that was not shut, and every tray after that one, for as long as she cleared work on nine days of road, would carry a book that did not shut and a strip that did not fold.

She stood at the foot of the ladder with the box against her chest and the wind off the flat behind her, and she was not afraid, and what she had instead was a want with a shape in it.

She wanted to see the end of that fine hand before anybody else did.
