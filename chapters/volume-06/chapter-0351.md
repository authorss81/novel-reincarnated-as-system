# The Last Heavy Thing

She took the ditch line off the board at the seventh hour on the twenty-second of a month because it was two days and it had to be done and because doing it was the only way she had of not thinking about the twenty-third, which was the following day.

She got the whole eleven feet in on the first day and six feet of the bank back on the second and the head was in on the second evening with about four hours of light left, and she sat down on the crest with the bar across her knees and did not get up for nine minutes.

Then she walked east and stood in the gateway at the works and asked the man behind the stack a question she had been carrying for about four days.

“Anything heavier than a footing.”

## The Ditch Line

She got in at about nine feet at the seventh hour on the twenty-second and worked down the near side with the bar and the four that were not on her arm open at her side, and a ditch is a cut that has had its bank taken off one side and left standing on the other, and what comes out of it goes back into the far side of the same ditch where it was taken from.

That is the cheapest work on that board and everybody on that ground knows it, and it is the only work on it she could still take in about four days’ time, and she had nine holds in her left hand and five counts of breath going up a loaded bank and about two and a half hours of daylight left when she started.

She got about twenty feet of it in and put the same twenty feet back in and the bar came up in the one hand the whole of both days and she did not once have to put a piece down and come back for it.

Then she did the second twenty feet at about the ninth hour in about a quarter of the light left, and the head of it was against the ditch end and there was about sixty feet still to do on the day after, and she stood on the bank at about half past nine with the bar across her knees and the ditch open below her and the dark coming on over the elms.

The bar was good. The bar had been good for four years and the one hand had been good enough and there was nothing on nine days of road that had found out otherwise, and she had been standing on a bank two days before with nine pieces in it and knew all about it.

Then she carried the bar east and asked the man behind the stack what was heavier than a footing, which is a thing she had been carrying since the eighteenth.

## What He Said

He put the bar down on the cart bed and wiped his hands on his coat and looked at her for about as long as it takes to count nine.

“The ramp,” he said.

“The ramp is not on the board.”

“No,” he said. “The ramp is not on the board and it is done once and it is done in the autumn and it is dressed stone laid on edge, and it is the heaviest thing on this ground, and there are three men in this yard who do it and it takes them nine days.”

He leaned the bar back against the side of the cart.

“It is not for anybody. It is not a listed thing and no one has asked for it and it is not paid out of the board. It is paid out of the yard in the autumn by a man who is not me, and I have not told anybody on that gate about it in nine years because it is not mine to tell and it is not the season for it.”

## What She Said Back

“When is the season for it.”

“About the twenty-third of a month, in the autumn, for about nine days,” he said. “And you are not having it. You know that without me telling you and you asked me because you wanted to hear a man say it out loud so that you could stop asking.”

She stood in that gateway with the bar on her shoulder and did not say one word, and he went back behind the stack and did not come out again.

## What She Had Asked For

She had wanted a line on a board that her hand could not do, and she had been working at it out loud for eleven days.

And she was going to get it in the morning, on open ground a mile and a quarter east of a mill whose wheel had not turned, with a bar lying on the grass and nothing within about half a mile that had ever heard anything.

What she had not worked out, and worked out standing in that gateway with the bar on her shoulder, was what a woman does on the morning after she has got the thing she asked for and it has taken the thing she asked it to take.

There was a yard two miles east with a ramp in it that nine men could not do in nine days each, and there was a board with four lines on it and a gate that paid by the reach, and she was going to walk out of the ground where all three of those lived in the morning, at the fourth hour, on her own, and come back into it at the seventh with less.

## The Shed, Second Hour

She lay on the boards with the ring burning nine feet off and the strip against her ribs and said it out loud in the dark because it was the fourth hour and there was nobody.

“I am taking one in the morning.”

He took a while.

“The last six took the end of a lift, then the feeling in a hand, then a hold, then a count of breath, then a reach, and then the heavy end, and every one of them took it out of the work and not out of the rest of you,” he said. “I have watched six of them go through a woman I am standing next to and I have never once watched one take a thing she could still use.”

That was four sentences and every one of them was in the present, and he did not say that he was sorry and he did not say that she should not do it, and he had not told her the price of anything, which was the arrangement, and the arrangement had never once been broken in four years.

## The Last Thing Before The Light

She got up at about the third hour and put her palm flat on the flat stone by the door and it was cold and her hand was warm and it told her nothing.

Then she stood in the doorway with the bar in her left hand and put her right hand out flat on the frame of it, four fingers and a thumb, and the arm went open about a minute later the way it did, and she shut it and opened it and shut it.

Then she took the bar off the frame and held it out in front of her with the one hand, the way she had held it about nine hundred times, and thought about the fifth hour of the following day and about what a bar wants.

Eight hours.

That was all the warning she had and there was nobody to give her any, and she was eighteen years old and she had a bar in one hand and a ramp about four miles east of her that nine men in a yard could not do in nine days each.
