# Eight Holds

She had eleven of them before the ninth of a month and nine of them after the first of a month, and the difference between the two numbers was a stage and a floor she could not feel.

That had been true since the fourth hour of the first of a month in the mill yard with a bar laid down on the flat stone. The stage had taken the cold out of her hands and the hands were warm and the grip was still there.

She had not asked about it because there was nobody on nine days of road to ask and she had not wanted to find out.

## The Price, Named Before

She told it to him at the top of the low drain on the far side of the elms on the seventh of a month, at about the seventh hour, sitting on the sill with the bar across her knees and the light going off the water in it.

“The next one takes a hold out of my left hand,” she said, in the ordinary voice. “Nine goes to eight and it does not come back, and I am going to spend it into the head at the low drain because that is the work I have taken and it is two days and a box and I cannot carry the box on eight.”

Four counts of breath, and the wind along the ditch, and about nine seconds.

“All right,” he said.

She had said the price before she said the word. That was the arrangement and it had never once been broken in four years.

## The Days Before

The second of a month she worked half a reach at Pei’s gate and took two and a half out of the fold in the inside of the door frame, and the third she started on the head at the low drain at the fourth hour with the bar and about a hundred and ten bricks in the bank.

On the fifth she took the low side out to a foot and a half and found the clay under it about as hard as the clay in the ditch between the two farms, and on the sixth she went up to the second foot and lost about an hour on a stone that would not come and had to be wedged and not lifted.

She did the whole of it in two days because the board said two days, and she was at the gate at the fifth hour on the seventh of a month with a bar over her shoulder and one box to wait for.

## The Ninth

She went out past the last hedge at the fourth hour of the ninth with the ring lit at the mill behind her and the bar laid down on the grass, and she stood on open ground about a mile and a quarter east of the mill with nothing within about half a mile that had ever heard anything.

She did it standing up.

The stage came the way the five before it had come, and there was no announcement, and there was not going to be one.

Nobody inside to be told. No county book on that ground to take anything down. The sound of a stage is a sound and a sound has to go somewhere, and it went into the ground under a hedge on flat ground a mile and a quarter from a mill and about a quarter of a mile into the air, and a dog on a farm a mile off did not bark, and nothing came back.

It was over at about four minutes.

[Ning Yan, past the line, servant-born, under no ceiling any county holds. Golden Core, stage six, is held.]

The sentence came out in the far-side house form, the same length as the five that had come before it, and then there was nothing after it. She had stopped listening to it by the third one. All she did now was wait it out.

## What It Took

She got the bar up off the grass.

She had picked it up off flat stone a hundred and forty times in five months and she had done it with the fingers on her left hand closing on it and the heel of the right one under it, and on the ninth of a month at about the fourth hour and a quarter, a mile and a quarter east of a mill, the fingers closed on it and the heel did not get under.

She took it the other way and it came up.

That was the first thing. She put it down and picked it up again, twice, and the second time she did it the same way the first time had not gone, and the third time she did not bother.

Nine holds. Eight.

## The Plain Part

A hold is not a finger.

It is not the right index finger, which is gone, and it is not the arm that is open half the time, and it is not the four fingers she still has on that hand. A hold is a thing in the left hand that closes when she means it to close, and there are eleven of them and there have been eleven of them since she was nine years old, and she has never once had to think about them.

Now there were eight.

She stood on the grass a mile and a quarter east of the mill with a bar in her left hand and four fingers and a thumb on her right and worked out, in the ordinary voice, what the two numbers had in common, and they had nothing in common except that both of them were things her hand could not do.

Her left hand would not close on something it had closed on every day of four years.

That is what it cost. It is not on her face and it is not in her chest and it is not one of the four things anybody within nine days of road would be able to see.

## What She Wanted In The Shed

She walked back and got to the mill at about the sixth hour and broke four lumps on the flat stone by the door at eleven minutes a piece and banked the ring and sat on the boards with her back to the wall.

What she wanted, and it was a want with a shape in it and she had had it since the second of a month, was to know whether eight holds would clear the next thing she took off that board.

There were six lines on it on the ninth.

She had nine of them in her left hand a week before and nine of them on the ninth of a month, and she was not going to be able to work that out in a shed with the fire going, so she lay on the boards with the bar against the wall where she could reach it and put her left hand flat on the flat of the plank and closed it.

It closed.

It closed and it was nine fingers’ worth of a thing she had had all her life, and she had told it at the top of a ditch two days ago that it was going to be eight.

So the difference was not in the closing. It was in the grip. She had held this bar four thousand times and a hold was the difference between holding it and having your fingers close on nothing while the weight of it goes somewhere else, and she had nine of eleven of those in a left hand on the ninth of a month.

She got up off the boards in the dark and went to bed on the boards because that was where she slept, and she lay there and put the flat of her left hand against the plank and could not make it be anything other than ordinary, and that was the first night in five months she had gone to a thing she could not feel.
