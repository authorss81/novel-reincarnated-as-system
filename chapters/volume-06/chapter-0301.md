# What The Counter Said

She walked back west on the twelfth because the road west was the only road she had and because a question about a book can only be asked of a room that used to hold it, and that room was two miles and four hundred yards behind her in the direction of the post.

She went at the second hour. The bar was over her left shoulder and she had walked this road about forty times in five months and had never once walked it with somewhere to be.

The trestle was still standing. The post was still standing with its board on it, and the strip on the board was not hers and had not been hers for a season, and she went by it at four hundred yards and did not cross the ground to it.

The low building had a counter in it and a stove behind the counter and a shelf on the wall. There were six men at their cups and the spike was on the desk and the man behind the counter had a book open flat under his palm.

He looked up when she came in the way he always had, which was the way a man looks at a person who has been in the room before.

## What A Counter Keeps

“You have had nothing,” she said.

“No,” he said.

That was the whole of the opening and it was complete. She had put a season’s column into that room on the second of a month four months back and had put every day of it in herself, at the plank, in her own hand, with twelve gaps in it that she had entered herself and explained to nobody.

And from the first of the month there had been nothing in that room at all, because there was no season.

He had not asked her about it on the seventh, when he had told her the book shut at the dark and went in a tray and the tray went out on the first cart, and he was not asking her about it now. He had asked her nothing about it for five months and he was not going to start at the end of it.

But it was in his room.

That was the part she had not thought about. A counter holds the shape of what it has been holding, and when a book goes out of a room the room is a different shape, and a man with a book open flat in front of him has to account for the difference between the shape yesterday and the shape today.

He had written it already. She could see the line, about a third of the way down the left-hand side, with a date against it in the small hand.

## The Line

She read it over the edge of the counter without leaning, because she was five months past the day when anybody in that room had needed her to lean.

Date at the left. A line under it that said a book had gone out of that room on the first of the month.

And at the side of it, in the margin, the rule.

Not her name. Not a name of any kind. Not a figure. A rule that house keeps at the side of the page, the way a house keeps a rule at the side of anything it has written four hundred times, and it said that a book sent up leaves the room clear.

That was all.

It was a line with a date in it about a room.

Every counter on that ground would have had a line with a date in it about that room, and every one of them would have had the same rule at the side of it, and not one of them would ever have had to look at who had been in the book that went out.

She stood there and read the shape of her own absence in a clerk’s handwriting and it did not have her in it anywhere.

## The One Question

She got one question into the room and she had chosen it on the road, which was the only place she had anywhere to choose one.

“Does a person come back for a book that has gone up.”

He wrote for about as long as it takes to finish a line and then put the pen down flat, which was a thing he did when he was finished and not when he was finished with her.

“No,” he said. “A book that has gone up is read and it stays where it is read.”

“And the line.”

“The line is the line,” he said. “I write one at the first of every month and the first is the first, and the rule at the side of it is the rule, and it is the same rule on every reach from here to the far end and I did not write it.”

He said the last part without any weight at all and he said it to her because she was standing in the room and not because she had asked, and she understood the whole of what that meant, which was that this office had not become interesting to itself in five months.

She did not ask him to send for the tray.

She did not ask him to write her name anywhere, not on a line, not in the margin, not on the back of a sheet, and the not-asking cost her something she had not known she was going to pay and she paid it standing there.

She did not argue with the answer either.

## What The Answer Was

It was the ordinary answer. It was the answer a man gives in a room with six other men in it at the second hour of the twelfth of a month, and he gave it in the ordinary voice, and the six men went back to their cups about the time it was finished because a line has been written and the writing of a line ends a thing.

Nobody in that room had done anything to her and nothing in that room was about her.

She stood at the counter after he went back to his page and worked out that she was the only person inside two miles who knew what that line meant, and that the line was going up a stair inside a week along with everything else that had ever been written about her by somebody who was doing a job.

That part of it had not changed on the road and it had not changed at the counter either. She had put it there herself with a stage, in the open, at the second hour of the seventeenth of a month, in front of eleven men who could see her and nobody at all who could hear anything, and not one thing anybody said in that room had put a hand on it since.

## The Road Back

She went out at the fourth hour and the sun was on the road and she had nothing in her hands, which was the shape of every walk she had made in five months.

Half a mile short of the mill she stopped.

She had asked what happened to the book and had been told in the ordinary way, and the answer had been that a line about a room had been written in a hand she had never seen, in a rule she had never read, by a man who had not known and would not find out.

Somebody two miles east had picked that line up and put it in a tray with the word *good* at the top of the column above the hours, and somebody up a stair had read the column, and the tray had not come back down.

Her five months had gone up a stair and come out as a line with a date on it about a room.

She went on to the mill with the bar on her shoulder and the road going flat about a quarter of a mile on.

## The Thirteenth

She went back on the thirteenth because she had not finished looking.

That was the whole of the reason and it was a bad one and she knew it was a bad one at about the fourth hour of the second mile, which is where a woman thinks properly on that ground, because the road is flat and there is nothing on it and the bar is heavy.

She wanted to see the room.

Not the line. The line she had seen. What she wanted was to stand in a room where a shape had been left by her going out of it, and there was no such room, and she had come two miles and four hundred yards to find that out a second time.

The stove was going and there were six men at their cups and the man behind the counter had the book open flat under his palm and the line was where it had been on the twelfth, with the date against it and the rule at the side of it, and nothing about it had moved in a day.

She looked at the shelf on the wall.

That was all there was to look at. From the second of a month four months back to the thirtieth, that shelf had held a book with a season in it, and a season was a working thing that had a beginning and an end and hands in it, and from the first of this month the shelf held nothing but a sheet of paper weighted under a bit of iron to keep the curl out of it.

That was the whole of what an office has of a season when the season is over.

She stood in the room long enough that one of the six men finished his cup and went out, and then she went out too, and the man behind the counter did not look up and did not say anything, and the door was a plank door and it did not close behind her.

She went east at the fourth hour with the bar on her shoulder and nothing in her hands.

A line with a date on it about a room had gone up a stair inside a week along with everything else that came from that ground, and she was the only person inside nine days of road who knew what any of it was about, and she was two miles from the mill and had eaten the last of what she carried out of the yard on the morning of the eighth.
