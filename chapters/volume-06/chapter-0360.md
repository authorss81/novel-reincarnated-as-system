# The Length Of A Reach

The board at the works had been bare for about a month and the season had not turned and the gate paid one copper a day, and on the twenty-second of a month she went out past the mill at about the seventh hour with no bar, because the bar takes two hands and she had left it against the fence of the shed where it had stood since the twenty-third of the month before last.

There was a gang of four men clearing a field bank about two hundred yards off and she was on the next bank over on her own, taking stones out of the bottom of a hedge that had gone over in a wet year before she came, and there was nothing on nine days of road for her to do that day and she had done it for three days.

## What Happened

She put her hand flat on a stone about the size of a flat loaf that she had not touched and had not been near.

It came.

Not up. Along. It went out of the hole it was in at about the pace her hand went and it stopped with her hand on it, about the length of her own arm away, and she put it down on the grass where it stopped, and it was down.

She took her hand off.

The hole it had been in was the shape the stone had been in. There was no dust around the far side of it. Nothing was broken and nothing was moved and nothing was anywhere near it.

## What Did Not Happen

The four men were about nine feet off at the near end of their bank and not one of them stopped.

That was correct of them and she would have said so to anybody who had asked her. A stone that goes four feet and stops is a thing a man with a bar can do, and they were four men on a bank with bars, and there is no man on nine days of road who is going to look up off a stone for one that moved a foot and a half.

She stood in the grass and waited for something to be startled and nothing was startled.

## The Part That Does Not Come Back

She stood on that bank for about nine minutes with her hand open on the grass where a stone had been, and she was not surprised, and she went looking, the way a person goes looking for a feeling they have just lost, and there was nothing there.

And where he was — in the room with the floor and the table and the two shut doors, in the place she could not see and had never asked about — nothing at all happened, because she had not put this there.

She had been able to put everything else there for four years. She had put a hold and the whole of an unnamed man and a fire in a shed and a refusal she had not written in there.

She put nothing in there and the stone came, and the surprise did not come back.

It will not come back. It is not a thing anybody gives her twice.

## The Twenty-Fourth And The Twenty-Fifth

She went out again on the twenty-fourth and again on the twenty-fifth and she took four stones out of that hedge the ordinary way, with her hand on each of them and her arm open, and she lifted the fourth one about two inches and put it down again, and nothing came, and that was ordinary and she had expected it to be ordinary and she was not disappointed, and the not being disappointed was the other half of the cost.

She had a thing now that she had not chosen and could not repeat and would never be able to practise, and the whole of what she could do with it was put one stone down clean and then never do it again.

## The Strip

The carter came by on the twenty-fifth at about the eighth hour with the shafts down and a strip in his hand, because a strip had come off the bundle at the works and it had her name at the top of it and the man who kept the board would not hold a thing for anybody and had given it to the first cart that went west.

She took it at the gate post and stood with it in her hand, and there was nothing else in either of them.

The first of it was the ordinary reading and she could read that. Her name. A column of days with dates down the margin and hours against them and a season marked at each end and the word above the hours that said good.

And about a third of the way down it, in the fine hand that she had never once been able to read, four or five lines with a rule drawn under the last of them.

She stood at a gate post three miles east of a mill whose wheel had not turned since before she came and read the top of it and could not read the rest.

## What Was In Those Four Or Five Lines

She knew what was in them.

That was the part she had not expected. She had carried a strip folded in three against her ribs for thirteen weeks wanting to see the end of that fine hand before anybody else did, and she had got it, and she did not need to read a word of it.

## The Word At The Head Of It

The fine hand had put a word at the head of those four or five lines before it put anything else there, and she could not read one letter of it.

She knew the word. It was the one after Golden Core — Nascent Soul, the fifth — and she had known the name of it since before she could count to nine, and she had gone into it three days ago on a bank behind a mill with four men two hundred yards off and nothing said out loud.

It was on a strip in a hand she had never been able to read, and a carter had put it into her hand at a gate post three miles east of a mill whose wheel had not turned, and nothing within nine days of road had made it happen and nothing within nine days of road could name it.

## The Date On It

There was a date on that strip and she knew the date because she had stood on a bank a quarter of a mile from where she was standing and put her hand on a stone she had not touched.

And it was written in a hand she had never seen by somebody who had not been within nine days of road of the hedge and had not been there and had not heard it.

The hand could read what happened.

She could not read the hand.

## Nine Days Of Road

Nine days west, and a stair, and a floor, and nine men on it who had not gone up that road in five months because she was not carrying their hours. And a tower with a light in it that comes on at the fourth hour and does not stop, and a clerk’s arms going up four flights and coming down empty.

She had put her name on a wall once and an office had found out she existed.

Everything that ever happened to her inside that boundary was announced. Every stage, every hold, every count of breath, every finger, every date the men had read out at a plank about four hundred yards inside it — announced, out loud, in a room, to nine people who had to write it down before she finished saying it.

She was standing on a bank in somebody’s ordinary country with a furnace behind her at a mill that no office built, and a column ahead of her that every office above the mill could read, and she had done one thing on that bank that not one person within nine days of road could have priced and not one of them had heard.

## The Last Of It

It was in a hand she had never seen, on a strip a carter had put in her hand at a gate post, and it was on its way up a road to a room she had looked at for nine seconds from the bottom of a stair and had never been in.

She turned the strip over. There was nothing on the back of it.

She put it inside her coat against the oilcloth where two other things had lain for thirteen weeks and were not there now, and she went west on foot toward a gate that paid one copper a day and a shed with a fire in it that lit itself.

She was not going back inside. She had said so to nobody and she had never had to, because there was nothing on nine days of road that could take her there and nothing in nine days of road behind her that had not already been counted.

She will not walk it again.
