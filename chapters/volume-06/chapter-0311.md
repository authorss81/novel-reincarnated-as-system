# The Brick Money

She went to the shed past the mill at about the eighth hour on the twenty-sixth with two and a half coppers in her hand and the bar over her shoulder, and she came out of it knowing exactly what a furnace of her own costs at this stage, and she stayed outside in the yard for about nine minutes afterwards working it out, which is a thing a person does with a number rather than with a building.

## Nine

The shed is a lean-to with about nine different things in it standing on the ground, and there is no counter and no shelf and nothing priced on anything, and a man stands behind the stack who is not in any hurry and is not unwilling and does not come out for a woman with eight coppers in her coat.

She asked for the clay and the brick that a poor firing of a ring takes, and he said nine coppers.

That is the number. She got it once and he said it once and she did not ask him again about it in any form, and she has not asked anybody about it since.

Nine coppers for a full charge, at prices past the mill, in a country where a day of clearing at a gate about five hundred yards from that shed is worth two and a half.

## The Size Of A Charge

The second thing she found out took her about four minutes and she had it before she was off the step.

A full charge does not come in a size one arm can carry.

It comes in a sack. It comes in a sack the way it comes from the ground it was dug out of, and a sack of brick and clay weighs what a sack of anything weighs, and she had a bar and four fingers and an arm that is open half the time and she was going to have to carry it half a mile from that shed to a shed with a ring in it at the end of a day’s clearing.

She had learned in about four months what that costs to learn, in a yard four hundred yards past the post, on a flat stone at the dark, with the flat of a second finger against a lump of bought clay.

Eleven minutes to break one piece to a carrying size.

A full charge is not one piece.

## The Arithmetic

She did it in the yard with her back against the outside of the lean-to and it took four lines.

A reach is a reach.

Half a reach was what she could open in a day at the fourth hour, and the rate was five coppers, and two and a half is what is left of five on half of one.

Three days of half reaches is seven and a half.

A charge is nine.

So: four days of clearing for one charge, and then three trips for the same one.

Four days because she could not carry a whole charge in a load and three trips because the material comes out of that shed the size it comes out of that shed and not the size one arm can hold.

She sat down on the edge of the stone kerb at the edge of the yard, which is a thing she had not done in front of another person in a long time, and put the bar across her knees, and looked at the nine coppers she had not got.

That is the whole of the arithmetic and she has not done it differently since.

## What She Did Not Go And Find Out

There were three things on that ground that could have moved a sack of brick and clay half a mile for a woman with a bar, and she stood in the yard and named all three of them to herself, and then she did not do any of them.

A clearing paid in something other than money.

A cart round.

A shed that owed her.

That is what was on that ground. There was a cart round that went out of the mill yard every month whatever was in it, and there were farms all along two miles of flat ground with drains on them, and there was a man in a lean-to who would have lent her a bar and a rope for a month for the sake of nine coppers.

And the one thing on that ground that can pay for brick in a size one arm can carry is not coppers.

She had worked that out standing in the yard at the eighth hour of the twenty-sixth of a month, out loud, in the ordinary voice, to nobody, in one sentence.

And then she had got up and gone into the shed and asked for a third of a sack and paid for it out of what was in her hand that evening.

She broke it to a carrying size on the flat stone by the door at the dark with the bar’s end and the flat of a second finger, which was the longest the flat of that finger had ever been and which cost her about a third of an hour for a poor piece, and she carried it back to the shed at the mill in two loads with a bar under it because one arm is one arm.

## Three Days Is Enough

The twenty-seventh was half a reach.

The twenty-eighth was half a reach.

Seven and a half in three days, less what a third of a sack cost her, and the ring came up on the night of the twenty-eighth banked low in the corner of the shed floor, lower than it had ever been, and it gave her back about as much as a thing of that size gives back.

Three days is enough for a ring to be told three times that it is not being fed.

She did not say out loud what the ground under it does, and she has never said it out loud, and she was not going to start in a shed that belonged to a man who had never asked her a single question in three weeks.

What she did say out loud, at about the ninth hour on the twenty-eighth, sitting on the boards with her back against the wall and her right hand out flat on her knee, was nothing at all.

In the dark, with the clay cooling:

“Every day you are lit, I am one day further from being able to carry what you need,” he said.

She put the oilcloth against her ribs and left it there.

## The Third Of A Sack

It came to the shed in two loads and she was not proud of the second one.

A third of a sack is about what she could put under one arm and lift off the ground without putting her shoulder through it, and she had worked that out in the yard by looking at the sacks and then at her own right arm, and she got the first load up onto the flat stone at the back of the shed about the ninth hour and the second load about a quarter of an hour later.

She put it down in the corner where the ring was, out of the wind, and stood looking at it.

There was enough of it in that corner to keep the ring doing what a ring does at about one third of what it was doing in the morning of the sixteenth of this month, and there was not enough of it in that corner to ever make it hot again without another trip to that yard.

That is what one third is. That is the whole of what one third of a sack is, and she had known the number was nine since the eighth hour of the twenty-sixth and had known the size since about four minutes after that, and having both of them at once in a shed with a ring in it is a thing she had not been ready for on the twenty-sixth and was entirely ready for on the twenty-eighth.

## What She Wanted

It was a want, and it had no shape in it, which was the new thing.

She was not frightened, because the only room she had been frightened of she had been in. She was not ashamed, because there was nothing anybody had done to her that she had not put her own hand to. She was not angry, because there was not one person on that ground she could have been angry at and she had worked that out on the twenty-fourth and it had not improved since.

She was hungry in a way that had nothing to do with food, and she has not been hungry since the first Foundation and she was not going to call it hunger in her own head, and it went on the page once and she got it out of herself and did not say it to anybody.

She wanted a thing she could carry in one trip that would make the ring hold.

That is the whole of it. It is not a plan and she did not have one. It is a want with no shape in it, in a shed two miles east of the last working point, on the twenty-eighth of a month, with half a reach a day and a charge at nine and a flat stone by a door.
