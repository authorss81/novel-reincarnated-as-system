# The Closed Season

She stayed at the mill because the mill had a floor and she did not, and because on the ninth she found out what she was for in that building, which was trays.

A tray with a warped lid weighs what four wet bricks weigh. She got the first one up off the stack and turned it flat-side down and set it down without a knee touching the boards, and the man with the rule watched the whole of that and then went back to his own work and did not say anything about it.

That was the whole of the hiring and it took about as long as the tray took to lift.

She lifted four on the ninth. He lifted six. Neither of them said anything about the other four, and neither of them was wrong, and by the tenth she was setting them into a row instead of a stack because a man who sorts boxes all day will tell you what saves him walking round a pile, and what saves him walking round a pile is not having to look at any of them.

## The Question

She asked it on the eleventh, at the second hour, between two trays, and she had the question ready in about the way a person has a thing ready that they have been carrying a long way.

He had a tray on the bench with the mouth of it open and a cord in his hand and the book in it showing at the edge, and she put her two fingers on the rim of the bench on the side she could reach with a whole hand and said:

“What goes up.”

That is a working question. She chose it for that reason and she chose it in the morning for about an hour before she came in, the way she used to choose the end of a bar she was going to set down against.

He did not stop.

“A book that shuts clean on the thirtieth is a finished book,” he said. “It goes in a tray and the tray goes on the first cart up to here. Every reach on this ground sends one a month.”

He set the cord and turned the tray.

“Up the road past the store there is a stair in the counting house and everything that goes up that stair gets read against everything else that went up it,” he said. “Nothing comes back down.”

Then he put the tray on the stack, which made eleven, and turned round and went to the door to look at the road, because he was a man who looks at the road.

## What She Did Not Ask

She had one question. She had asked it at the second hour of the eleventh and she had got five sentences and she was not going to ask another one.

A second question about where a thing goes tells a man that the first one mattered, and she had spent four years learning how much a question that matters can cost a person who has to write it down.

She did not ask whether her tray was on that stack.

She did not ask whether she could carry one of them down the road herself.

She worked it out instead, standing at the bench with her hands on it, out of two things she had had before she set foot in this yard: a season that had shut on the thirtieth, and a mill that the books are taken to.

That is all it took. She had been carrying the arithmetic for five months without knowing she was carrying it, and it came out in the yard in about as long as a tray takes to turn.

The book closed clean.

It went up.

## The Relief Of A Rule

She had expected something with a door on it.

That was the honest shape of her thinking on the ninth and the tenth, walking the yard and looking at the stack and not knowing what a stack of trays was for. She had expected that whatever this country kept about a person it would be kept somewhere she could be brought to and shown, in a room, by a man with a key.

Instead the whole of five months had turned out to obey a rule a man in a shed could say out loud in one breath between two trays.

A book that shuts clean on the thirtieth goes up.

She said it over to herself twice, in her own head, standing at the bench, and found that she did not believe it because it was too simple, and said it a third time and found that she believed it more.

That is what a rule does. It is not a comfort. It is the first thing since the crossing that has been simple, and she distrusted it immediately and could not help using it, which is what people do with rules and is the whole of what rules are for.

She picked up the next tray.

## The Tenth

On the tenth the cart came in at the fourth hour again and the bag had six in it, and she noticed that he did not read anything on the way out of the bag and did not read anything on the way into the tray, and that he still put them in the order they came.

Then she noticed that it was not random.

She watched him take the sixth one out and hold it an inch off the bench before he set it down, with the tray tilted about a finger, the way a man tilts a thing to find out how much is in it.

“He is not reading them,” she thought. “He is weighing them.”

That was a thing a man could do in thirty years and never once think of as knowing anything.

It meant that the whole of what any of those trays was could be got out of one hand by anybody who cared to learn, and that no office on this ground had ever needed anybody to learn.

She thought about that for most of the rest of the day, on the floor of a shed with a broom leaning in the corner and eleven hours of lifting in both arms and nothing to do with any of it until the light went.

She had a bar against the wall by the door and four fingers and a thumb on one hand and nine holds in the other and a body that did not get cold the way other bodies get cold, and none of it had been in use since the seventh of a month.

She had not been in use since the seventh of a month.

That was the sentence the whole of the ninth and the tenth had been sitting on, and she came round to it about the seventh hour with the light going off the boards, and she did not find it cheering and she did not find it frightening either.

She found that she was tired in a place she had not been tired in since before the crossing, and that the tiredness had come out of her arms and her back and the two square inches of her palm that took the weight of a tray, and that she would sleep on those boards like a person who had done a day.

## Eleven On The Stack

The cart came in on the eleventh at about the fourth hour with a bag on it, and he emptied it and put four more on, and the stack went to fifteen and then he covered it with sacking and came back in and looked at her.

“You are not on any book here,” he said.

“No.”

“Books do not stop here,” he said. “They stop up the road.”

That was the whole of the conversation about it and it was the correct answer, and she had asked for it and she got it, and neither of them made anything of it.

She was not on any book here.

There was a yard and a floor and a stack of trays and a man who tied cords and did not read the spines of anything, and a woman who lifted trays and was not asked where she had come from and had nowhere to be at the dark.

None of it was written down anywhere.

She had not been that unaccounted for since she was a girl in a hall with no roof, and she had not known it was a thing she had lost until the floor of a shed gave it back to her.

She found that she did not know what to do with it.

She lifted trays.

## At The Dark

He came out of the door at about the seventh hour with the rule already in his coat, and stood in the yard and looked at the road and then at the stack and then at nothing much.

“You can have the floor till the cart wants turning back,” he said. “Not after. A floor with a man in it is a different thing.”

That was not advice and it was not a kindness and it was a man telling another man where the boundary of a thing sat, which on that ground was the ordinary way of being decent to somebody and cost him about nothing.

She took it the way it was given, which is to say she put it in her head and did not put it anywhere else.

At about the eighth hour the cart came out of the yard loaded to about the height of a man, trays roped twice under boards and sacking, and it went east, and the road it took was not the road she had come in on.

Everything on this ground sent one a month. Every reach of them.

Eleven trays on a stack was a normal morning, and the sorter had not looked twice at any of them, and she was the only thing in that yard that had ever wanted to know what any of it was for.
