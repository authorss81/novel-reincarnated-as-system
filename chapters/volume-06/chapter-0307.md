# The Cut She Went Down

The choke on the twenty-second was not the choke she had been working on.

It was on the low side of the same bank, about ninety yards below where the drain ran open, where the field had been under water for two days back from a break in the hedge, and where a man has to go in on his back to get at the stones.

She went in because the water was coming into the field from there and the field had a crop in it that a man with a family had nine months of in, and because the gate pays by the reach and that was the reach that wanted doing.

It was cold enough in there to take the feeling out of both hands inside about a minute.

## The Shoulder

The clay on the shoulder of that cut was standing in water and had been standing in water since the rain two days back, and a bank that has been wet and then dried on the top and stayed wet underneath does not hold.

It came in while she was on her back with the bar under the third stone.

Not fast. It did not slide. It leaned, the way a wall leans, and kept leaning, and she saw the light go out of the sky above her in a strip about a hand wide and then in nothing at all.

Then it was on her legs.

She got the bar across the cut under her shoulder and got her knees in and drove it against the far side until the clay took the weight and stopped leaning, and then she lay still in the noise for a moment counting, because counting is the thing she does.

She started at one and had got to nine before the clay moved again and came another span in.

And the part of that she would think about afterwards was not the cold and not the weight.

It was that there was nobody.

## Under It

There were about four minutes of it in the dark.

She did not think about the bar and she did not think about the bank. She thought about the ordinary things, in the ordinary order, the way a person does when the body has taken over and the head has been left the part of the job that does not need hands.

She thought about the eighteen in four marks and where they were.

She thought about a room with a stove in it four hundred yards behind her and a shelf on the wall with a sheet of paper weighted under a bit of iron.

She thought about a door standing open for anybody off the road nine days west, which she had been told about in the middle of a working afternoon by a woman who knew nothing about her.

And then she stopped thinking about anything in particular and lay under about two feet of wet clay on her back with the bar across her shoulder and the light a hand wide above her, and she understood one thing clearly, which is that a person in that position makes no sound at all.

Not a small sound. Not a noise.

Nothing at all.

There is nobody inside nine days of road who would come, and there is no shape of a person that could be heard from under a bank, and she had never in her life needed to know that and had known it inside a minute of getting under.

## Nobody

There was nobody within a mile of that cut who would have come.

She was on her back under a bank two feet of standing clay over her with the light out, two hundred yards from a gate where a man and a boy of nine were about their own afternoon, and it had not once occurred to her, at any point between the fourth hour and the dark, that it might be a thing worth asking herself.

For four years she had been the strongest body within nine days of road, and she had spent the whole of that advantage underground in a hole with four fingers and an arm at half, and the thing she was angry about afterwards was not the bank.

It was that it had taken a hole to find out.

There was no version of that afternoon in which a man stood in that cut. The boy was nine. The farmer had a field with a crop in it. The nearest other work on that ground was half a mile off and the nearest neighbour was most of a mile off and neither of them knew she was in the ground.

Nobody died. Nobody was hurt except her. Nobody was saved.

## Getting Out

She got out with the bar.

There was nothing clever about it. There was a place where the clay had come off the shoulder and left a ledge, and she got the end of the bar into the ledge and put the heel of her hand on it and pushed, and the bank held, and the bar did not bend, and that was the whole of the arithmetic.

Four fingers and a thumb on one hand and an arm open half the time on the other and nine holds out of eleven in the left, in about as long as it takes to count eleven, alone, and then a second stage with the bar as a ladder and four counts of breath everywhere.

She came out of it at about the seventh hour into air and light and stood in the wet grass at the bottom of the field with the silt going grey-brown off her coat and her right arm hanging the way it hangs now, and she could not get it to close for about nine minutes and she stood there and let it not close.

Nothing broke in her that she has not had back once.

That is what she checked, standing in a wet field at the seventh hour, in that order: the four fingers, the arm, the nine holds, and the count of breath.

All four of them were what they had been at the fourth hour.

## What She Did Not Do

She did not go back down into that cut that day, and she did not go back down into it the next day, and the drain on the low side of that bank is still silted and the water still comes into that field and comes out of it the way water does.

She did not tell Pei anything she did not have to.

She told him the low side was under from the break in the hedge and would want a man in it with a spade and two men on the rope, which is true, and which a man with a field would have worked out for himself in about a minute without being told it by a woman who was standing in his gateway with silt to the knee.

She did not tell him about the shoulder.

There was nothing in that for her. There would have been a look, and the look would have been a question, and she was not going to stand in a gateway and be asked by a man with a household whether she had been under a bank, and she was certainly not going to answer him.

## The Cost

It cost her a day.

The twenty-second, gone, in the middle of a run of days she kept in her own head in a piece of oiled cloth against her ribs and that no plank on that ground had ever heard of and never would.

And the morning of the twenty-third was going to go with it, because a woman who has been under a bank for four hours goes west at the second hour or she does not go at all, and she had known that on the seventeenth when she had read three words on a strip of cloth and worked out where the only room on that ground was that could answer them.

So the twenty-second was gone and half of the twenty-third was about to be, and she was going to spend the half of it in a room she had never stood in.

She had never spent a day under a bank in her life. She had spent four hundred days standing up in the open where eleven men could see her, in the light, at the second hour, and that had been the whole of what she had been able to offer anybody.

At about the eighth hour she got back to the shed at the mill with the ring banked low behind her, and she did not light it, and she put her right hand out flat on her knee and looked at the four fingers and the thumb and thought about a hole in the ground and what it had been that had taken a hole to find out.
