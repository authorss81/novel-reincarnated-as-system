# The Column In Two Hands

She went west at the second hour on the thirtieth and up the stair, and the man with the brush had about nine sheets squared up in front of him and a paste-pot on the table and did not stand and did not say good afternoon.

She had one question and she had had it ready since the twenty-eighth.

“How many books have I been in.”

He put the brush down flat.

“Two that I can tell you about,” he said. “That one, and one I have never had.”

## The One He Had Never Had

The copy of her column stood on the stack at the end of the table, second pile down, and it was hers and it was in a hand she did not know and it had five months of days in it and a word above the hours.

She had seen that word once, standing at a table on that floor, and had not been able to read a number in the line under it.

“You are going to ask me what the second one is,” he said.

“Yes.”

“I cannot tell you and I am not going to look for it,” he said. “What I can tell you is what I do with yours. Every month I copy it out against another book, by hand, because the two of them were put across from each other. That is a cross-reference. It is done in the ordinary course and it has been done every month since the twenty-third of a month back.”

“Copied against what.”

“Against whatever it was put across from.”

That was the whole of what he had and he said it once and picked the brush up again.

## Why A Cross Reference

“Off the top of your head,” she said. “Why does anybody read a thing about a person against a different book.”

He thought about that for about as long as it takes to write four words, and then he said it in the ordinary voice, and it was the truest thing anybody in that room had said to her in five months.

“So that a thing about you is also a thing about what you have done,” he said. “Otherwise you have got a person on one page and a person somewhere else, and nobody can say the two of them are the same one.”

She stood in the middle of that floor with the paste in the air and did not say anything for about nine seconds.

## The Sentence That Goes With It

A book that shuts on the thirtieth is finished.

That has been true on that ground since before she came and it is true of the book her days are in, and it was true of it in the room below at the fourth hour of the twenty-third of a month when a man found her name off a column and wrote it at the top of a sheet without asking her.

A person is not finished.

She had never put those two facts next to each other in her head in five months and she had been living in the gap between them every day of it.

“A cross-reference does not shut,” she said.

“A cross-reference does not shut,” he said.

## What He Said About The Floor

She looked at the stack against the wall, a man’s chest high with a string round it, and the second stair going up out of the light at the head of the room.

“This floor,” she said. “How long has it been here.”

“My father had it.”

He did not look up when he said it and neither did the three at the other table.

“It is a long room with tables down both sides and it has been that since before I came to it,” he said. “What comes up that stair gets read against everything else that went up it. Nothing comes back down. That is the whole of what this floor is and it is not a thing anybody has ever decided.”

He turned a sheet over and squared it.

“There is a line out west,” he said. “It is kept. There are men on it and they are paid by the day and their families are on it. Nobody east of here has ever needed one thing out of it, including me, and I have been copying books for eleven years.”

## The Other Three At The Tables

She watched them for about half an hour before she said anything else.

There were four people in that room. The man with the brush, and three others at two tables, and one of the three was a woman of about forty who had been copying a single column all afternoon and had not got to the end of it, and she did the same thing on the same kind of sheet and wrote about four words to a line and it took her as long as a man ties a gate.

It was not difficult work. Anyone who can read can do it. What it took was to do the same eight hundred strokes a day for eleven years without once deciding what the column meant, and every one of the four of them had been at it long enough for it to be in their hands.

None of them looked at her while she stood there and none of them stopped, and she understood inside about four minutes that she was in a room where a person is a thing being done to a column of.

That is what it means to be read. It is not being watched. It is being the eighth hundred strokes of somebody’s ordinary afternoon.

## The First Of A Month

She went down the stair and across the room and out past the counter, and the man behind the counter had the seasonal book open flat and did not look up from it.

She stood at the door and worked the thing out on the step in daylight.

Two books. One with her days in it, written in a hand she had never seen by a man who had found her name off her own column without asking. One nine miles east that she had been entered into out loud at a gate with a strip of cloth, against material, in a book that is copied.

And from the twenty-third of a month back the two of them had been read across from each other by a man with a brush on a floor above a counter, once a month, in the ordinary course, because somebody had put them across.

She could not see either of them. She had never seen either of them.

She could see that they were being read together.

## What She Wanted In That Room

She had wanted to know whether she was findable. She had known the answer to that for five weeks and it was yes and there was nothing she could do about it.

What she had not known, and found out standing on a step at the second hour of the thirtieth, was that findable was not the whole of it.

A line that is read by one office is a fact. A line that is read by two offices and set across from each other is a fact about what the person has done, and that is a different thing, and a fact about what a person has done is the kind of thing that has to be dealt with by somebody.

She wanted to go back up and ask one more question and she did not ask it, because she had asked one a month and got it, and asking a second one tells a man the first one mattered, and she had spent four years learning what that costs a person who has to write it down.

So she walked east with the bar on her shoulder and carried the strip that had been lying on the counter since the sixth of a month, folded in three, because it would not fold in two.
