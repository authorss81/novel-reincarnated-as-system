# A Strip With Two Columns

The strip came down on the seventeenth of a month, in the afternoon, on the bench under the lean-to at the works, and the man who kept the list left it lying there face up for about an hour and a half before she came in off the flat.

He had not had it off a cart. It came down the same road the cart went out on, in a bundle, with about nine others, and it was one of a great many and the man behind the stack had not looked at it.

That was the whole of what it cost anybody. On nine days of road a strip comes down every nine or ten days whether or not there is anything in it, and he had put nine or ten of them up on his board since she had started coming to that yard, and he had read maybe one.

He read this one because her name was on it.

## What Was On It

Two columns, side by side, ruled, with a margin at the left.

The left-hand column was days.

Five months of them. Dates down the margin and hours against them and a season marked at each end, and at the top of the column, in a hand she had never seen and had been reading about for five weeks, her name.

She put her finger on the top of it.

It was not the way she wrote it. She had written it four times a month for five months at a plank with the flat of her second finger and eleven minutes a line, and this was not that, and there was no part of this that her hand could have made.

Under the days, at the foot of the left column, there were two more lines.

The first of them had her name at the left of it again and the word material under that and the mill written against it, and a rule under it, and nothing at the right of the rule.

The second of them had nothing at the left of it at all.

It was in a finer hand than anything else on the strip. It was a single line with a mark at the head of it that was not a name, and under that mark there were four figures and a rule, and there was no date at either end of it and there was nothing under the rule.

## The Right-Hand Column

It ran the whole length of the strip, from the margin down to the foot of the left column, and it was the same hand as the second of the two lines under her days, and it was finer than any hand she had ever seen on a piece of paper.

At the top of it there was a mark like the one on the line under her days, and under the mark there were four figures, and then the word HELD, and under the rule at the foot of the column there were two more words in the same fine hand.

She could not read a single one of them.

She stood at the bench with the strip lying on it and read the right-hand column four times.

She could not read the four figures. She could read the shape of them and she could read the word HELD and she could see that the figures in the right-hand column were the same four figures as the figures at the head of the line under her days, and that they had been put there by the same hand on the same strip on the same afternoon.

That was a fact about paper and not about anything else, and she stood there and made herself hold onto it for about nine seconds before she let herself have the rest of it.

## What He Read Out

He could read the left column and he could not read the right one, and he read the left one out in the ordinary voice with his thumb on it, which is what a man does when a thing about him is going to somebody.

“Ning Yan, of Lowwater,” he said. “Then five months of days, and a season at each end, and the hours against them, and there is a word above the hours that says good and that is not mine to read either.”

He went down to the foot of the column.

“Two more lines. One of them has your name on it and the word material and the mill against it and a rule under it with nothing at the end. The other one has nothing at the left of it and a mark at the head of it and four figures and a rule with nothing at the end.”

He stopped there and took his thumb off the paper.

## The Words Under The Columns

Under both columns, in the same hand as the fine one, there were four lines and nothing else on the strip. He read them out word for word, because that is what a strip is for.

“What is copied at this reach is copied twice. The column of Ning Yan, of Lowwater, is kept and is copied and is not on this ground. The holder is to be presented in person in the room named under the holder. The doors of that room shut on the twenty-eighth of a month.”

Then he squared it up on the bench and put his hands flat on either side of it and looked at her.

“I have kept this board eleven years,” he said. “I have had strips off that bundle before and I have never once had one with a word on it at the bottom.”

“So have I,” she said.

## What It Was

A sending.

That was the word for it in the mouth of nobody on nine days of road, and she found it herself at about the fifth hour on the seventeenth of a month standing in a gateway with a piece of cloth about as wide as a hand lying on a bench.

It was not a thing about her. It was a thing about him, sent through her, by people who had read a column and found a holding under it and had written down the two of them on one piece of paper with a rule between them.

And it was not a strip that goes on a board.

Every strip she had known about on that ground went up or came down and got pasted on a board and was a reading. This one had four lines of instructions at the bottom of it in a hand that was not a keeper’s hand, and it was not going on the board.

## What She Did With It

She folded it in three, because it would not fold in two, and she put it inside her coat against the oilcloth with the eighteen counterfoils in four marks and the two pieces of board with four nails in them, and it sat against her ribs where nothing else sat.

She worked half a reach at Pei’s gate on the eighteenth and the nineteenth and took two and a half out of the fold in the inside of the door frame both days, and she did not go east, and she did not go up the mill road, and she did not take it out again for six days.

On the twentieth of a month she took it out and held it in both hands at the fourth hour standing at the far side of the elms with the wind off the flat.

Two columns. A name she could read and four figures she could not, and a room named under the holder in a hand she had never seen and would not be able to see for as long as she lived on nine days of road.

And four lines at the bottom of it, of which the third one had been about half of it.

That was the whole of what she had, and she wanted a person in a room to tell her what a holding is, and there was not one other person on that ground who had been in a room with a floor above it except a man with a brush who was not permitted to write in his own notebook.
