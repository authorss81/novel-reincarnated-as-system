# The Ground Past The Mill

The first of a month came up clear and she was on the mill road at the fourth hour with the bar over her left shoulder and nothing else to do that day except the thing she had been putting off since the seventh.

There was no book to shut. There was no plank and no queue and no season and no line of anybody’s hours in a book four hundred yards behind her. There was no work booked and none of it would shut on anything.

So she walked east.

## The Wheel

She went past it at a hundred yards, on the track, at a walking pace, in the light, with a bar over her shoulder, and she looked at it properly for the first time in five months.

It is not turning.

It has not been turning since before she came, and it was not turning at about the eighth hour of her second day on that ground five months ago, and every day since, and she had looked at it four times from half a mile off in the dark and had never once gone past it.

Going past it is not a thing. That is what she found out at about two minutes past the fourth hour. There was no gate and no wall and no man to ask and nothing on either side of the track, and a woman can walk past a wheel at any hour of any day, and it does not stop being a wheel, and it does not do anything.

She had kept four hundred yards between herself and a board at a rise and half a mile between herself and a wheel for five months, and both of those distances had been correct, and neither of them had been about the board or the wheel.

## The Flat

Past the mill the ground goes flat.

That is the whole of the geography and there is nothing on it and nothing on the far side of it and nobody within nine days of road who has any reason to go on.

It is farmed. It is hedged at the ditches and open between them and it has been cut and it has not been cut, and there are about four houses on it in four miles and a track that goes on east and does not come back for a long way.

She walked about a mile and a half of it before the light came properly up over the eastern hedge and looked back.

Behind her, two miles west, the mill was sitting in its yard with the wheel on the side of it and the lean-to against the far wall and the stack of trays under it, roped twice, with boards and sacking thrown over.

The board was gone from under it. It goes every morning or it does not go every morning, and there was not one person on that ground who cared which.

## What Is In A Stack Two Miles West

Two miles west of where she was standing, a woman with a brush had nine sheets squared up in front of her on a table.

She did not know that and there was no way on that ground for anybody to have told her, and she thought about it anyway for about nine minutes, walking east on a flat road with a bar on her shoulder.

There was a stack of trays in a yard under sacking. Every reach on that ground sent one a month. There was a floor one flight above a counter where a strip came down and an answer went up, and a room above that with a stair going on up out of the light which she had looked at for nine seconds and had not gone up.

And somewhere in that yard, under boards and sacking, in a tray with a cord on it, there was a column that ran from the second of a month four months back to the thirtieth with fifteen gaps in it now instead of twelve, and a name at the top of it, and a word above the hours that she had put there herself in the light of a morning when she was seventeen.

The twenty-second was not in it.

The twenty-second was under two feet of clay on the low side of a field and she was the only living thing that knew that, and a man with a brush had written *I was under a bank* in the ordinary hand because she had told him to.

She could not get to any of it. Not the tray and not the stack and not the floor and not the sheet. She could get to the counter and she could not get above it, and there was a man with a brush about nine sheets of the way further.

## What She Left At The Mill

The three boards and the roof she had built in five months were at the yard four hundred yards past the post and she had not gone back for them and was not going to.

The bar was over her shoulder.

The pan and the knife were in the shed at the mill and the ring was banked low in the corner of it against the wall behind a third of a sack, and it had not been hot since the twenty-first and would not be hot again for four days at a rate of two and a half a day.

The oilcloth was against her ribs with eighteen in it in four marks and no nineteenth that any living thing on this side of nine days of road could ever produce.

And she was two miles and four hundred yards from the only building on that ground where anybody had her in a book at all, and she was going to keep going east for a while.

## The Second Morning

She was on the same road at about the fourth hour on the second of a month with about a mile and a half of it behind her and no reason at all to be on it.

She was not afraid.

She noticed that on the first morning and took it for granted, and then noticed on the second morning that she was not afraid again, and that noticing it was the part that took her about nine steps.

She had been afraid of three things for five months and she had been afraid of all three for five months and she had gone on walking through all three without once putting the bar down.

The three things were still there. The strip was on a stack in a room one flight above a counter. The rate was five coppers a reach and she could open half of one. The ring was banked low in a corner of a shed with a third of a sack behind it.

She was not afraid of any of it on the morning of the second of a month, and the three of them were all exactly where they had been on the eighth.

That is the thing she took away east with her on the second morning, and she took it away at a walking pace, because there was nothing else to do with the morning.

## On The Boards The Night Before

On the first night out past the wheel she had slept on the ground, because there was nothing to sleep on and because she had not gone far enough for there to be a house, and in the dark at about the second hour:

“It has been twenty-three days since you walked out of that yard,” he said. “That is the longest you have gone in one direction without stopping.”

She did not answer him out loud and she did not tell him to stop, and she sat with her back against the boards and let the cold come up through her.

She was eighteen years old and she was standing in a country where an office could read her and a wage could be set for her and there was nobody within nine days of road who knew her name, and she had been afraid of all three of those for five months, and she had gone on walking.

Past the mill the ground goes flat and it is farmed and it is lit and there is nothing on it, and at about the fifth hour on the second of a month she went past the last hedge about a mile and a half east of a wheel that had not turned since before she came.
