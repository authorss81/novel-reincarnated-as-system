# He Will Not Give One

The bundle came down on the third of a month at about the second hour and there was a strip on the works board that had words at the bottom of it, and that was the third one since the seventeenth of a month, which is six weeks back, that had words.

She read it standing in the gateway and then she took it down off the nails and carried it east, three miles, with the bar on her shoulder, because she was not going to ask a man in a gateway a question about a holding in the open where nine men could hear the answer.

She got to the shed at the mill at about the sixth hour and put the strip flat on the boards and put her palm on it and told him what was on it before she asked for anything.

## What Was On It

The column on the left was hers and the holding under it was his, and at the foot, under the four lines, there was a fifth line that had not been on the other two.

“The holder named is the only holder named. A second holder is to be named under the first, and the naming is to be done by the first holder.”

It was the ordinary hand. She could read every word of it, and that was the first time she had been able to read anything on that paper except the one line at the top that carried her column.

The fine hand was the same fine hand. She still could not read a word of it and she had been carrying it since the seventeenth of a month, which is six weeks back.

## What A Second Holder Was

A holding was a thing under a column that kept the column from drifting. It was a mark at the head of it and four figures and a rule, and there were no letters on it anywhere, and every book on nine days of road had one for every column in it.

A second holder was a second mark under the first one, with a second set of figures under that, and the point of it was on the face of the strip and she could read the ordinary hand on the top of it.

It was so that if one name could not be produced, another could.

That was the whole of it, and it was ordinary, and it was in four lines at the bottom of a strip about as wide as a hand in a hand she could not read, and it was the first thing anybody had ever put on that paper that she could think of as a kindness.

She put her hand flat on the boards and looked at the ring burning nine feet away and asked him for it.

“Can you name one.”

## The Refusal

He did not take as long as she had thought he would.

“No,” he said.

She stood on the boards with her hand flat on the strip and did not say the next thing for about a minute.

“There is a man at the works,” she said. “There is a man behind the stack and he has a cart that comes twice a week and he is right about everything he says and he has never done anything to anybody. He could stand under it and not know what it was and his cart would go up that road twice a week.”

“You have worked that out in a minute,” he said. “That is the fastest you have ever worked anything out about me.”

He was not angry. He was doing the thing he did with the terms, which was let them come to him instead of going to them, and the strip was lying on the boards with the words at the bottom of it and the words were not in a hand he could do anything about either.

“I am not going to put a second name under that line,” he said. “That is the whole of what I have and it is not a thing I am deciding this minute. I decided it before you asked me, weeks ago, and I have not said so because you did not ask me until tonight.”

## Why

“Then tell me why,” she said, “in words I can write down.”

He was quiet for about half of a minute.

“Because a holding is not a mark on a line,” he said. “A holding is a person the line is held for, and every book on nine days of road has one for every column in it, and they have all got exactly one, and not one office on this ground has ever asked for a second, because they have never needed one.”

He stopped.

“A second holder is a second person an office can send for,” he said. “And it would be on that line for as long as the line is there, and the line is not going to end while the thing it is holding is standing up, and so that man at the works would be on it at the far end of his life and would not know that is what he was standing under.”

He looked at the strip on the boards.

“He has a cart that goes twice a week,” he said. “And in about a year that mark will be in two books in two rooms, and somebody in one of them will put a hand on it, and a man who is sixty will be fetched off a road because he stood next to me in a gateway once and did not say no.”

## What It Costs

She said it into her own head before she could stop herself, and it came out in her mouth anyway.

“You are refusing a thing that would make me safer.”

“Yes,” he said.

## The Plain Part

He did not put a second name under the line.

She was eighteen years old and she had one copper a day at a gate and a bar that took two hands and three lines left on a board and a furnace that lit itself in a shed she walked two miles to, and in about three weeks there was going to be a room nine days of road east of a post with doors that shut on the twenty-eighth, and the only way anything had ever got into that room was one name on a line.

That name was him. He was the only person in nine days of road who could be put in a room, and she was the only person in nine days of road who had a road, and he had just said no to the one arrangement that would have meant neither of them had to stand in it.

She had carried the request for four days before she asked him. She had asked him for a thing that would have kept the line open without her and without him, and it had cost him nothing at all to say yes, and he had said no out loud on the boards of a shed at the second hour of the fourth of a month.

He had said no in his own words and he had not once asked her what she was going to do.

## What She Said Back

“All right,” she said.

And then, because she had learned four years ago that a thing said in a shed at the second hour stays in the shed at the second hour and nothing else, she said the rest of it out loud anyway.

“I am going to have to tell somebody no as well,” she said. “And I do not know yet who, and I have not worked it out.”

He did not say one word about her hours or her column or the four fingers or the eight holds or the four counts of breath. He had said four sentences about a man at the works and he had said no, and he was not going to be asked again.

She folded the strip in three and put it inside her coat against the oilcloth, where the first one had been since the seventeenth of a month, which is six weeks back, and the two of them lay against her ribs and there was nothing in her that had come back from the night.
