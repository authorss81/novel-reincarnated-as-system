# The Bundle Going Up

The cart came up the gate road on the twenty-ninth about two hours after the light, low on the bed and roped twice, going west, and it came to a stop at Pei’s gate because a man with a loaded cart stops at a gate and not because of anything else.

He was about thirty. He had the reins in his left hand and did not get down.

She was standing at the gate with the bar over her shoulder because she had come down off the bank early, which she had never done in nine days at that gate, and because the twenty-ninth was in there somewhere and she had not got to the bank yet.

He looked at the bar and then at her arm and then at the road.

“You are not walking this,” he said.

“No.”

## The One Question

She asked it at the gate with her hand flat on the post, and she had it ready, and it is the only question she has ever asked a carter about anything west of the post.

“Is there anything that comes back down that road.”

He thought about it for about as long as it takes for a horse to move one foot.

“No,” he said. “There is a stair about nine days west of here. Men go up it carrying bundles and they come down with nothing in their hands, and they have been doing that since before I was driving.”

He said it in the ordinary voice, to a woman at a gate, and then he said: “It has never once occurred to me before and it has occurred to me now because you asked.”

That was one question and one answer and she did not ask a second one about that stair and she never did.

## The Road East

The other two things she got for free, because a man with a loaded cart and about two hours of road in front of him talks.

The road went east for two days past the flat ground and then it stopped being the road she knew.

Past about the second day there were more of his carts going up than coming down, because past the second day there were more reaches on it, and every reach sent one a month, and the whole of the first two days was one ground with a mill on it and a number of reaches that fitted in a head.

And a loaded cart came out of that yard every month whatever was in it, and a driver going that way would take a person up with him who was going the same way, and nobody on that ground thought anything of it because everybody on that ground was doing it.

“You can get up if you are going up,” he said.

## The Ride

She got up.

There was a board on the near side of the bed under the load and about a foot of room on it, and she put the bar under the rope where it went across and sat down on the board with her back against the side of the load.

It was the first time she had sat down in daylight since the seventh of a month.

That is the whole of it and it is not a small thing and she was not going to make it into one. She had walked two miles and four hundred yards four times in six days and she was eighteen years old and there were boards about nine inches off the ground in that bed and she had a bar under a rope with her feet on a cart going west into the wind.

The cart went on. She did not say anything for about half an hour, which is longer than she had gone without saying anything to anybody since she crossed.

## The Stack

He came into the mill yard about the fifth hour and slowed to unload and she got down off the board with the bar and stood in the yard while he did it.

And there was a stack.

She had never seen it. She had been past that yard twice in five months and had stopped half a mile short of it every time, and the yard had been a yard with a shed in it and a wheel that was not turning and a man with a rule.

There was a stack of trays under a lean-to against the far wall about as high as a man, roped twice, and there were more behind it under a second lean-to, and there was another stack further along the wall, and a fourth.

They were not on shelves and they were not in racks and they were not in a room with a door. They were on the ground, under sacking, in the open air, in a yard, with the boards and sacking thrown over them against the weather.

That was the first thing about nine days of road and five months of her own column that she had ever wanted to see all at once.

She did not go near it.

She did not count it and she did not ask him how many there were and she did not go along the wall and look at the cords or try to see whether one of them was hers.

She stood in the middle of a yard about two hundred yards from it with a bar in her hand and looked at it for about as long as it takes to look at a thing, and then she went to the shed at the back and put a third of a sack down in the corner and got out the broom because he was going to want the floor and he had not asked her and she was going to do it anyway.

## The Thirtieth

She was in Pei’s bank at the fourth hour on the thirtieth and out of it at the seventh with a reach and a half open and the money in the front of her coat.

There is nothing on that ground that falls on a date.

The seventh used to be something nine days of road behind her and the seventeenth used to be something and the twenty-seventh used to be something, and for five months they had been three days a month that a woman walked two miles for. Here there was no plank and no book and no queue and no man keeping a palm flat on a page to stop the wind getting at it.

The thirtieth came and the thirtieth went and it was a day with a reach and a half in it and about nine hours of sun on a field, and there was not one single thing about it that anybody else on that ground knew was happening.

She had been marking days in the inside of her head for four years in a way that had a shape to it, and the shape had been: which day is this out of the three. She did that arithmetic about nine times between the fourth hour and the seventh and it came out the same answer every time, and on the seventh occasion she noticed herself doing it and stopped and stood in a drain bank with a bar in her hand and laughed out loud, once, about as long as one breath.

There was nothing in that either.

## The Same Shape

The thing she took west on the road at the sixth hour was not the same thing.

It was the same shape.

A man with bundles going up a stair about nine days west of a mill, and coming down with nothing in his hands, and it had been going on longer than he had been driving, and he had never thought about it, and he had thought about it on the twenty-ninth of a month because a woman at a gate had asked him a question.

Past the last working point the same sort of thing went up a stair about two miles west of that yard, in a building with a counter and a stove in it, once a month, in trays, on a cart, whether or not anybody had asked.

And there was not one word for the second of those on that ground, and there was not one person in nine days of road who had any reason to join them up.

She wanted to tell somebody. She had wanted to tell somebody for about as long as she had known it, which was about four years, and she had told nobody, and she was not going to tell a carter on a cart about a stair he had driven past nine times without once seeing it.

She let it go.

He got the gate open where the track comes out and she got down off the board and he went on west with the load and did not look back and never thought about that stair again in his life, which is the correct ending for him and the worst one there is for her.

The twenty-ninth closed with a broom in her hand and a third of a sack in a corner and four hundred yards of flat ground between her and the only room in the world that had her days in it.
