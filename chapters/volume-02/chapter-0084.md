# Nights in the Tower Loft

The copying floor inside Feng Lushi's tower is a long room with five tables and a stove that never quite warms it, and the county copies sit behind the fifth item in the wall the way a secret sits behind a habit.

Ning Yan learned the room carrying paper for three days. She came in with loads at the eighth hour, set them on the intake table, and took away the sealed copies for the town. The clerks nodded to her the way clerks nod to hands. Feng sat at the fair-hand desk by the window with his cough and his brush, copying steadily, twenty-two years of fair handwriting going thin at the edges.

The four county copies she had sent down in her own hand with no name on them sat behind the fifth item in the copying room wall. She knew without being shown, because Du Hong had written it and Feng had said nothing about it, which in Feng is confirmation. He had read all four, walled them in, and entered none of them upward. The unindexed column stayed at five. First month in twenty-two years he had entered none of a thing.

“You eat,” said Feng on the third evening, without looking up. “Loft hands eat.”

“I eat,” said Ning Yan.

She ate at the stove with the clerks, soup and bread, left-handed. Nobody asked her name. A hand is a hand. After, she climbed to the loft with the shutter that did not shut and lay on the mat with her coat for a blanket, listening to brushes below.

“You rest here,” said Yuan. “You breathe easier here than on the floor.”

“I breathe the same,” said Ning Yan. “Cold is cold. But no wall leans here.”

“No wall leans,” said Yuan. “I watch with you and the night is quiet.”

## The Fifth Item

On the fourth night Feng called her down after the clerks left.

He stood at the wall with a lamp, coughing into his sleeve, and looked at her the way he looks at paper, which is directly and without comfort. Then he set the lamp on the table and took down the fifth item, which was a bound volume with a loose spine, and showed her the space behind it without showing her the papers.

“Four,” he said. “Yours. In your hand. No name on them.”

“I know,” said Ning Yan.

“They sit here,” said Feng. “They go nowhere upward. Do not thank me for it.”

“I do not,” said Ning Yan.

He put the volume back. His hand shook on the shelf edge, and he held it there till it steadied. The cough took him for a minute, wet and deep, worse than the winter past. When it passed he wiped the brush and set it in the rack with care, the way a man sets down the only thing he has.

“The buyer came to the tower once,” he said. “To the stair, not the floor. He asked which hand keeps the Qinghe advancement. I told him the county office keeps it and the tower copies upward. Both true. He paid nothing because he asked nothing I write.”

“He pays at the desks,” said Ning Yan.

“He pays at the desks,” said Feng. “Records, not wordings. Wordings go wrong in two counties by nightfall. Records sit.”

She nodded. The distinction between the two Bureau rooms stood in front of her in lamplight: the copying floor here, where county sheets are copied upward by mortal clerks, and the county office on the square, where Sun He holds the county book and stamps. Different rooms, different books, different hands.

A sheet copied here is not stamped there, and a stamp given there does not come back up this stair, and a person who forgets which stair a thing went down finds out at the worst possible hour with the paper still in his hand.

“Column stays at five,” she said.

“Five,” said Feng. “No audit. No Lung. I copy what I am given and I omit what I omit.”

She went up to the loft and added to the docket in wash code: tower holds four, enters none, column five, no audit, no Lung. She did not write thanks. Thanks between them is a thing done by not saying it.

## Carrying Work

The household passed six hundred and ten worked by the week's close. Three hundred and thirty standing. A hundred and two in her own hand.

She earned it carrying. Stone for the wall, oil for the lamp, paper for the floor, water for the stove. The loads built the legs the way the flats had built stage eight, out of weight, and the left-hand form settled into the hips. The breath stayed short, two counts, never lifting to four. She climbed the stair once a day and drilled in the yard at night, lath for a hook, frost on the boards.

Du Hong came up most evenings with news and soup. He had been noticed for carrying her copies, as they both knew, and he carried the noticing openly.

“The county clerk knows my name now,” he said on the sixth night. “I carry tower letters and furnace sheets. A hand with a file.”

“A hand with my hand in his file,” said Ning Yan. “Twice.”

“Twice,” said Du Hong. “The cheap door. I said it before you did.”

“You said it,” said Ning Yan. “It stands.”

He left the soup and went down. She drank it left-handed on the mat with the square noise thin through the shutter gap. The tower lamp burned below in its cage. Feng coughed and wrote and coughed.

“You shelter here,” said Yuan. “You want to stay and you know it is not safety.”

“I shelter,” said Ning Yan. “I do not mistake it. Shelter is a mat and soup. Safety is a stamp.”

“Safety is far,” said Yuan. “I see you walk toward it daily.”

On the seventh night the wind got under the shutter and lifted it all night, banging it against the frame. She got up twice to tie it with cord, left-handed, breathing short in the cold, and twice the cord slipped. The third time Du Hong came up the stair with a nail and a stone for a hammer, and fixed it in ten minutes without speaking, because loft hands fix what bangs.

“You climb for a shutter,” she said.

“I climb for quiet,” said Du Hong. “You breathe short and the shutter bangs. Both keep me awake below.”

He went down again with the stone in his hand. She lay on the mat with the shutter still and the wind outside, listening to brushes below and coughing under the brushes. The tower holds paper the way the furnace holds heat, steadily and without comment, and the hands inside it eat soup and fix shutters and copy fair.

She slept with the docket in her lining and the four copies walled behind a loose spine below her. Six hundred and ten. Three hundred and thirty. A tower that copies nothing upward for her, a column at five, a cough getting worse, a mat under a shutter that does not shut.

It was not safety. It was a roof that was not sect ground, and that was a different thing, and she had spent the day learning the difference the hard way.

## The Page

On the ninth night she asked Feng the question straight out, because the day she asked it sideways would be the day he answered it sideways too.

“Does anything in this tower have my name on it.”

He did not ask which kind of name. He put his brush in the rack and turned the fair hand over and looked at it.

“The intake schedule,” he said. “Entered as labour, two loads a day, your hand and the name you sign. It goes up on the third evening by the carts.”

“And the mat.”

“The mat is not entered anywhere,” said Feng. “There is no paper in this tower that says a person sleeps here, and I would not put one up if there were, because everything this floor writes goes up the stair and out.”

She stood in the copying floor among five tables and a stove that did not warm them and understood what she had come down the pass to find, which was not what Du Hong's letter had said and not what the man in the good coat was buying.

She was living under a man who could not write her down without handing her to whatever the tower copies up to. She was eating soup from a boy whose name a county clerk had written in a file. She was entered on a furnace book four miles above her and nowhere else, and a page saying that no household in this town had her was worth money to a man who had understood paper better than she had.

“Then a clerk can write that I belong to nobody,” she said, “and nobody in this tower can write the opposite.”

“A clerk can write whatever the law lets him write,” said Feng. “The county is the only room in this town that will put a line under a person and stand a county hand behind it. That is not mine to give and I am not going to pretend it is.”

She took the stair once and lay on the mat with her coat for a blanket. The breath went short on the landing and stayed short. The shutter was still where Du Hong had nailed it and the wind still worked at the gap, and the square noise came up in three trades at once and none of them was hers.

So there were two numbers now and she could not add them together. There was a household standing at three hundred and thirty on a mountain book that a county clerk has never read, and there was a page in a county file, being written this week, for coin, saying that no door in Lowwater has her name on it.

The hours were the first half of the answer and they were four months of signing four and a half at a time. The county was the second half and it would not even look at her without the first half in her hand. And a man in a good coat could put his page into a file at the eleventh hour on any morning he chose, and it would sit there and get older whether she had hours or not.

She lay in the dark and made the decision in the way she made all of them, which was out loud, alone, and in numbers.

“I go up on the dawn cart,” she said. “The book has to square before anything else is worth asking for, and every week I sit in this loft is a week that page gets older.”

“You will lose the square days,” said Yuan.

“I will lose them here or I will lose them there,” said Ning Yan. “The book does not weigh where I sleep. It weighs whether I sign.”
