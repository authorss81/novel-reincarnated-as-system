# Clearance Without a Look

She rode up with the water cart at dawn on the seventy-ninth day with the household at nine hundred and twelve worked and twenty-eight standing.

The cut held frost in the shadow and the barrier board stood with the captain's line on it, no order, no clerk's name. The Watchman nodded her through on the cart sheet. Old Gao had the barrels loaded and his mark beside hers. Nothing unrecorded. That was the rule she had made after the burial register, and she keeps the rules she makes.

The quota office anteroom held four benches and the smell of wet wool that does not leave in nine years. She sat second and waited, breathing two counts, with the tithe sheets in her sleeve and four months of hours in Shen Xiu's winter book. The clerk with the broken finger sat at the third table with his own family's sheet, waiting his turn like everyone else. He nodded to her once. Slow writing keeps the book balanced. Both knew it.

“You want this,” said Yuan. “You fear it and you want it.”

“I want it,” said Ning Yan. “I sit and I wait. You watch the brush and you keep quiet.”

“I watch,” said Yuan. “I keep quiet with you.”

## The Signing

Shen Xiu called her at the ninth hour.

He sat at the far table with the winter book open, brush in hand, list beside it. He did not look up. He had looked up once in three hours on the nineteenth day for a second and a half, and she did not expect it again. Tired and honest, never forgiven, never cruel. The machinery he operates does harm without his malice and without his permission. That is what institutions are.

“Ning household,” he said. “Ninth estate. Furnace column struck whole. Account closed.”

“Yes.”

“Quota clearance is separate from the column.” He turned the sheet a quarter. “Hours asked nine hundred and forty. Hours worked nine hundred and forty. Standing nothing.”

She did not move. Nine hundred and forty. The whole of her obstacle, level at the close. Four months of tithe in an enemy's ledger, two blocks in the week and then three, furnace floor and tower loads, fever and short breath, entered slow by a clerk with a broken finger four feet from the furnace mouth.

“How,” she said. It was all she could get out.

“The floor worked,” said Shen Xiu. “The tower schedule worked. Your line worked. Every hour you have worked, and the days you did not.” He turned another page. “The winter book closes square when every estate on it is level or accounted for. Five estates not yours stood out by six hundred and eleven in the winter. They are level now or accounted for. That half is mine and has nothing to do with your arm.”

He picked up the brush. He did not look at her face. He looked past her at the wall with the frame of twelve unsigned sheets on it, two in a roundhouse hand she had logged in wash code, nine in other hands, one the hours sheet. Then he signed.

Clearance. Household owing nothing. Winter book square. Arithmetic, not favour. No plea moves it and no sponsor moves it. Only hours move it, and the hours had moved.

“Thank you for the figure,” she said, the way she had said it on the nineteenth day. “It was in the book.”

“It was in the book,” said Shen Xiu.

She stood with the signed sheet in her sleeve. The room went on round her, brush moving forty sheets an hour into asked and worked and owed. Nobody looked at the servant-born girl at the far table, because the quota office is a room where you look at your own household and every household is in the same condition.

At the door she stopped. There was something to say about the early report, three days early, never forgiven, and she did not say it. There was something to say about his son's clerkship and the fourth line, and she did not say that either. Something close to respect sat between them, and it confused her more than hatred would have.

“You sign it,” said Yuan. “You hold it and you go down.”

“I hold it,” said Ning Yan. “I go down entered. That is all.”

## The Walk Down

It took eleven minutes to get to the furnace floor and she spent the whole of it with the clearance in her sleeve.

The floor took her back without ceremony. The shift leader gave her the channel. The foreman had six on the board and six in the hole, name on the line. The clerk at the table four feet from the mouth looked at the signed sheet when she showed it, nodded once, and wrote *cleared* against her line in his slow hand.

The refused slip under the cover with three seals showing stayed where it was. In about nine weeks no clerk would need to find it. The clearance had found it first.

Nine hundred and forty asked. Nine hundred and forty worked. Her own line complete and continuous from the day the book opened, every block signed, the days not worked accounted for as days and not as gaps. That was what it had cost: everything she had done, and the record of everything she had not.

She worked the channel for four hours with the clearance in her coat. The wall stood straight. The leak ran cold. The breath pulled short at two counts. At the second night hour she signed once more, though there was nothing left to stand, because signing is what a hand does when the book is square.

Then she rode down with the water cart at dawn, entered on the sheet, into Lowwater mud and market noise. The tower stood above the roofs. The county office stood on the square. Du Hong waited at the tower door with his face set the way it gets when he has decided something.

“I have it,” she said. “Signed. Looking past me. No cruelty in it.”

“I know,” said Du Hong. “The carts told the town before you did. The county is next.”

She climbed to the loft and lay on the mat with the sheet under her hand. Nine hundred and forty. Nothing standing. The second leg owned, after the sponsors' seal, with only the county's countersignature to go.

She did not thank Shen Xiu. He had not asked for it, and she had nothing to thank him with that would not make the arithmetic into a favour. The book was square. That was the whole of it, and it was enough to carry into the square.
