# The Licence Clerk

The fall ended in the night, without any decision, and the morning came up hard and blue and completely silent, which was the first silent night she had had since the eighth day of the autumn, and she stood on the quarters' step for a moment and listened to nine women at a trough make no sound at all.

Smoke lay flat on the terrace. There was not a breath of wind and the whole mountain was scrubbed and every voice on it carried four times as far as it had the day before.

The road to Lowwater opened for market that morning, the third day after the fall, and she heard it from the ninth step before she reached the Hall: the wheel-noise coming up off the cut, and the shout of the Watch at the barrier, and then the sound the Watch made when a rope came off a trestle.

Nine steps. The Hall with no roof and the braziers at the north end and the table at the west side with one clerk at it.

He was not Xie Shuo.

Xie Shuo had read a record in a building with no roof and entered one word in it in a fair hand and had been ruined quietly off page by people who never once came near him, and he was gone from that table, and nobody had said so out loud, and the hall had a new clerk at it who was about twenty-eight and had ink to the elbow and a throat he cleared about every four minutes.

“Which of the Qinghe furnace,” he said, before she had got to the table.

“Ning Yan.”

His brush stopped. He did not look up and he did not look at the stairs, and she understood that he had done both of those things on purpose and had been practising.

She put the folded sheet down on the table in front of him and kept her hand flat on it.

“Where did this come from.”

“I did not put it there,” he said. “It was on this table at the fifth hour and it has been on it every morning since, and on the day before yesterday it was on the Hall table at the west end with the queue papers under it.”

“Whose hand is that.”

“A copyist's. It is not a hand that writes names. It is a hand that writes down what it has been given.”

“Then give it something. Write my name.”

He took a slip out of the tray and squared it and wrote three characters on the top of it, and then he turned it round.

They were in the wrong order.

“So,” said Ning Yan.

“Yes,” he said.

## What She Came For

She had a list. She had made it in the wash water at the fifth hour with her left hand, and it had four items on it, and the first was what paper lets a servant-born practise past registration, and the second was what it costs, and the third was who signs, and the fourth was whether she can get it before the thaw.

She had expected to be afraid of three sponsors. What she found in the chair across from him was a man who was afraid of three sponsors, and who had decided that the safest way to be useful to a person with a document and a name in it was to answer questions and stop there.

“What lets a servant-born practise past registration,” said Ning Yan.

The brush went on for another second.

“That is a Clause Four question,” he said.

“I do not know what that is.”

“No,” he said. “Nobody does, until they have one, and you are the first in this sect in my time to ask me for it, and I have been at this table eleven days.”

He went down the corridor — not out of the Hall, down it, twenty feet, to the press — and came back with a board under his arm and a thin grey book off a high shelf, and put the book down in front of her with the spine cracked at a page about two thirds of the way in, because people crack spines at pages they have been asked about.

“The Servant Advancement Settlement,” he said. “Eleven clauses and a schedule. Six hundred years old and printed twice in four hundred and some of the printing is not the same as the printing.”

## Clause Four

He read it in the voice of a man reading out a line he had read a great many times, and it went on for a page and a half, and the shape of it arrived before the words did, because he started with the exceptions and she had a habit of listening to the shape.

*A servant-born who has broken in the open, upon the hearing of the bound and in the presence of the Hall, may draw sect qi, may take paid work outside the crew, and may be taught past her stage — only under licence, countersigned at the county.*

Then the conditions, which were five, and then the sentence that had a number in it.

“Stop,” said Ning Yan. “Go back one.”

He went back one.

“*The licence is of three parts, and no part is the licence.*”

“Read that again.”

He read it again.

“Sponsors’ seal. Quota clearance. Countersignature at the county.”

“That is three.”

“That is three,” he said, “and any office in nine counties will tell you it is three, and I will tell you that in nine years I have watched a great many people look at three and see a road, and it is not a road. It is three rooms and you have to stand in the last one with your shoes on and nothing in your hands but the first two.”

He looked up. It was the first time he had looked at her and it lasted about a second and a half.

“The first is the maintaining members,” he said. “For you, that is three men with a seal and they are four streets from you. It is the hardest one and it is the one you will be offered most easily.”

“Why the hardest.”

“It is a paper about whether anybody will stand behind you. Everybody in nine counties can see whether three men have put a seal on a thing.” He went back to his clearing his throat. “The second is the quota. The quota office has to certify that the household owes nothing to the Bureau for the year and that the winter book is square. That is arithmetic. It is not a favour and there is nothing to be charming about.”

“And the third.”

“The third is the county office at Lowwater,” he said. “They stamp it. The registrar there is a man called Sun He and he has held the county book nineteen years and he does not stamp a thing he has not read twice. They copy it upward to something they never name, and then you are a person who practises, and you can take paid work outside the crew and be taught past your stage, and your stage can go up as far as the licence says and no further.”

“As far as what.”

“As far as it says.” He turned the board over. “I do not know. It is set per licence. It is not the same number for everybody and I have not been in a room where I was told why.”

## The Name of It

“What is it called.”

He said it as though it were a small thing, because to him it was.

“The Licence to Practise Past Registration.”

Ning Yan wrote it on the back of her hand with a wet thumb, in her own hand, in the wrong order, four times, so that her hand carried it for the rest of the day.

“The Hall,” she said. “You work in a hall that has no roof and eleven hundred disciples.”

“We work on paper,” he said. “The Hall is not a shelter. It was never a shelter, it is a room where paper is signed and it has not been a room where anybody lives since the third generation of the founding.”

“Noted.”

“You said noted.”

“I write things down,” said Ning Yan, and tapped the side of her coat, and did not explain what was in it, and watched his eyes go to the lining and come back very fast.

“Who witnesses the countersignature.”

“Does it have to be witnessed?”

“You asked the question. Answer it properly or I will not ask again and you will wish I had.”

He was quiet for long enough that she thought he was going to refuse, and then he said, “There is a form. It is not compulsory. It says the county prefers a person of standing in the trade to attest that the applicant is who the paper says she is, and the county has a standing sheet for it, and there is a name on that sheet for this district.”

“Feng Lushi.”

He stopped.

“You know the name.”

“I know the name,” he said. “Everyone knows the name. He has four hundred volumes and a cough and he has copied that announcement upward four times in a column nobody reads and everybody in this sect knows about it including men who have never been in his stair.”

She put that away without moving her face.

“He is a procedure,” she said. “That is all I am going to say about him today.”

## What It Costs Her

“Two parts I can tell you about and one I cannot,” he said.

“The quota. What is it in.”

“Hours.”

The whole room went cold at once, and it was not the open sky.

“It is counted in hours,” said the clerk. “Your household’s hours against the year. Every hour worked in the furnace crew counts to the household and comes off the year, and the household is square when the hours worked are equal to or greater than the hours asked. And the hours asked for this year are in a book in the quota office and I have never seen it.”

“By whom.”

“By a clerk of the quota office, on the third evening of the week, into a book that goes up on the first of the month.” He said it the way a man says a thing he has been told by somebody who was not him. “Your hours. Entered in ink. Counted slowly, on purpose, because a clerk who counts fast gets the wrong number and a clerk who counts slow gets the right number four days later.”

“Whose hand counts it.”

“Shen Xiu’s office keeps the winter book. His clerk writes it and he certifies it at the close of the month and nobody can call a number in that book wrong in nine counties except Shen Xiu.”

She sat with her hands flat on a clerk’s table in a building with no roof and did the arithmetic, and the arithmetic was very short.

Four months of furnace-crew labour. Entered in an enemy’s book in hours by a hand that does it slowly. Certified by a man who reported her early in the winter for his own reasons and never once asked her about it.

“All right,” she said. “How much is four months.”

“I do not know.”

“How much of a month.”

“I do not know that either,” said the clerk. “Nobody knows. It is the number that makes the books square and it is set at the close of the year by people in a county office on the far side of eleven days of road and it does not come up the mountain to be explained.”

She looked at the book with the spine cracked in it for a moment longer, and then she took the charcoal stub out of her left sleeve where it had been since she was fourteen years old, and she did not take the book away.

“Say the clause while I write it.”

He said it. She wrote it on the inside of a laundry docket in the hand she used for the wash code, which is a child's slanting scrawl and cannot be read quickly by anybody, and she got the three parts in and the five conditions and the words *countersigned at the county*, and she wrote it out twice and checked it twice and checked that it could not be read quickly.

“Thank you,” said Ning Yan.

“Do not thank me, it is a page.”

“You have been at this table eleven days,” she said, “and the man who was at it before you read a record out in a building with no roof and wrote a word in it that he was not asked to write. I know what happened to him and I am not going to pretend I do not. So. Thank you.”

He looked at the floor of a hall with no roof for a second and then he squared the grey book on the table and put his hand flat on it.

“You will need the schedule certified,” he said. “The furnace schedule, attached to the application, with the hours entered. Not today. Today you have nothing to attach.”

“I have the seal leg.”

“You have three men who will put a seal on a piece of paper,” said the clerk. “That is not a leg. That is a promise to sign a leg. And when they do it your name goes into the county queue, and the county queue is a public thing, and there will be nine names in front of you and behind you, and every one of them will know that a servant-born of Qinghe is being examined for a licence.”

He said it kindly. He said it the way a man says a thing to a person he cannot do anything for.

She put the docket into her coat and stood up, and it was at that point, in a building with no roof, with the wheel-noise still coming up off the cut outside, that she understood that she had walked in at the ninth hour with four items on a list and was walking out with one, and that the one was the *name of a thing*.

“Clerk.”

“Mm.”

“How much is the quota leg in hours.”

He put the brush down in the rack. He squared the grey book. He looked at the open floor of the hall where the sky was, the way a man looks at a place he has decided not to go.

“I have been at this table eleven days,” he said, “and I have been asked that question twice this morning by somebody who has not come up the stairs.”
