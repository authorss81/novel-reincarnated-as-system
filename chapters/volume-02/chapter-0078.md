# What the Bound Is Told

She took the second stage on the settling floor at the second night hour with five men in the hole and her left hand on the rail.

The night had the shape nights have when the thaw runs all day and the frost comes back after dark. Water stood in the channel and the channel ran cold, and the foreman had six men posted on the board with his name on the line and six in the hole for the first time since the beam. The timber over the cold end stood sound with his charcoal mark on it. Nothing was wrong.

That was the horror and the mercy of it. The work was ordinary and the body was at its edge and the edge gave.

It gave the way a stuck door gives, without sound. She was holding the wall straight through the crack, breathing two counts in and down into the plates, when the air went where it had not gone for weeks. The stripe along her ribs went hot, then cold, then quiet. The heat took the wall and held it. Her legs did the turn from the yard form without being told, weight low and left hand leading, and the channel cleared in one pull.

She knew before she stood upright. She had been gathering toward this since the fifteenth day, through five and a half weeks of one-handed tithe and a rebuilt form and cold practice behind a tower. The body keeps its own book and it had just closed a page.

She finished the hour. That was the first thing. The wall stood straight and the hole worked and she did not leave a lip to lean for the sake of what she felt under her ribs. She held the channel till the shift changed, signed nothing early, and went up the stair with her coat shut over the leak that had gone quiet for the first time in four months.

Yuan had said nothing through the whole of it. That was how she knew he knew.

## The Terrace

She went to the terrace because a stage taken on a floor belongs to the floor, and the release of it belongs to the bound.

The Ninth Steps stood empty at the fourth night hour with frost on the treads and the town lights below. She climbed to the sixth step and stood with her hands on the cold rail, breathing the short breath that had been hers for a week and would be hers for longer now. The tablet lay flat against her chest. The log lay in her lining. The refused slip lay under the tithe book cover with three seals showing.

“You feel it,” said Yuan. “You know it and you hold it still.”

“I feel it,” said Ning Yan. “I hold it till you speak. This one is not mine to release.”

“It is not yours,” said Yuan. “It is mine and I carry it.”

She waited. Four months past the bound had learned she existed in a roofless hall with the rain coming in. Now it would learn again what she had become, and every man buying proof would have a number to buy against. The man in the good coat paid for records at county desks. A stage is a record no clerk can lose.

“Do it,” she said. “You do it now and you do it plain.”

He did it. He let go of what he had been holding since the second night hour, the report made and then released, and the layer took it and spoke it to every ear inside the bound at once.

[Assembly Hall of Qinghe. Ning Yan of the Qinghe furnace crew, servant-born. What is carried past the first is let go. Condensing, stage two, at Qinghe is held.]

The sound went out over nine counties the way announcements go, audible and nowhere, in a voice that is not a voice. She stood on the sixth step and heard it go, and heard it finish, and stood inside the quiet after it with her hands on the rail.

She was furious. It came up through the quiet like heat off a wall, clean and entire. He had decided it, not her, four months after deciding the first one without asking, and the third term stood between them with its plain words: nothing decided about her without telling her first. He had told her. He had told her by doing it in front of her, the way the layer does everything, by announcement and not by asking.

“I do not thank you,” she said.

“You do not thank me,” said Yuan. “I ask nothing and I take nothing.”

“You tell me now,” said Ning Yan. “You tell me what it costs and you tell me plain.”

“It costs fever,” said Yuan. “It costs breath. You burn for two weeks and you breathe short for a month. The breath stays short after. I see it in you now.”

Two weeks of fever. A month of short breath that never fully leaves. She took it standing on the step with frost under her boots, the way she takes hours, without arguing. The stage was hers. The release was his. The cost sat between them where costs sit in this book, in her body.

“You let it go,” she said. “Four months after the first, you let them tell it again. Every buyer in Lowwater has my number now.”

“They have it,” said Yuan. “I hold you still and I do not hide it.”

“You do not hide it,” said Ning Yan. “You never hide it. That is the use and the price of you.”

## Fever's Start

The fever came by morning.

She woke in the second bunk with her face hot and her hands cold, and the quarters smelling of coal and sleep, and nine men getting up round her. She tested her right arm twice because the rule allows twice, and it answered once and not the second time, and she got up and dressed left-handed and went down to the floor.

The shift leader took one look at her and pointed at the bench.

“Bench,” he said.

“Channel,” said Ning Yan. “Four hours. If the wall leans, bench me yourself.”

“You burn.”

“I burn and I work,” said Ning Yan. “The book does not weight for burning.”

He gave her the lip because there were nine men and a settling job, and she held it for three hours before the fever took the fourth apart. The wall leaned twice. She sat the second half of the block on the bench with her head down, calling the channel while another hand worked it, and the shame of it was worse than the heat.

At the table the clerk looked at her face and said nothing. She signed four and a half hours with a hand that shook, and he entered it slow and blotted it and turned the book back.

“Three hundred and nineteen worked,” he said quietly. “Six hundred and twenty-one standing.”

She nodded and went up the stair to lie down with the tablet burning against her skin. The girl at the wash house sent up willow tea by a boy, and she drank it because refusing it would make it a favour, and slept through the middle of the day with the arithmetic ticking behind her eyes.

Three hundred and nineteen. Six hundred and twenty-one. Thirty-six in her own hand.

“You rest,” said Yuan. “You sleep now and I watch.”

“I sleep,” said Ning Yan. “I work tomorrow. You watch the breath and you tell me true.”

“I watch it,” said Yuan. “I tell it true and I soften nothing.”

She slept. The bound talked round her in nine counties about what she had become, in the exact words the layer had given it, and the man in the good coat wrote down a number and put it with the two things he had been refused, in the order he had been refused them. She had paid for visibility with fever and breath, in ink no clerk could lose.

She did not thank him. She did not stop him either. That was the whole of it, and it would have to be enough.
