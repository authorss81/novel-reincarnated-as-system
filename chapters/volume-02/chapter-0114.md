# Glad of the Noise

She took the fifth stage on the cold end of the furnace floor at the fourth hour of the afternoon, with the channel running and nine men on the other two hundred yards of it, and she did not ask for the floor and she did not take it on the terrace.

She had learned in the winter what a body does when it is emptied on purpose, and what it costs to be emptied on purpose at the wrong hour, and she was not going to do the careful version again with a hole in her record.

The edge came up out of nine days of load the way a held breath comes up out of a chest that has been full for eleven days. The stage sat in her at the fourth and the fourth was holding a sentence in a man who had no hands, and the fifth came up through it and found it and went round it the way water goes round a stone in a channel.

Then it went past the fourth. That was the part she had not asked about.

The crack opened the whole way down, the whole length of the left ribs, cold and wide and doing work with nothing asked of it, and the heat that came up out of her was not the heat of a body releasing something.

It was the heat of a body being let go of something it had been holding since the autumn. It came up through the floor plates and put a smell out of the settling floor that four men on the far end of the room looked up at.

The whole channel went at once. She felt it go the way she had felt the channel tear on a road at the foot of a tower two months ago, except that this time it went where it was supposed to go, and the mouth of it was the ceiling she had signed for herself in her own hand in a county office, and for one breath she could feel the paper on the top of the fifth stage the way a man feels a roof.

She came back into herself on the floor with her cheek on cold brick and both hands out flat and the breath in her at two counts.

She did not lose consciousness. She had expected to lose consciousness. She had budgeted for the whole of an hour on the floor with nine men standing over her deciding what a man does with a woman who has gone down on the cold end of his floor.

“Five,” said Yuan.

“Five,” said Ning Yan. “That is five.”

The third term was standing between them with its plain words and she had used it four months ago on a terrace and had meant every one of them.

“Let it go,” she said. “I say it as an order. You let it go now, out loud, in the four clauses, and you let it go while I am standing up, and you do not wait until I am on a step.”

“You say it and I let it go now,” said Yuan. “I hear you. I let it go.”

It went out over nine counties in a voice that is not a voice, while she was getting up off the floor with her left hand on the wall, and she never heard it and never would. It was said once, in the place where it is said, and every county took it in its own time in the words of whoever read it out.

It was not the sentence from the terrace in December. That one had said what was carried past the first was let go, and the one after it had said what was carried past the second, and a third one in that shape would have been a clerk copying his own last line and she would have gone up the mountain with a filing copy full of a boy's handwriting.

This one said nothing further is let go.

Nine counties heard a woman reach the end of what they would let her do, in a town where the biggest thing anyone was saying about her was that a servant-born had come up through four stages in one winter on a broken aperture and a licence nobody could name.

A county clerk writing the third of it out got told the ceiling and thought it was a figure. A good coat on a square four miles down the road heard a number and a place at once, and wrote both down, and that was the whole of what the noise bought and it was exactly enough.

Feng's clerks on the tower stair at the fourth hour of a day in the spring heard it too, four miles off and in the wrong place, and one of them said it out loud for the room, and the room did what rooms do.

## The Noise

That was the whole of the bargain and she had made it in the mud and had been carrying it since.

The noise was the record. There was no other way for a stage to get into a county book, and a county book was the only thing in nine counties that could stand between a woman and an office that did not want her standing.

If the layer said nothing, the county wrote nothing, and a county that has written nothing has nothing to strike, and she would have stood up a filing at the end of the thaw with one, two and three on it and a registrar deciding on a morning in a month whether a hole was a breach.

She had held a stage back for eleven days so that a market would not price her on the morning she buried Du Hong. It had cost her a stage off every book in the bound and nine days of not eating and it had not cost her one hour of paper, because a filing with a gap in it and a filing with a lie in it are two different animals, and she had chosen the gap.

This was the other half of the choice, and it cost the other thing.

“Let me be plain with you,” said Yuan, “because you are going to want to know what it bought and I am not going to let you find out later. There are nine counties and a tower four miles down the road and a county office on a square with a clerk at the side of it who has your mark on two documents. That sentence has just told all of it where you are standing, tonight, in the rain of a market day.”

“I know,” said Ning Yan. “I told you to say it. That is the fourth thing I have done this year that I cannot get back, and it is the first one I have been glad of.”

“You are glad of it.”

“I am glad of it,” she said, “and I have not been glad of anything since the sixth of the winter, and I would like that noted somewhere, and there is nowhere to note it, so I have noted it here, standing up, in front of nine men who will not sign my shift.”

Two hundred yards away, on the other end of a settling floor in a room two hundred and forty hands had gone through in a day, nine men had stopped working.

They were looking at her. Not at the floor and not at the wall. At her, standing up off the cold end with her right arm out to the side and her left hand on the brick and her chest moving at two counts, in the middle of a smell of hot stone that four men on the far end of the room had smelled and had looked up at.

The foreman took his pipe out of his mouth.

“That was the bound,” he said.

“That was the bound,” said Ning Yan.

Nobody came over. Nobody said anything. The shift finished at the second night hour and the men went up the stair and one of them carried her channel bar because she could not lift it with one arm and did not ask.

At the top of the stair in the dark she stopped, for the length of four counts, and listened to a bound that had just been told where she was.

“If I had held it,” she said, “there would be nothing on any paper in nine counties that I had reached five, and the ceiling on the county sheet would be a number I had never arrived at, and a licence countersigned at a number I had never arrived at is a licence for a body I do not have.”

“Yes,” said Yuan. “That is what it is.”

“Then it is worth the noise,” said Ning Yan, “and I am glad, and I am not going to stand up here and pretend to a man with no hands that I am glad because it is the right thing to do for the filing.”

“You are not pretending,” said Yuan. “You are standing in the dark on nine stairs being glad, and I can hear it, and it is the loudest thing you have said to me since the ninth step.”

She went to her bunk. Ten sleepers in twelve bunks and a lamp going at the foot of the stair and a licence that did not exist yet, and the fourth stage a hole in her record that nobody on earth could ever fill, and above her the roof of the Hall four hundred yards off with rain going through it onto a floor with nothing on it.

Six weeks to the return. Four days down. The channel under about three-quarters and thinning by ordinary use, and there was nothing in the layer tonight that had cost her anything except that she had told the truth out loud, twice, in the dark, to a man who cannot be thanked and cannot be forgiven and cannot be told to stop.
