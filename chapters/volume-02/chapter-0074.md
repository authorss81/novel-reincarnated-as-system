# What the Body Owes

She woke before the bell with the cold stripe burning under her coat and her right hand open on the blanket.

It was open the way it gets when the eleven minutes come in the night and go without waking her. She tested it once against the edge of the bunk, closing the fingers round the rail, and felt the grip hold and then thin. She counted eleven breaths and on the ninth the fingers loosened. She let go on the tenth and did not test it again.

Twice a day. That was the rule she had made on the seventh day past, and she keeps the rules she makes for her body the way she keeps tallies, because a number taken every hour is a number that takes the hour.

The quarters held nine men breathing and one empty place at the foot of the stair where two bunks had been drawn together to make room for coal. The room slept ten now where it had slept twelve. She dressed left-handed in the dark, seated the tablet flat against her chest, checked the log in the lining by touch, and went down to the floor for the ninth hour.

The melt had come back in the night and then frozen again, so the yard was iron on top and mud under. The furnace breathed through its mouth with the steady pull it has in midwinter, and the tithe table stood four feet from it with frost on the trestles and the big book shut against the damp.

## The Bench

The shift leader met her at the door with a look she had seen four times that winter.

“Bench today,” he said. “Plates from the bench. You watch the channel and you call it.”

“No,” said Ning Yan.

“You coughed blood yesterday.”

“I coughed iron,” said Ning Yan. “Every hand on this floor coughs iron by the sixth hour. You put me on the bench and four men are short, and the settling job is still open, and I am not the reason it stays open.”

He stood downhill of her the way he stands of everything, and his mouth did the thing that is not quite a smile.

“You are one arm,” he said. “One arm and a leak at the rim. I have nine men and a boy. I am not spending you to prove a point.”

“Then do not spend me,” said Ning Yan. “Use me. The channel. Four hours. If the wall leans, bench me yourself.”

He looked past her at the floor, at the five men going into the hole with stone on their backs, at the new timber over the cold end with his own charcoal mark on it. Then he nodded once, short, and gave her the lip.

“You lean, you sit,” he said.

“I lean, I sit,” said Ning Yan.

She took the left side of the lip and put her hand flat on the cold rail. The heat came off the wall in its leaning sheet, toward whoever stood nearest, and she opened the crack and let it go down through her into the plates instead of out. The rim leaked sideways as it always did. The cold stripe woke and spread two fingers wide and then settled into the ache she had learned to work inside of.

The first hour was clean. The second hour her arm came back and she ran the hook with it for eleven minutes, plate and chain in order, and planned the whole floor eleven minutes to the left. For eleven minutes she was at seven parts in ten. For fifty she was at full with one arm. By the third hour she had the bad minutes in the hour down to eight, and the wall stood straight twice as long as a breath held it.

At the fourth hour the shift leader came down the lip and stood behind her without speaking. She felt him watch the wall, not her, for forty seconds and then another forty. The wall did not lean.

“Good,” he said. “Plates now. Two hours. Then go up.”

## Iron and Air

By the fifth hour the iron was in the front of her throat and would not clear.

She had carried iron in her chest since the autumn, and the cough that started on the sixth stair, and both were worse since the stage had moved under her skin. She had not yet taken the second stage. The body knew something was near that she did not yet have a name for, and it told her in the way bodies tell, by making the ordinary work cost more.

She worked the plates left-handed, hook under and lift and carry, stack and return. The plates did not care about her arm. The men beside her had stopped offering to carry for her a week past, which was kinder than the bench, and she took the rhythm they gave her and matched it.

“You breathe short,” said Yuan. “You slow down now and you breathe.”

“I breathe when the plates are stacked,” said Ning Yan. “You count the minutes and you keep them quiet.”

“I count them,” said Yuan. “I keep them with you and I say nothing else.”

At the eighth hour she went to the table. The clerk did three men ahead of her slowly, checking each line against the floor board, his broken finger making the brush drag on the downstroke. When her turn came he turned the book without being asked.

Her line stood at thirteen and a half in her own slanting hand, with *member on schedule* against it. She signed four and a half more for the morning block, blotted it, and watched him enter it against the twenty-fourth day.

“How does the book stand,” she said.

He looked at her for a moment, the way clerks look when they decide how much of the truth is theirs to give.

“Two hundred and forty-seven worked,” he said. “Six hundred and ninety-three standing. Against nine hundred and forty asked.”

She did the subtraction without moving her face. Thirty-six hours in six days from the whole household, eighteen of them hers. The floor was working short-handed and still moving the number. The number did not know about her arm.

“It moves,” she said.

“It moves because hands work,” said the clerk. “Whether I write it fast or slow.”

“Write it slow,” said Ning Yan. “Slow is right.”

## The Second Block

She ate in the quarters at midday standing up, because sitting down in the middle of the day is a thing that makes the second block harder. The nine men ate round her and talked about the settling timber and the price of oil and a carrier who had lost a wheel on the Lowwater road, and none of them talked about the burial. That was correct. The burial had its entry in the register and the roster had its marks, and the floor had its work.

At the fifth hour past midday she went down again for the evening block. The channel was skinned with cold where the night air got in at the door crack, and a skinned channel does not run. She opened it with her left hand on the rail and her shoulder into the work, and the heat took the skin off in ten minutes.

Her arm came back once in four hours and went again. The leak ran and the stripe burned cold. The cough came and she swallowed it, because coughing on the lip breaks the hold and breaking the hold leans the wall. At the second night hour she signed again, four and a half more, and the clerk entered it with the lamp low.

Twenty-two and a half would have been the count if the week had closed yesterday. Eighteen now in her own hand on the new page, with the nine behind it on the old. Two hundred and forty-seven worked in the winter book. Six hundred and ninety-three standing.

She walked up the track with her lungs going like a bellows with a hole in it and her coat stuck to the stripe. The wash house lamp burned below the stair. The girl of twenty-four sat in its light with the lath splint on her leg and a slate on her knee, copying figures in a slow hand.

Ning Yan stopped in the doorway.

“You should be lying down,” she said.

“I lie down all day,” said the girl. “The healer says the bone knits lying down and the household says the hours knit standing up. I copy slates sitting up and both are satisfied.”

Ning Yan looked at the slate. It was a carriers' list, names and marks, and the girl's hand was careful round the names.

“The gate man asked again,” said the girl. “Yesterday. Where you sleep. The carriers' names on the papers. The clerk told him nothing. I heard it from the woodpile.”

“I know,” said Ning Yan. “He gets nothing from that clerk.”

“He gets nothing from me either,” said the girl. “I sleep under this roof and I know which bunk is yours and I have said nothing to any man in a good coat.”

Ning Yan nodded once. There was nothing to thank her for that would not make it smaller, and she had learned that winter what thanks cost.

“Then sleep,” she said. “Copy nothing after the ninth hour.”

She went up the stair and lay in the second bunk with the arithmetic for company. Two hundred and forty-seven. Six hundred and ninety-three. Eighteen in her own hand on the new page.

“You pay too much for it,” said Yuan. “You rest now because I ask it.”

“I rest,” said Ning Yan. “I do not stop. There is a difference and you know it.”

“I know it,” said Yuan. “I watch it and I learn it.”
