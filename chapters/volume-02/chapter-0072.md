# Two Names in the Entry

The frost came back in the night and took the mud out of the ground by morning, so that the top of it was hard and two inches under the top of it was not, and that is the condition a grave wants and the condition that has to be got right fast.

The burial ground of Qinghe is on the slope above the Lowwater road, four hundred feet down from the terrace and three hundred more down from the furnace, and it is a terrace of flat stones in two rows with the road going past the bottom of it, and it is where eleven hundred people have been going for six hundred years.

It is the correct ground. There is nothing to complain about. It is nine minutes from the furnace door down a track that is a river in a melt, and it is four hundred and eleven feet from the gate of Qinghe, and from the gate it is visible, and the whole sect can stand on the top row in about an hour and a half.

They buried two men in about nineteen minutes.

The sect does furnace dead quickly and quietly. That is not cruelty; it is the arrangement that has kept nine hundred hands on a furnace for six hundred years, because a furnace does not stop for a funeral. There was no bell. There was a hole and there were six men and there was a form on a board.

The man with the board was about seventy and had been the burial clerk of Qinghe for a generation and had a name she had heard on a page once and did not use.

“Name,” he said to the men carrying the first one, and they said it, and he wrote it, and she did not hear either of them say it, and then she looked at the board and saw it written in a slow old hand.

*Cao Lei.*

She had known the name for four months. She had heard it every day of her first winter on that floor, in a dozen registers, off nine mouths, in the half of her that never stopped listening. She had never once, in four months, put it into her mouth on purpose.

She went down the flat stones.

“Twenty-two years of the Qinghe furnace crew,” said the man with the board. “Mother dead, no issue. Father a furnace-man, unsettled. Name for the record.”

Nobody answered.

The man with the board waited about four seconds and then wrote a line in the cause column, because that is what the column is for, and he wrote it in the way a clerk writes a thing he has written nine hundred times: *slag beam, settling floor, second night hour.*

Then he stopped and looked at her.

“Mind if I say the next one first.”

“Say it first.”

He looked at the second board.

“Xu An,” he said. “Twenty-six years of the Qinghe furnace crew. Eleven years on the floor. Mother living in the eighth estate. Father dead. Name for the record.”

Nobody answered again.

He wrote it and he took the brush to the cause column and he waited with the tip of it about an inch off the paper, and there were nine men of that crew standing on the top row in a wind, and an old man of seventy with a brush he had been holding since before she was born.

“Cause,” he said.

## The Cause

“They took the crew roster,” said Ning Yan.

Nobody in that burial ground said anything at all, and there was a wind, and the wind went along the flat stones.

“Eight days ago two officers of the maintaining members came up the stair of our quarters with an instrument for an examination of crew effects and I refused to consent to the examination of my bunk, and I refused it aloud, and they honoured it.”

She said it in the voice she used for tallies. She had been told in the autumn that this was the voice she used when she was reading tallies to eleven women who did not want to hear them, and she used it now in front of nine men who very much did not want to hear it.

“They did not come for the plate. I know that they did not come for the plate, and they told me so while they were packing up, and I want that said at this burial so that nobody repeats afterwards that they came for my goods.”

“They came for an inventory of the debris of the trial. I would not let them into my bunk, and it is legal, and it held, and it is still held, and I would do it again this morning.”

“And then they took the roster. The board. Because a board is sect property and I am not, and because the instrument is silent on a board, and when an instrument is silent on a thing, the thing is taken.”

She got to it.

“The board had two men's marks against a shift that was mine. That is all it said. It did not say why. It said two men of the crew had taken my ninth hour to the second night hour. It said it for eleven days. And it said it against a schedule that was certified out of the same two books on the sixteenth day at the ninth hour of the morning.”

The old man’s brush was an inch off the paper and had not moved.

“A foreman with a settling job he was short-handed for, and no men to put on it, read that board and put those two men on the settling work doubled for every shift from the eighteenth day to the close of the thaw. He did it in eleven minutes. He did it because the two of them were on a schedule that did not balance, and the balancing falls on whoever is standing nearest it.”

“A beam that should have been shored came down on the settling floor on the second night hour of the twenty-first day and it came down on two men who were on a shift that was standing in for mine, on a shift that was false, in a hole that four men should have been in and was four men in, and I could not get to it in time.”

She stopped.

“I am not going to say the rest of it in a softer voice than that.”

“You are not,” said Yuan.

“You were going to.”

“No,” he said. “I was going to say that the beam was wrong because he was short of hands and that it is not hers and that it never was, and it is true, and it is also a way of putting one of them on a different shelf from the other, and she has just told me out loud in front of eleven people that she is not going to allow it.”

“I told you on a channel floor at the fourth hour of the morning.”

“Yes,” he said. “And it is the same rule and you are keeping it in front of witnesses this time.”

## The Cause Column

The old man’s brush came down onto the paper.

He wrote for about a minute and a half and the wind took the wet off it, and she read it standing where she could read it, upside down, the way she read the debt paper over a clerk’s tray.

*Struck by slag beam, settling floor, second night hour of the twenty-first day.*

And under it, in the same slow old hand:

*Shift was stood by two men of the crew against a member's shift upon a roster the member refused examination of. Beam not shored to standing. Cause named by the member at the burial.*

Nine lines.

“Did you ask her for that,” said Xu An’s mother.

She was a small woman of about fifty who had come down the track at the fifth hour with eleven people and had not said anything at all since the bottom of the track, and she had been standing about eight feet away with her hands in her sleeves for the whole of it.

“Yes,” said the burial clerk.

“You were not to.”

“It is in the column and it is in her hand and she is a member of this sect and she has the standing. I was not to refuse it,” said the old man, “and I would have put it in if I had not been asked, because I have been doing this for a generation and that is the only column I have ever had and I have never once written *fell* in it in my life.”

The small woman looked at her for a long time.

Then she said, “You will not be at my door.”

“No,” said Ning Yan.

“I am not saying that as a kindness.”

“I know,” said Ning Yan. “It is a statement about what is going to happen and you are right and I am not going to argue with you about it in front of your son.”

The old man had put the brush down.

## The Register

Afterwards they put her on the north step of the Hall with the board of the register and the burial book open on her knees, because the entry of a death and the entry of the record of a death are two different documents in a place with nine hundred hands in it, and the second one is the one that gets read.

She wrote with her left hand.

It came out in the ugly slanting way a left hand writes and it took her four minutes to do the two names and it took her the better part of an hour to do the rest of it, and there was a clerk sitting at a table four feet away who did not read over her shoulder and who offered her the brush twice, because it is a bad brush and a clerk knows it is a bad brush.

*Entered on the twenty-second day at the burial hour by Ning Yan, maintaining member of Qinghe: that Cao Lei and Xu An, of the Qinghe furnace crew, were ended on the second night hour of the twenty-first day beneath a slag beam on the settling floor, in work doubled upon them by the order of the floor foreman, upon the settling floor of this sect, where the strength required is six and the strength posted is four;*

*that the shift they were working stood for the shift of the undersigned, entered upon the crew roster under the marks of those two men upon the sixteenth day of this month, at the eighth hour of the morning, in the presence of two officers of the maintaining members and eleven witnesses;*

*that the undersigned was at the Hall on that morning and took the hours of the ninth hour to the second night hour for the certification of a schedule, and was not on the floor;*

*that the undersigned refused the examination of her effects on the sixteenth day at the eighth hour and again at the tenth, and that the officers honoured it, and that the crew roster was taken in place of the examination, and that the undersigned made no objection to the taking of the roster and had no right to make one;*

*that the beam was not shored to standing and that the shoring was signed off by the foreman in the seventh hour of the twentieth day;*

*and that the undersigned caused both of these things and entered them herself and asks that this entry be read whenever the roster is read.*

She read it back to the clerk, who wrote it fair.

It is the first document she has ever written that says she did something.

In four months she has put her hand under a man's name to make herself expensive, and she has sworn a false thing to forty-one witnesses, and she has made a boy put his life on a piece of paper and called it protection, and she has been frightened of all of it and none of it had her in it as the reason.

This one has her in it as the reason, in her own hand, in a register that the sect keeps for four hundred and eleven years.

## The Last Hour

She carried the refused licence slip down to the tithe table at about the seventh hour of the evening.

It was folded in four and it had been in her coat for two days and it had a county clerk's hand on it and nine lines on it and the three sponsors' seals on it above the nine lines.

The clerk with the broken finger looked at it and then at her.

“That is not a schedule.”

“No.”

“That is not a furnace sheet and it is not a household line and it goes in no book I have.”

“No,” said Ning Yan. “Put it under the book.”

He did that. He put the corner of it under the cover of the tithe book at the side nearest the hand that writes, and it sat there with the three seals showing.

That is what she wanted. In about nine weeks a clerk at the quota office will open that book on a third evening and count nine hours against a member, and there will be a piece of county paper under his wrist telling him what the nine hours are for.

“Enter the reason,” she said. “In your own hand. On my line.”

“That is not permitted.”

“Then I will enter it and you will counter it.”

He looked at the book for about four seconds.

“Short,” he said, “and not permitted, and if I enter it the foreman asks me why a crew sheet has a county refusal under it, and I will have to tell him, and then it is a thing on a floor.”

“He already knows.”

“Then he already knows and I still have to answer for it.”

“Then write *member on schedule*,” said Ning Yan, “and that is true, and it is short, and he will not ask.”

He wrote it. *Member on schedule.* Four words in a clerk’s hand against the twenty-second day, and under the cover of the book with three seals on it, and the clerk put the brush in the rack and blew on the ink and did not say goodnight, which was correct.

## Nine Hours

She went down on the cold end at the ninth hour of the night because the frost had put a skin on the melt water and the channel was skinned over and a skinned channel does not run and a skinned channel has to be opened, and because there was a foreman who had signed a shoring at the seventh hour and nine men on a floor and about seven hundred hours in a book a hundred feet and eleven steps away.

There were five men in the hole.

The foreman had put five, which was one more than he had put four, and he had put it himself and not the board, and when she asked him about it at the lip he said, “It is not the same job,” and looked at the shoring, and she looked at the shoring too, and there were four posts over the cold end with a signature on them and nothing else.

“Shoring is yours,” she said.

“It is mine,” said the foreman. “It was signed and it stands and I will put six men in that hole in the morning and I will do it on the board this time so that there is a line with my name on it.”

“Good,” said Ning Yan.

She worked the channel for four hours with her left hand on the rail and the crack open and the cold stripe along her ribs, and she got eight bad minutes in the hour down to seven, and she did not look up once after the first ten minutes, because a woman on a cold end looking up is a woman on a cold end counting the hours to the close of the thaw.

Down at the wash house the girl of twenty-four had her leg in a splint made of a lath and a piece of cord, because that was what there was, and she would be off the settling floor for the rest of the season, and the difference would be taken out of a household that was not hers.

At the second hour a boy came up the track from the burial ground with a slate and a lamp and asked whether there was anything at the bottom of the ground that belonged to the quarters, and said that the old man with the board had sent him, and said it in the voice of a boy who has been sent.

“Six and eight,” said Ning Yan. “Six and eight a week is his wage, and there are thirteen weeks of it on that slate and not one of them has been drawn. Count them off and give me the sum.”

She stopped.

The coat was on the plank in the settling hall. There were men in that hall going through the things of two men who had been on a floor, and there were men paid to do it, and a wage slip in a dead man's pocket is not money. It is an unclaimed quarter of a furnace-crew year and it goes down a column, and the column on that coat was his father's, and his father was unsettled, and an unsettled column takes anything unspent that comes near it.

“Take this down to the old man,” said Ning Yan. “Tell him a quarter’s wage came off that coat and went into the furnace column of the father and that I will get it back out with my own hand in the morning, and tell him nobody is to open that coat tonight.”

The boy looked at her.

“Nobody is to open that coat,” he said.

“Nobody is to open that coat.”

He went down the track with the slate. She went back to the channel, where the frost was coming on and the water was making the noise of a channel that is neither skinned nor open, and she worked it for another two hours.

She did not think about a name again until the ninth hour of the morning, when she carried two pairs of boots and a wage slip down to a grave in the cold with nobody with her, because she had not asked for anybody to come.
