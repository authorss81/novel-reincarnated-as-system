# The Furnace Tithe

She had not been on the furnace floor for eleven days, and the first thing that happened at the fifth hour past midday was that the floor did not know her.

That was not a figure of speech and it was not unkind. Eleven days of Hall and terrace and a crew's quarters had taken her out of the counting, and the counting is not written anywhere, and a furnace floor counts a body the way a ledger counts a name: it knows how much it did at the ninth hour on the sixth day of the autumn and it expects the same tomorrow.

The shift leader came over inside the first minute.

He was about fifty and had been on that floor twenty-six years and had a way of standing that put him slightly downhill of whatever he was looking at, which is a thing you cannot teach. He looked at her right arm, and then at her face, and then at the middle distance, and his mouth did something that was not quite a smile.

“We will set you on the plates,” he said. “Nobody carries anything for a month. You watch and you move coal.”

“No,” said Ning Yan.

“There is the bench by the door. You can sit on the bench.”

“I have held a night shift.”

“You have held a night shift,” he agreed, “with four spare and me standing in the gap, and you will not be able to do that now, because the two men who were standing in the gap were called to the settling work at the first hour and will be back at the fourth.”

He was being kind. She had known men like him her whole life, and had worked under four or five of them, and the shape of the kindness is always the same: it takes the work out of your hands and calls it care, and it is impossible to be angry at, and it means that for the rest of your life nobody will know what your hands are for.

“I am not going to sit on the bench,” said Ning Yan, “and I am not going to be the reason four men are short, and if you set me on the plates I will move plates until you tell me I cannot, and then we will have a conversation and I will lose it, because I have lost four of those.”

He looked at her for about three seconds.

“All right,” he said. “The channel. Walk it slow.”

## The Channel

She found it in the third hour and she did not look for it.

The channel is the run where the slag goes, the long cut down the middle of the floor with a lip on each side that a man stands on while the heat comes off it in a wall. The wall is not straight. It leans. It leans toward whoever is standing nearest the lip, and the man nearest the lip is the man who is about to have his eyebrows gone.

You correct it by taking the heat off it. You take the heat off it by breathing it in and holding it under your own sternum for four counts and then letting it go down into the plates, and the plates take it and cool a little, and the wall stands straight for about forty seconds, and then it leans again.

She had learned that standing on that floor at fifteen with a coal scuttle in her hands, and she had learned it in the exact words of the wall in the kiln, and she had never once been the only person standing on that lip.

At the third hour she put her left hand flat on the cold rail of the lip, because the rail is what keeps a body from stepping backwards, and she opened the crack, and she let the heat come off the wall and go down through her into the plates instead of out.

It leaked at the rim. It always leaked at the rim; that was the price and it had been the price since the sixth day of the winter and it did not get cheaper. What it did was put the wall straight and keep it straight for about twice as long as a four-count breath did, and it did it without the two men either side of her having to know.

She worked the channel from the eighth hour to the second night hour.

She did not tell anybody on that floor what she was doing. Not the shift leader, not Cao Lei, who was on the lip beside her for three hours and asked her one question and got a short answer and did not ask again. Nobody saw a technique. What they saw was a woman working a channel slow on the left-hand side and keeping the wall straight by something she did not appear to be doing.

## What It Cost

By the fifth hour there was iron in the back of her throat and by the sixth there was iron in the front.

That was normal. That had been normal since the autumn and she had a whole chest full of it and a cough that started on the sixth stair. What was not normal was the rim. The crack was leaking, and the leak went the way leaks go, sideways and up, and by the ninth hour the left side of her ribs had gone from hot to a cold stripe about two fingers wide, and from the cold stripe to a place where touching her own coat hurt.

She had noticed the stripe twice a day for eleven days and had decided, on the seventh, that she would notice it twice a day and no more, because a number you take every hour is a number that can take the hour away from you.

So she did not look at it.

She also noticed, standing at that lip with her left hand on cold iron for six hours, that the floor had made an entirely different person out of her right arm.

That was the part that nobody warns a woman about. Her arm had come back a third of the time, and it came back on the channel, and it came back *serving*. She ran the plate hook with it for eleven minutes and it did what it was told for eleven minutes, and then it went out like a lamp being pinched, and the whole of the useful window was eleven minutes and there was no way on that floor to predict it.

She planned around it. She moved her whole working rhythm eleven minutes to the left, which meant that for eleven minutes in every hour she was at 70% and for fifty minutes she was at 100% with one arm, and by the second night hour she had that down to about eight bad minutes.

Nobody in that building could teach her that. It had taken eleven days of not being able to use anything to work out how to spend what she had.

## The Table by the Furnace Mouth

The tithe table was four feet from the furnace mouth, which was a thing she had opinions about and did not express.

There was no tithe book in the Hall. The Hall had ink and nine clerks and no arithmetic at all; the whole of what the Hall did was sign paper and let it go up. This was three planks on two trestles and a third plank with a groove cut in it for a brush, and the brush had been cut flat, and there was a book on it about the size of a door.

The clerk sitting at it was about twenty-three and had a finger that had been broken and set and was now permanently an inch too long on the end of the hand, and a cough that was worse than hers, and he was not writing.

He was not writing because he had four other men waiting to be written about and he was doing them on purpose slowly.

She stood at the table and understood the whole of it inside about ten seconds, standing up, in the heat coming off the mouth of the furnace.

He was not slow out of laziness. He was slow because a clerk who enters a number fast enters the number the shift leader says, and then at the close of the month the book has to balance, and the balance is his problem and not the shift leader's.

So he wrote down every hour of every shift from four separate men, and checked it against the floor's own board, and did it the slow way, because the slow way is the only way that ends with a book that balances.

He was protecting the book. It was the only thing he had and he was doing it with a broken finger and a cough in a room four feet from a furnace mouth.

“Name,” he said.

“Ning Yan.”

He wrote it and looked up and looked past her at the lip and then back at the book.

“That is not a name I have on this floor.”

“No,” said Ning Yan. “It is not.”

“Which is the point of the sheet,” he said, and turned the book round.

She had seen a version of that sheet a hundred times. Hours worked, hours asked, house against year. Nine columns and a rule at the top and a place at the bottom for a person to sign that they had been told what it said.

Below the columns, in a fair small hand that was nothing like his, there were six lines already.

*The hearths of this sect are worked by hand and the hours are the return. No hour worked is an hour asked. An hour worked is entered against the household and counts toward the year.*

She read it three times.

Somebody in this building had written that down on purpose. She looked at the six lines and then at the twenty-three-year-old with the bad finger and she knew, with total certainty and no evidence at all, that it was not him.

“You have read this,” she said.

“I copy it out for the floor,” he said. “It is on the wall at the quota office.”

“Then somebody in this sect wrote a thing that only makes sense to a person standing at a table where hours get entered.”

“Yes,” said the clerk.

“Who.”

“I do not know,” he said. “It came down on the fourth day past with the new book, and it is signed with nothing, and it is on the wall of the quota office in a frame, and there are eleven other sheets in the frame and none of them are signed either.”

The frame. Somewhere in a building she had never been in, on a mountain she could not leave, there was a frame on a wall with twelve unsigned sheets in it, and one of them said that the hours she worked were the hours the county counted at the countersignature, and somebody in this sect had known that eleven days before she had.

## Signing

“Sign at the bottom,” said the clerk, “and it enters.”

“Entered as what.”

“As hours.”

“As hours toward what.”

He looked at the top of the sheet, at the rule, at the six lines, and then at her.

“As hours,” he said.

She took the brush. It was a flat brush cut to a width so that the writing would be legible in about four inches of space, and it was the same brush shape as the one in Shen Xiu's hand in the autumn, when he had marked her as hurt instead of lost on a boundary bank.

She wrote with her left hand. The name came out in the ugly slanting way a left hand writes and it took her about four seconds and she did not apologise for any of it, and the clerk blotted it, and when she lifted the brush the six lines on the sheet were still the six lines on the sheet.

Four and a half hours. One shift. One night.

Two years ago Ning Bo's six years had been carried forward in a column and settled by being near the nearest unspent thing, and there had not been one hour of labour in that column anywhere. She had gone looking for labour in the furnace's whole history and had found that the furnace did not keep it, and the reason the furnace did not keep it was on a sheet on a wall in a building she had never been in.

She looked at her own name at the bottom of four hours of the year, written in her own hand, at a table in the middle of a furnace floor with the clerk's cough and the heat coming off the mouth.

“Then that is the first thing I have done for myself in four months that is on paper,” said Ning Yan, “and it is in his book.”

“Every hour you work on this floor is in this book,” said the clerk. “Whether I write it or not.”

“I know that. It is why I signed.”

She got her left hand off the table and stood a second with the heat on the right side of her face, because there was a thing she wanted to say and she had four months to decide whether she was going to say it to a clerk at a table.

“Say it slowly,” she said. “When they stand there. Because you are right, and you cannot be right if they check it against the board, and I want the number to be right when somebody reads it in four months.”

The clerk looked at her for about a second and a half.

“Thank you,” he said.

He said it the way you say it in a room where nobody thanks anybody, which is to say as though it had a cost, and she had said it four times in nine days and this was the first time it had come back.

## What She Kept

She went up at the second night hour with her lungs going like a bellows with a hole in it and the cold stripe along her left ribs exactly where it had been at the fifth hour, and she did not go to the Hall and she did not write anything in the log.

There was one line she could have put in the log in the wash code — six lines on a wall in a frame with eleven others, unsigned, and one of them saying the hours she worked were hours the county counted — and she looked at the inside of her coat where the docket with Clause Four was folded and did not write it.

Not because she did not believe it.

Because a thing that appears in the log is a thing she has to be able to say she saw, and she had not seen the frame, and she had been told about it by a clerk with a broken finger, and in four months a single one of those things was worth having a clerk's file to put it in.

She had spent four months learning that the cheapest thing to write down is the thing you cannot check.

## The Stair

She was through the door of the crew quarters at about the third night hour with her eyes streaming and her right arm out of its own weight and eleven men asleep in eleven bunks and one of them awake at the foot of the stair, waiting.

Cao Lei was awake.

“Two men came up at the second hour,” he said. “With a paper. From the maintaining members.”

“What paper.”

“About effects.” He said it carefully, because he had been eleven years on a furnace floor and he had heard what paper sounds like before it has been read. “They said it was about the debris. After the trial. About anything of the Hall’s that the crew may have picked up and kept in the quarters.”

He came down two steps so that she could hear him, which he had not needed to do, and which she was going to remember for a very long time.

“They want to walk the bunks,” he said, “in the morning. And they are going to start at yours.”
