# The Man Who Pays for Writing

The square learned her loads before it learned her name, which was the only mercy the pending filing offered.

Ning Yan carried stone in the morning and paper after the fifth hour, strap across her chest, breath short, eyes open. The three desks under the east awning ran their trade steady as hammers, and the man in the good coat moved desk to desk the way a brush moves in a fair hand. He paid before the clerk finished asking and thanked after the clerk finished writing, and the clerks wrote the asking as well as the answer because he paid for both.

She set stone down at the tower wall and took a carrier’s strap for the afternoon, because carriers hear more than stone carriers and the hours count the same under the schedule.

“You watch him,” said Yuan. “You learn him and you do not touch him.”

“I learn him,” said Ning Yan. “I carry and I listen. You watch with me.”

“I watch,” said Yuan. “I learn him through you, and I touch nothing.”

She went to the first desk last, when the light had come off the awning and the queue had thinned to two women with a bolt of felt between them.

The first desk kept the Qinghe advancement copies. The clerk there was young with ink to the wrist, and he told the man for coin that the copies sit with the county office and not with him. He wrote down that he was asked and did not have them. The man paid for the writing and thanked him.

Ning Yan stood four feet off with a strap in her hands and watched the clerk’s face while he wrote, because a clerk writing what he has just been told is a clerk doing his duty and a clerk writing what he has been given is a clerk making a record. This one had the second face. He wrote slowly, looked up once at the man in the good coat, and then put his brush down flat, which is what a man does at the end of a thing he would rather not have been asked to do.

She filed that away next to the fact that he had answered honestly and been paid for the answering.

The second desk kept carriers’ papers. The clerk there was older and tired, and he turned his book for coin and read out four names that had taken sealed packets up the road. Du Hong’s name was second. The clerk wrote the asking and the answering, and the man paid for both and asked to have the hours of the carrying set beside the names.

That was new. Ning Yan walked past with a load on her shoulder and her face doing nothing, because a face is a thing a man reads from nine feet.

The third desk kept household lines. The clerk shook his head before the coin came out. No house in the town had her entered against it, he said, because sect hands are not county lines. When the coin sat on the wood he wrote that too, and dated it, because the man pays for records of refusals as well as answers, and a dated refusal sits in a file and gets older.

Two of three, worked politely in daylight. The third unasked because the third is a body, and bodies walk through squares with straps across their chests.

She did the rest of the afternoon with the strap and no ear on anyone, which is harder than listening and which she had learned on a furnace floor where every ear in the room had a use. At the fourth hour the strap cut her left palm across the middle and she wrapped it in the lining of the coat and kept carrying, because a hand that stops is a hand somebody counts.

At the fifth hour the man closed his tube and walked toward the counting lane with a younger man beside him she had not seen before. The younger one walked the way men walk when they are done being polite at desks and are starting to be paid for feet. He was counting the square. Not the clerks, not the carts, the loads, the way a load went up a shoulder and the direction it went when it came down.

Ning Yan set her stone inside the tower wall so that it would not be a thing to watch her lift again, and marked both faces in wash code on the Clause Four docket, and went back up.

“How does it stand,” Du Hong asked in the loft. He had flour on his sleeves and a cut on his thumb from tower twine.

“He buys hours now,” said Ning Yan. “Not only pages. He wanted the hours set beside the carriers’ names.”

Du Hong frowned. “My hours are tower letters and furnace sheets. Entered. I sign them.”

“I know,” said Ning Yan. “Walk no road unentered. Carry nothing with no name on the sheet, even mine. If a clerk asks what you carry, you tell him the sheet and nothing else.”

“You told me that on the mountain,” said Du Hong. “I walked entered then and I walk entered now.”

She looked at him across the mat. Eighteen, generous, bad at lying, with her hand twice in his file and his name now in a county book for her attendance. The cheap door, standing open in lamplight eating soup.

“Then we attend together and we walk entered,” she said. “Stamp days and tower readings. Nothing unrecorded.”

“Nothing unrecorded,” said Du Hong. “I learned that watching you write.”

“Write it down,” said Ning Yan. “Not the asking. The standing. Tomorrow, at the county, you say your name aloud and you stand where I stand, and if anybody writes it wrong you correct it in front of them.”

Du Hong looked at the soup. “They will write it right. I have been written right my whole life and it has never once been worth anything.”

“Then it will be worth something on the day it is worth something,” said Ning Yan. “You will not know the day.”

He did not argue. He had learned, in the month since the mountain, that arguing with her was a way of being talked into the true thing, and that she would get there anyway.

In the evening she stood on the square edge where a person can stand a long time and be counted as waiting for something ordinary. The office was shuttered and the queue was a scuff of mud. The desks were bare except for one clerk putting covers on. The counting lane ran noise behind its barred windows, brushes going, figures called to a back room.

She had come down a pass to get onto a page with a county hand on it, and the man buying proof had understood paper better than she had. He was no longer buying where she slept. He was buying who carried for her and when, which is the road bodies walk on.

A carrier went past with a load she recognised as tower water and did not turn her head, and she understood that the only reason she did not turn her head was that she had counted his shoulders a moment earlier. She had counted a stranger's shoulders in a square at the fifth hour because there were two men at the lane mouth and a woman on the square with a strap, and the arithmetic of that had been done for her by somebody.

“You see the turn,” said Yuan. “You name it and you do not fear it alone.”

“I see it,” said Ning Yan. “Records to bodies. I keep the sheet between us and them.”

“You keep it well,” said Yuan. “I see you and I am where you put me.”

She went back up the tower stair with stops, and lay on the mat till the chest settled. The docket sat in her lining with the new asking in code. The filing copy sat beside it with two attendance marks.

She had one hour before the stamp, and she spent it washing the cut in her palm at the trough with cold water and her teeth, because a hand that bleeds onto a county sheet is a hand a clerk asks about.

The hunt had tightened while she carried, and she knew it by the shape of it rather than by anything she had been told. It was no longer asking which office kept her. It was asking who walked for her.
