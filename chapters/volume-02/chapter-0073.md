# The Tithe Ledger

The morning after the burial she went down to the furnace at the ninth hour because there was no other hour that belonged to her.

The frost had put a skin on the melt water in the night and the skin broke under her boots on the track. The furnace door stood open and the heat came out of it in a wall, and inside the wall nine men of the crew were already on the floor. She counted them from the doorway without meaning to count, and the count came out at nine, and she kept it.

Nine men. Ten sleepers. Two names gone out of the quarters and out of the floor and into a register.

The shift leader saw her at the lip of the channel and came over with his hands full of hook and chain.

“You are on the plates,” he said. “Four hours. Then the channel if the cough lets you.”

“I am on the channel,” said Ning Yan. “Four hours first. Then the plates.”

He looked at her face for a moment longer than the work needed. Then he gave her the channel without another word, because there were nine men where eleven had been and a settling job still open above the cold end, and a woman with one arm on the channel was better than an empty lip.

## The Floor Short-Handed

The hole above the cold end had five men in it by the time she got her left hand on the rail.

The foreman stood at the edge of it with a board under his arm and a brush behind his ear. He had promised six on the board in the morning with his name on it, and the board at the foot of the stair said six, and the hole held five.

She looked at the shoring posts on the way past and did not stop, because stopping is a thing a woman does when she has time, and she had four hours of tithe to earn before the clerk closed the sheet.

“You are looking at my posts,” said the foreman.

“I am looking at the floor,” said Ning Yan. “It is my floor too.”

“It stands,” he said. “Signed and standing. I put six on the board and I have five in the hole because one is carrying stone. He will be back before the second hour.”

She held his look for a second and then let it go. The beam over the cold end was new timber with a signature on it in charcoal, and the signature was his, and the timber was sound. There was nothing wrong with it that she could see, and she had learned in the last three days that seeing was not the whole of it.

“Then I will work under it,” she said.

She opened the crack a finger's width and let the heat come off the wall and go down through her into the plates. The leak ran sideways at the rim as it always did, a cold stripe two fingers wide along the left ribs, and the wall stood straight for twice as long as a four-count breath held it.

Nobody either side of her saw a technique. What they saw was a woman working slow on the left side and keeping the wall straight by something she did not appear to be doing.

By the second hour the iron was in the back of her throat. By the third it was in the front. Her right arm came back for eleven minutes in the middle of the second hour and served, hook and plate and chain in order, and then went out like a pinched lamp. She moved her whole rhythm eleven minutes to the left without thinking about it, because the floor had taught her that in the last week and the floor is a better teacher than any voice.

“You count that,” said Yuan. “You count it now and you keep it.”

“I count it,” said Ning Yan. “I keep it because I need it, not because you tell me.”

“You keep it because it is yours,” said Yuan. “I see it and I hold it with you.”

She did not answer him on the lip. The channel wanted both of her eyes and most of her breath, and he knew that, and he went quiet the way he goes quiet when the work is real, which is one of the few mercies he has.

## The Table

The tithe table stood four feet from the furnace mouth with the big book open on it and the clerk behind it with his broken finger and his worse cough.

She came up to it at the end of four hours with her coat stuck to her back and her left hand shaking. Four men waited ahead of her to be written about, and the clerk did them slowly on purpose, checking each line against the floor board, because a fast number makes the book unbalanced at month's end and the balance is his problem.

She stood and waited and did the arithmetic while she waited, because the arithmetic was the whole of the winter.

Two hundred and eleven worked to the eighteenth day. Seven hundred and twenty-nine standing. Nine of those in her own hand, two shifts of four and a half, and the rest in other hands against her household. The refused slip sat under the cover of that same book with three seals showing, where she had put it on the night of the burial, and the clerk's eye went to it once and away.

“Name,” he said when her turn came.

“Ning Yan.”

He turned the book toward her. The sheet for the week had her line on it with nine hours entered and the words *member on schedule* against it in his hand. Below the columns the six unsigned lines about hours stood in their fair small hand, saying what they had said on the fifteenth day, and she did not read them again because she had read them three times already and they had not changed.

She took the flat brush in her left hand and signed for four and a half hours. The name came out in the ugly slant she had learned to live with, and the clerk blotted it and entered the hour against the twenty-third day.

“Again tomorrow,” she said. “Two blocks in the week. The same as last week.”

“Two blocks,” said the clerk. “I write what the board says and I write it slow.”

“Write it slow,” said Ning Yan. “I want it right when it is read in spring.”

He looked at her for a second longer than a clerk looks at a hand. Then he nodded once and turned to the next man, and she went back to the floor for the plates.

She worked the plates for three hours with her left hand on the hook and her right arm dead at her side. The plates were heavy and plain and honest, and they did not care how many arms she had. At the end of it she signed again for a second block, four and a half more, and the clerk entered it without comment.

Eighteen hours in four days, with the nine she had. Thirteen and a half in her own hand now, if the counting is kept the way a household keeps it. Two hundred and twenty-nine worked in the winter book. Seven hundred and eleven standing.

She did the subtraction standing at the table with heat on one side of her face. The number moved. That was the whole of it. It moved because she had moved it and nothing else had.

## The Wage

At the eighth hour of the evening she went down the track to the settling hall with a slate under her arm.

The coats of two dead men lay on the plank where the paid men had left them, folded as coats are folded when nobody wants to be the one who went through the pockets. She had told a boy the night before that nobody was to open one of them, and nobody had, and the wage slip was still in the inner pocket where she had said it would be.

Six and eight a week. Thirteen weeks undrawn. A quarter of a furnace-crew year sitting in dead cloth, with the father's unsettled column waiting under it to take anything unspent that came near.

She took the slip out with her left hand and carried it to the quota office anteroom. The office was closed and the benches empty, but the night clerk at the furnace door knew the column business and wrote the figures on the slate without asking why a maintaining member was carrying a dead man's pay.

“Tell the burial clerk,” she said to the boy who had carried the lamp the night before. “A quarter's wage came off that coat and it does not go down the father's column. I get it back out with my own hand in the morning.”

The boy looked at the slate and then at her face.

“Nobody opens that coat tonight,” he said.

“Nobody opens that coat,” said Ning Yan.

She walked back up the track with the frost coming on and the channel water making its half-skinned noise behind the furnace wall. In the wash house at the bottom of the quarters stair a lamp burned, and through the open door she saw a girl of twenty-four with her leg in a splint of lath and cord, sitting up against the wall with a bowl in her hands.

The girl saw her and did not look away. She had been on the cold end when the beam came down, and she had heard the gate man ask where Ning Yan sleeps, and she knew he had got nothing. That was in her face without being said, and Ning Yan nodded once and went up the stair.

“You are tired,” said Yuan. “You rest now and you eat.”

“I rest when the book is closed,” said Ning Yan. “I eat what the quarters have. You watch the count and you tell me if it moves.”

“It moves,” said Yuan. “I see it move because you move it.”

She lay in the second bunk with the Clay Tablet against her skin and the log in her coat lining and the cold stripe along her ribs going from hot to cold and back. Nine men breathed in the room where eleven had breathed a week before. The arithmetic sat beside her like a third person in the bed, plain and ungiving and hers.

Two hundred and twenty-nine. Seven hundred and eleven to go.
