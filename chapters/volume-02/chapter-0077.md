# The Left-Hand Habit

The cold yard behind the furnace held frost till midday and mud after, and she used it the way she had used the ash flats in the autumn, which is to say after the work and before sleep.

She slept in the second bunk of the crew quarters and went down to the floor at the ninth hour every crew day, and the cart went down the cut on three cleared days without her on any of them, twice with her standing at the trestle watching it rope up and once with her nowhere in sight.

By the thirty-third day the household stood at three hundred and one worked. Six hundred and thirty-nine standing. Thirty-one and a half in her own hand.

She was five and a half weeks into the one-handed tithe. Her left hand had learned the hook and the rail and the plate, and her legs remembered something her hands did not, from a laundry manual's back page copied by a laundry-hand's son. The Nine-Step was gone as a technique and lived only as habit in her calves and hips. She had not written it anywhere and she had not told Yuan where it came from, and she was not going to.

What she needed was a left-hand form that could carry it.

## Cold Practice

She drilled in the yard at the fifth night hour with a lath for a hook and the cold getting into her chest, while the crew slept above her and the furnace breathed through the wall behind her.

The form she built was plain. Step and turn, weight low, left hand leading where the right had led for years. The first week it felt like writing with the wrong hand, which it was. The second week the legs stopped waiting for the hands and started moving first. By the third week she could hold the channel pattern in the yard without heat, breathing in and down into the plates that were not there, while the leak ran sideways at the rim and the cold stripe woke.

It cost breath. Everything cost breath that winter. Four counts in and the chest pulled short, as if the air stopped a finger short of where it used to go. She shortened to three counts and then to two, and the pattern held, because the pattern lived in order and not in air.

“You push too hard,” said Yuan. “You rest now and you breathe.”

“I breathe enough,” said Ning Yan. “I hold the form and I hold the hours. You watch the edge and you tell me when it moves.”

“It moves now,” said Yuan. “I see it and I say so.”

She stopped with the lath across her knees and breathed till the stripe settled. The quarters lamp burned in the stair window above her and the melt ran in the gutter behind the furnace wall with its half-skinned noise, and down on the floor a man coughed who was not one of the nine.

“What is the edge,” she said.

“You stand on it,” said Yuan. “You eat little and you work much and you breathe short. I know the signs because I read them in you.”

“Then read them,” said Ning Yan. “Read them and keep them. Do not decide them.”

“I read them,” said Yuan. “I keep them and I decide nothing.”

She went up to the quarters and lay in the second bunk with her coat for a blanket. The log lay in the lining with Clause Four and the refusal wording and the burial entry and the frame of twelve sheets in wash code.

The refused slip with three seals sat under the cover of the tithe book at the table four feet from the furnace mouth, where she had put it on the night of the burial and where the clerk's eye went once a week and away. She had not filed again. She had filed once and been refused, and the next filing waited on clearance the way spring waits on thaw.

## The Fifth Week

The fifth week of the new form took her apart and put her back plainer.

She held the channel at the cold end every crew day, because the cold end was where the form got tested, and then she carried plate stacks up from the settling floor on her left shoulder because no man had offered twice and the crew was short two.

The shift leader stopped offering the bench. The foreman signed six on the board and posted six in the hole, with his name on the line this time, and the timber over the cold end held sound. The clerk entered her blocks slow, four and a half and four and a half, and read her week back without comment.

The carrying built the legs the way stage eight had been built, out of load, and the form settled into the hips. The crack-channel practice in the cold steadied the heat work, so the wall on the furnace floor stood straight longer and the leak cost less to hold.

She ate little because the quarters had little in the second month of the thaw and she took the thin part on purpose, since a man who eats properly in March cannot hold a wall in March. Yuan could not make her eat and did not try, which was new and not kind.

He watched her shorten the counts and hold the form and sign the book, and he said nothing about the edge unless she asked, because she had told him to, and he keeps the rules she makes the way she keeps tallies.

On the thirty-fifth night she stood in the yard with frost on the lath and ran the form through twice without stopping. The second time through, at the turn where the weight goes low and the left hand leads across, something opened under the sternum the way a door opens that has been stuck all winter. No sound. No light. Only the ease of air going where it had not gone for weeks, and the stripe going hot and then cold and then quiet.

She stopped with the lath in her hand and knew what it was. She had been at stage one for four months with a mouth for an aperture and a dead arm, and the body had been gathering toward this since the fifteenth day, through every signed hour and every cold drill.

“Not tonight,” she said aloud to the yard. “Not in a yard with no witnesses and no book open.”

“You feel it,” said Yuan. “You know it and you hold it.”

“I feel it,” said Ning Yan. “I hold it till the floor sees it. A stage with no hours under it is a rumour.”

She went up the stair and slept with the form in her legs and the edge under her ribs. Three hundred and one worked. Six hundred and thirty-nine standing. Thirty-one and a half in her own hand, continuous from the day the book opened.

The bound did not know yet. It would know soon, because there is no private breakthrough inside it, and when it knows every man buying proof has a number to buy against. She lay in the dark and counted the cost coming the way you count weather, without flinching and without thanks.

The cart at the trestle would have had her name on the sheet at the tenth hour and her feet on the ground at the eleventh, and the arrangement was hers to use or leave. She was going to finish the form first. The edge was in the form, and a stage taken on a yard with no book under it would be a thing people argued about in a kitchen for a year.

Let him release it. Let him decide that one. She would be furious and she would not stop him. The third term held: nothing decided about her without telling her first, and this one the body had decided before either of them.
