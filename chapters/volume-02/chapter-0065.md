# Bunk by Bunk

The frost that morning was on the inside of the shutters.

Not on the glass. On the inside, in the corners, in a white fur about two fingers deep at the bottom of the slats, which happens when eleven men breathe in a closed room all night in a hard frost, and it meant that by the eighth hour the whole of the crew's quarters had a roof of white ice over the window side of it and everybody in it was breathing white and talking low.

They came up at the eighth hour, which was early, and that told her something.

Two men, both in Hall coats, both dry, both carrying the same sort of oiled satchel that a clerk carries when he expects to bring a document back with him wet. The one at the front was about forty and had a seal on the satchel and the other one had a folded sheet in his hand and a piece of string on his thumb to hold it.

The shift leader took the sheet.

“Effects examination,” he said, reading. “By the maintaining members, under the sponsorship instrument of the fifth day, for the purpose of the trial debris inventory. Crew quarters only. Any article of this building found in the keeping of a crewman is to be taken and entered.”

“That is a real paper,” said the shift leader.

“It is a real paper.”

“Then I will not stop it,” said the shift leader, and handed it back, and went and stood at the foot of the stair with his arms folded, because a man of twenty-six years' standing who reads the paper himself is a man who has done his part.

## The Regulation

The older of the two came up the four steps into the room.

“There is one thing on that sheet which I am obliged to say aloud before I begin,” he said. “It is in the fourth line and it is a courtesy, and no one in this room should be under the impression that it is negotiable by anybody in this corridor.”

The room went quiet in the way a room does when eleven men understand at the same moment that something has just been said about one of them.

“A maintaining member’s effects may not be examined without her consent,” he read. “Consent may be withheld. It may be withheld without reason, and the reason need not be given, and it may not be challenged by the instructing party.”

He folded the sheet.

“There is one maintaining member in this room,” he said. “Her bunk is the second on the left. I will ask her for it in front of these witnesses in about a minute and if she gives it, her bunk is opened like the other ten and there is a clerk present for everything that comes out of it.”

Nobody in that room breathed.

“If she does not give it, her bunk is not opened,” said the inspector, “and I enter that on my sheet, and the instruction has nothing to say about me, because I did not come here to be right. I came here to be able to write *examined* on eleven.”

He turned round the room.

“Ning Yan,” he said. “Do you consent.”

## The Hour

She had the answer ready before he got to the end of it and she had had it ready since the first of those three sentences, and it was the first time in six days that she had wanted to say something.

“No,” said Ning Yan.

“No,” said the inspector, “thank you. Noted, at the eighth hour. Your bunk is not opened.”

He wrote it. That was all. He wrote two lines on his sheet with a small neat hand and did not look at her while he did it and did not look at her afterwards, and something in her chest came down about a quarter of an inch.

That was the whole of the rule.

Four months she had carried a document in a coat lining. Seven days she had carried a plate against her ribs. Six weeks she had carried four county originals folded into the back of a stolen laundry ledger, and a fair hand in Lowwater had read all four of them in one afternoon and asked which county wrote each one, and the answer to all of that had been four lines at the bottom of a sheet belonging to three men who had never once asked her a question.

She did not say thank you, because she was not thanking him.

Then Cao Lei stood up off his bunk.

## What the Two of Them Did

He got up off the edge of it in one movement, which for a man who had been on a furnace floor eleven years and had got up off a bunk at the second hour every day of his life meant nothing, and about half the room turned round.

The inspector’s brush had stopped. He was looking at the board at the foot of the stair, where it hung every working day of that winter under one nail, and she watched him decide not to look at it for another second.

Xu An stood up after him, on the other side of the room, so that they were not together.

“Say it out loud,” said Cao Lei.

“Say what out loud.”

“Your shift.” The shift leader said it. He had come back up three steps and he was looking at the two of them. “The ninth hour to the second night. Hers. It goes to you because you signed it.”

“It went to us on the eleventh day,” said Cao Lei. “It is on the board and the board is signed and the clerk up the mountain has the hours and the floor has the marks and the number will be the same in three places. She has not worked the ninth hour since the eleventh day past and neither of us has worked the second hour since the fourteenth.”

“It was signed by a member of the crew,” said the inspector, from the doorway, with his brush up. “Which member of the crew.”

“Two,” said Xu An. “There is no regulation about how many. There is a regulation about who.”

“You have signed a shift for a maintaining member.”

“No,” said Cao Lei. “We have signed a shift for a crew. There is one crew on that board and she is on it and we are on it, and the board does not have a place to put her title on because the board is a board and it has names on it. You may read it.”

He did read it. He read it standing in the middle of the room with eleven men behind him and it took him about eleven seconds, and she watched his eyes go down the week and come back up, and she watched him find the shape of two weeks that did not balance against anything.

“The hours are entered,” he said.

“The hours are entered.”

“By a member of the crew,” said Xu An, “which is what a man who signs a shift is.”

The inspector wrote something else. Then he put the brush down and looked at the two of them properly for the first time.

“This is not for the inventory,” he said.

“No,” said Cao Lei. “It is for a roster. A roster is a thing that goes where it goes. Take it or leave it. The shelf is in your anteroom or the board is on that wall and you can copy it and leave it, and either way we will be on the floor at the ninth hour tonight.”

“I am not here to take a crew roster.”

“Then you have wasted the walk,” said Xu An, pleasantly.

## The Hour She Bought

That was the moment she understood that she had been given something, and that it had a price on it that had not been on it in that room an hour ago.

A signed shift is a legal fact. She had known that for a week, because a man with a jar of oil had stood up out of a stool in a room with a stove in it and told her, and she had repeated it back at him before she let him do it.

The two men had stood up in a room with two inspectors in it and said the whole of it aloud to eleven witnesses, which is four times what she had asked of them, and for a reason she had not given them and never would have: that a thing said once in a room is in the chests of everybody in it, and a thing said in a room with an officer of the maintaining members in it is a thing that cannot be got back.

She had told them to say *asleep*. They had said the whole truth.

“You both,” she said. “Thank you. Do not do that again for me.”

“Was that not the idea.”

“It was not the idea,” said Ning Yan. “You gave them a thing to write down. There is no version of this morning where they walk out of here without a line in it, and now they have one, and now there is a piece of paper in a building with no roof that says two men of the Qinghe furnace crew stood up and took a maintaining member’s hours off her.”

“We know that.”

“I know that you know that. I am telling you what you have done so that in February, when a foreman is short, you will both know that this was the price of it and not a kindness.”

She had said thank you to a clerk two nights ago and he had said it back as though it cost something. She said it now to two men and watched it cost them both.

## The Hour of the Hall

She went down at about the eighth hour of the morning with the crew behind her and the frost going out of her hair, and she got the schedule certified.

It took eleven minutes and cost her nothing, and it was the best eleven minutes of her winter.

The clerk at the west end of the Hall had the furnace schedule on a board behind him, because the Hall keeps the week's roster of labour the way a county keeps its returns. It wanted her name on the schedule sheet as a member of the crew, and it wanted the hours on it, and the hours on it were the hours the floor had entered at the close of the shift before.

She sat in a chair in a building with no roof at the ninth hour of the morning while a man with ink to the elbow compared two books and said *yes* four times.

“Eleven to the seventeenth, the ninth hour to the second night, twenty-nine shifts,” he said, “and your hours are four and one-half and the rest is the two men who sign for you.”

“That is what the schedule says.”

“That is what the schedule says. I am certifying what the schedule says and not what the floor says, because that is the entire of what a certification is.” He blotted it. “Attached to the application. The sponsors’ seal is on the other sheet and I am not going to tell you whether a county reads two sheets together or separately, and I have never known and it is not my office to guess.”

She did not file anything.

She had a certificate of a schedule and a sealed application in her coat and she did not take either of them to a desk and she did not put a name in a queue, because the queue was a public thing and there was a man in Lowwater buying the name of a clerk, and because she had exactly one filing and she was going to spend it at a moment of her choosing.

She took the certificate and she went back up the stairs.

## The Fourth Bunk

She came up the four steps of the crew quarters at the tenth hour with frost coming up out of the stairwell behind her, and there were eleven men in the room and two of them in Hall coats, and they had done four bunks.

They were at the fourth from the wall, and there was a clerk on a plank with a lamp, and a young man's kit bag was open on the bed and a piece of roof timber out of it was on the lamp-lit plank, and there were two more pieces of it, and they had come out of that bag or they had not, and there was a clerk writing down which.

“Ning Yan,” said the older inspector.

He had not gone up to her at the foot of the stair. He had gone to the doorway, and he was standing in the doorway, and there were eleven men in the room behind her, and the four bunks he had done were behind him.

“Your consent was asked at the eighth hour and withheld at the eighth hour and I have entered it,” he said, “and I would like to ask you again in front of these eleven people, because the sheet says it is to be asked in front of witnesses and I would rather do it properly than do it quickly.”

The eleven people were in the room.

They had been in the room the whole time.
