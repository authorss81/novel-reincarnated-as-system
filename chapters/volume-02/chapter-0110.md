# The Sealed Slip

It came up in the quarter's crate, folded in three, sealed with a wafer of grey on a thread, and it came to the copying floor instead of to the county office, which Feng noticed and did not comment on.

He put it on the corner of his table with the day's other paper, worked nine lines, and then stopped and came and found her at the intake board.

“You will want to be inside,” he said. “Whatever that is, it is going to want two of us and it is going to want you standing where I can see you, and that is not discourtesy. That is a paper with a seal on it arriving in a tower and not going to the room it was made for.”

She went in. The copying floor smelled of lamp oil and the binding glue and, under it, the wet-paper smell Feng had had all winter, which was his own chest.

He broke the wafer. Three folds, four lines, and a form she had seen a hundred times coming out of the county office on the ordinary stationery, the cheap kind, with the county's own small mark in the corner and nothing at all above it.

*Received at the office of the Lantern Register at Lowwater, in the ordinary course of copying upward, and held:*

*Request, from without the bound, that the term standing in the second position of the status column be struck wherever it is copied within this office and within the county sheet of the applicant's own advancement.*

*Copy the shape of the term as it is set. Do not read it. Strike it. Keep the shape.*

*Entered at the hour.*

Ning Yan stood in the middle of a copying floor and read a thing four hundred years old being asked off the page, and the whole of it came down her at once and none of it was new and all of it was the first time.

It did not name the word. It named the **position** — second position of the status column — which meant the paper could be carried into a room by a man who could not read it, acted on, and gone out again with the term still not in anybody's mouth.

Six hundred years. Eleven entries. A status word on each, in a hand nobody living uses. And a request from outside the bound, on ordinary county stationery with nothing above the county mark, to take one word out of every copy inside.

“Why does it not name it,” said Ning Yan.

“Because it does not come from somebody who wants a word read,” said Yuan. “It comes from somebody who wants a word gone. Those are different clerks.”

“Read me the last line.”

“The office.”

“I cannot read the office,” said Yuan.

She turned round.

“You cannot read it.”

“No,” he said. “I cannot read it. It is the same place as every other one — a name in the position where a name goes, and it is not legible to me, and it has never been legible to me, and I have told you that three times in a year and this is the fourth and I am telling you now in front of a man who is not you, because you will want it in a record and a record is what this is.”

Feng had not looked up. He was looking at the slip flat on the table with both hands flat on either side of it the way a man stands at a table he has no intention of touching.

“Twenty-two years I have sat in this room,” he said, “and that office has never once put a name I could copy on a sheet that came into this tower, and I have never once asked why, because a keeper is paid to copy and not to ask.”

“Today you are going to copy the shape of it,” said Ning Yan.

“Today I am going to copy the shape of it,” said Feng. “And I am going to tell you the thing about it first, because I am an old man and I have been doing this for twenty-two years and I have learned exactly one way of not being the man who regrets it.”

He put his hand flat on the slip, not on the line, well away from the characters.

“I cannot read that word,” he said. “I want to say it plainly and in this order, because I want it written down in my own head in this order and not any other.”

“First, I cannot read it. Second, I am going to copy the shape of it. Third, when they come and ask me whether this tower ever held it, I am going to be able to lay two sheets down and say here is the shape of it and here is where I struck it.”

“I will be telling the truth in both directions at once.”

“And the word.”

“The word I will not have,” said Feng. “I will have the shape of it, which is a thing a man can produce, and not the word, which is a thing a man can be got to say. Those are different properties, and this tower has just been reminded of it by a slip.”

He copied for the better part of an hour.

That was the part she could not watch and did not try to.

She sat on a stool by the window with her hands in her lap and looked at the shapes coming onto the fair sheet one at a time, hook and curve and the brown at the edge of a stroke that is six hundred years old.

She learned that a man can faithfully reproduce the whole architecture of a thing he has never once been able to read, and that this is not a contradiction but a job, and that the tower had been doing the same job for twenty-two years with announcements she had never asked about.

Then he struck it. On the county hand-copies walled behind the fifth item — all four, one after another, a line drawn clean through with a dry brush and no smudge — and on his own working sheet, and on the shelf copy of the county sheet of her advancement that the tower kept because towers keep things.

The word stayed on her own county sheet in the county office on the square, because the county keeps its own, and it was going there next, and she knew that while the brush was still moving.

She did not ask him to read it. She did not ask him whether he recognised the hand, and she sat on a stool by a window for an hour and did not ask him a single question, because the one question worth asking in that room would have been an order to a man who had just done her a thing no one was ever going to thank him for, and she was not going to spend it.

The thing to notice was not the hand. The thing to notice was that the hand was ordinary.

A small fair hand, a Lowwater county clerk's, the brush held the way they all hold it here. There was nothing in it that Yuan could read any better than he could read the office name, and Feng had copied it without a flicker and had not stopped and had not gone white.

“Not that one,” said Yuan, quietly. “It is not a hand I know and it is not a hand I half-know. It gives me nothing at all.”

“You cannot read the office.”

“I cannot read the office,” said Yuan. “And I want to say the second thing now, before the door shuts on it and you have to ask me again in a year.”

“The office.”

“I cannot read it. It is the same place as every other one — a name in the position where a name goes, and it is not legible to me, and it has never been legible to me, and I have told you that three times in a year and this is the fourth.”

“I am telling you now in front of a man who is not you, because you will want it in a record, and a record is what this is.”

“And the other thing.”

“The other thing is the eleven,” said Yuan. “I do not know what became of them. I have never known. This request does not tell me whether they died or were filed or were bought or went out of the bound on their own legs.”

“It is an instruction and not an answer.”

“It is the first thing anybody outside this bound has ever put in writing about them,” said Yuan, “and it is an instruction and not an answer, and I will not have you remember it as anything else.”

Feng sealed his sheet and put it at the bottom of the crate with the fair hand on it, and did not enter it upward, because he entered nothing upward now that was not going up anyway, and the column in his unindexed rank stayed at five where it has stayed since the market day.

Two heads held the word. Her own, and the one that had never recognised it and had said so the first time and had said so again this hour in front of a witness who could not hear him.

Three, if a man counts the paper, and the paper was being struck now, one sheet after another, on a fair copying floor in a town four miles from a county office, by a keeper of twenty-two years who had gone at it without being thanked and would not be thanked and had not asked.
