# The Quiet Lane

The two men were in the lane by the tower on the fourth morning and they were still there on the ninth, and in between they bought nothing at all.

That was the whole of it. She had braced for the good coat and the tube and the young one with the file, and what she got was two men on a wall with their hands on their knees, in daylight, saying nothing to anybody, in a lane a carrier crossed four times an hour.

She watched them for a full day from the intake table with the shutter up.

They sat. A man came past with a hand barrow of paper for the crate and one of them got up out of the way of it and said good morning and sat down again in the same place on the same stone.

They ate out of a cloth at the midday hour. At the ninth hour, when the county hour was, they went and stood at the corner where the lane comes out and watched the queue come out of the office. At the tenth hour they came back and sat down again.

They did not follow her. She went out at the tenth hour and came in at the eleventh and went out again at the second and neither of them turned a head.

The lane runs one way down to the square and one way up past the water butt. She counted forty-one people through it in a day, and every one of them had a reason, and not one of the reasons was a woman.

“You have been looking at them since the eighth hour,” said Yuan.

“I have.”

“They are not hiding it. That is worth something, and it is worth less than it looks like.”

“How much less.”

“They are not hiding it because a thing paid for by the day does not need to be hidden to be paid for.” He said it without weight. “For three months a man buying proof walks into rooms and has to be polite about it and cannot ask twice in the same lane.”

“Now he has a man in a lane. He can sit in the same place for a week and cost the same. The buyer is not in Lowwater and does not want to be seen in Lowwater and is not in a hurry, and a man on a wall costs a man on a wall and no more.”

“He watched Du Hong die for it.”

“He watched Du Hong die for it and he did not lose the claim,” said Yuan. “That is what I am telling you, and I am telling it plain. The proof failed on the body. The body is in the ground on the far side of town. The claim does not care.”

He waited a beat, the way he waits when he has said a thing and cannot add anything to it.

“The claim is eight hundred jin against the Chosen One and it has three proofs unbought and it is standing and it does not decay and it does not need the cheap door again, because the cheap door was one of nine and there are eight left and every one of them is further away and dearer.”

She stood at the shutter with her hand on the frame and did the sum, because she works figures the way other people breathe and she could not help it.

Eight hundred jin was a furnace crew's year to the last ten hours. It was priced at that because somebody inside the bound could pay it. And the man paying it was sitting on a wall in the sun in a town where a hundred and forty people carried things for him and not one of them was in danger of anything at all.

“He has stopped buying because there is nothing left worth his money,” said Ning Yan. “And he has put two men on a wall so that he does not have to come himself and see whether anything has changed.”

“Yes.”

“And what changes it.”

“That you stand still long enough to be cheap. Or that you are wanted in a room by somebody with paper, and go, and the room is a place, and a place is a body and a name at the end of it.”

He stopped. He did not add anything, because he had said it and could not make it better by saying it again.

“Every arrangement you have made since the ninth day has been two people and a county. That has been eleven weeks and it has been excellent. There is no cheap door left in it and he knows it and the young one with the file told him on the road.”

“Then he waits.”

“Then he waits,” said Yuan. “He is good at it. Nobody inside this bound has ever been better at it than a man who owns a counting room and holds paper on three of nine households and does not need the money back by the end of the season.”

## The Walk

She went down at the tenth hour and walked out of the lane in daylight, in front of them, at a walk.

Past the square. Past the three desks with their boards in. Turned at the well and came back the same way. Stood at the trough for the length of a count. Came up the lane.

They did not follow her and they did not write and one of them nodded as she passed, in the way of a man acknowledging a person who is going about her business on a street in daylight.

She stood at the trough with that.

She had spent four months being hunted, and the hunting had a shape, and she had built her whole winter around the shape of it: the lane, the hours, the load, the nine men, the man on a wall, the Watch at the bridge, the captain's board with nothing on it but her name. Every one of those had a place for her to stand and every one of them had cost her something, and she had paid all of them in full.

And the wall had two men on it and was costing her nothing at all.

There was no place on that wall for her to stand. Nothing was shut behind her. Nobody was coming through a door in the night, and there was nothing on the other side of it but a man in a counting room four hundred miles away who had lost a cheap door and had decided that the most economical thing left in the world was to sit on a wall and wait for a woman to get tired.

She counted the days she had been able to be tired in the four months since the claim was entered, and she could not find one where somebody had come through a door, and she had been tired every one of them.

## The Test

She did a stupid thing on the eighth day and it was the only stupid thing she did all spring, and she did it because a woman who has spent four months working out what men are will eventually go and put a hand on the thing itself to see whether it moves.

She went down at the ninth hour with four coins in her hand and stopped at the wall and held them out to the nearer of the two.

She did not offer them in a voice. She put her hand out at waist height, the way you put a hand out to a dog, and stood there and waited.

The man looked at the coins and then at her hand and then at her face, and he said, “No, ma'am,” and he said it kindly, and then he said, “You would not want me to take that.”

“What would happen if I did.”

“I would have to write it up,” he said. “And then the answer would be no, and it would be no from a woman who tried, and it would be a thing in a room I have never seen.” He nodded at his partner. “We are not to take anything off anybody in this lane. We are to be here.”

“Who is we.”

“The gentleman,” said the man, “and you already know that, or you would not be standing here with money.”

She put the coins back in her hand and went up the lane, and the whole of the four hundred miles of the transaction had taken about ninety seconds, and both of them had been entirely correct, and neither of them had moved.

It is not often that a woman gets to put her hand out and find out exactly what a thing is made of. It was made of an instruction, and the instruction was in four counties, and the two men on the wall were the visible end of a piece of paper that had been in a layer since the autumn.

That is what an unanswered claim is. Not a chase. A standing instruction with a body attached to it when somebody decides the body is cheap enough.

She wrote it in the docket in the lining, in the wash code, in one line, because there was one fact in it and the fact was not new.

*Looked. Two men. Wall. Buying nothing. Four days. Unhurried.*

Then she stopped at the bottom of the stair and looked back up the lane, at the shutter of the copying floor with the two clerks in it and the lamp going in the middle of the day because the old man upstairs did what the old man upstairs did.

Below her the intake table stood with a form on it and a crate under it going up at the quarter, and eleven weeks of that was the whole of what she had, and none of it was a door.
