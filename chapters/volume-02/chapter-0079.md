# Work Through Fever

The fever ran fourteen days and she worked twelve of them.

She knows this because the tithe book knows it, in her own slanting hand, four and a half hours at a time. The clerk entered every block slow, checking each against the floor board, and read her week back without comment. The book does not weight for burning. A household with fever in it is the same line as a household without, because the day the book weights by what a person can do, the book is a different book.

The first three days she held the channel and lost the fourth hour to the bench. The next four she worked the plates from the bench, hook under and lift and carry, stack and return, sitting down between loads with her head against the wall. The last five she stood the lip again, breathing two counts where four had been, holding the wall straight by the crack while the leak ran cold at the rim and the stripe burned through her coat.

“You breathe short,” said Yuan. “You sit now and you breathe.”

“I breathe sitting,” said Ning Yan. “I breathe working. The hours do not breathe for me.”

“You burn still,” said Yuan. “I see it and I say it plain.”

“I burn,” said Ning Yan. “I sign. That is the whole of it.”

The quarters learned her fever the way quarters learn everything, by smell and sound. Nine men gave her the corner bunk's blanket without a word and took it back in the morning without being asked.

The shift leader stopped offering the bench as pity and started ordering it as tactics, which she obeyed because tactics can be argued with on the floor and pity cannot. The foreman signed six on the board every morning with his name on the line and posted six in the hole, and the timber held sound.

On the fifth day the willow tea stopped coming up from the wash house because the girl had run out of willow and would not ask for more. Ning Yan went down in the evening with her own packet from the tower loft and set it on the stool without a word.

“You cough worse,” said the girl.

“I cough and I work,” said Ning Yan. “You copy slates and you knit. Which of us disobeys the healer.”

“Both,” said the girl. “Sit before you fall. That stool holds.”

Ning Yan sat. The steam got into her chest and sat there, and she breathed short till it settled.

“He wrote it down,” said the girl. “The buyer. Your number. The whole wash house heard the layer speak. Two counties said the words wrong by nightfall and the tower copy has them right.”

“I know,” said Ning Yan. “A number to buy against. That is what a stage is in a market.”

“Are you afraid.”

“I am fevered and short of breath and seven hundred hours from clearance,” said Ning Yan. “Fear takes hours and I spend mine working.”

The girl nodded as if that were an answer, which it was not, and went back to her slate. Ning Yan climbed to the floor for the night block and signed again, and the clerk entered it with the lamp low.

## The Short Breath

By the second week the fever thinned and the breath did not lift.

She learned the shape of it the way she had learned the eleven-minute window, by counting. Two counts in and the chest pulled short, as if the air stopped a finger short of where it used to go. Stairs cost one stop. The channel cost holding the rail. Talking cost choosing words. She chose fewer words and held the rail and climbed the stairs once instead of twice, and the work went on.

Shen Xiu's book moved while she burned. The household passed three hundred and fifty worked, then three hundred and eighty. Her own line reached forty-five hours, continuous from the day the book opened, every block signed, no gap the county could point to. The refused slip sat under the cover with three seals showing, and the clerk's eye went to it once a week and away.

“You pay it daily,” said Yuan. “You hold the wall and you pay it.”

“I pay it,” said Ning Yan. “I do not weigh it. Weighing takes breath.”

On the twelfth day she stayed abed till midday on the shift leader's order, which was the first order she had obeyed all winter without arguing, because arguing takes breath too. She lay in the second bunk with nine men working below and the tablet against her skin, and listened to the furnace breathe through the floor.

“You let it go,” she said in the empty room. “You decide it and you do it. I live inside it.”

“I decide it,” said Yuan. “I live inside it with you. I do not leave it.”

“I do not thank you,” said Ning Yan. “I do not stop you. That stands.”

“It stands,” said Yuan. “I keep it as you keep hours.”

She slept till the fifth hour and went down for the evening block. The wall stood straight. The clerk entered four and a half hours against the forty-ninth day, and read the household at three hundred and fifty-five worked, five hundred and eighty-five standing.

Three hundred and fifty-five. Five hundred and eighty-five. Forty-five in her own hand.

The clerk blotted the entry and kept his hand on the book a moment longer than procedure needs. He had written her line eleven times now, always slow, always checked against the floor board. The refused slip sat under the cover with three seals showing, and his eye went to it the way it does once a week.

“You burn less,” he said.

“I burn less and breathe short,” said Ning Yan. “The book wants hours. I bring them.”

He entered the hour and turned the page back to show her the running line, the way he shows no one else, because a hand that signs left-handed in fever earns one plain sight of the account. Nine hours from the old page, thirty-six on the new, every block signed, no gap. The household around it moved with eleven hands and a short crew, thirty-odd in the week, and hers inside it like a bone inside a hand.

“It balances,” he said. “Slow balances. Fast does not.”

“Then slow,” said Ning Yan. “I want it right in spring.”

She walked back past the wash house and saw the girl asleep with the lath splint straight and the slates stacked. The fever had left her hollow and light, the way burn leaves iron, and the breath stayed short the way the clerk had said nothing about and Yuan had said plain.

Two weeks of fever, nearly done. A month of short breath, just started, never fully leaving. A stage the whole bound could recite, bought and paid for in her own body.

“You work it ill,” said Yuan. “You hold the wall and you cough blood into your sleeve.”

“I cough iron,” said Ning Yan. “I hold the wall. The floor needs hands and I am a hand.”

“You are more,” said Yuan. “You are tired and you are more. I see both.”

“I am tired,” said Ning Yan. “I sign. That is the more.”

She climbed the stair and lay down with the arithmetic for company. It moved because she moved it. Nothing else moved it. That was enough to sleep on.
