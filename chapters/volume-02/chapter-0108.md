# Stone and Paper

Two hundred and forty hands went through the Lantern Register tower in a day, and Ning Yan stood at the table on the sixth morning and counted every one of them, and knew the number of places a stranger could stand.

The intake table was a plank on two trestles at the tower door with a board on it and a brush on a string and a crate under it. Loads came in off the lane — stone, oil, paper, water, lamp oil, seal-wax, bound copies for the crate — and each one was written in five columns.

What, how much, who carried, from what hour, and a sixth column with the hour of the ninth-line, which is where she had put herself eight weeks ago on the word of a man with a fair hand.

“Write the sixth column,” said Feng. “It is the same column as the fifth for most of the day.”

“For most of the day,” said Ning Yan.

“For most of the day,” said Feng, and went up, coughing.

The sixth column was why a man in a good coat had stopped buying pages and started buying hours. The fifth column was a time. The sixth was a body at a time, and a body that stood in the same column forty times a day at the ninth hour was one of forty and not a description.

## A Day at the Plank

The day is nine hours and it has a shape, and she learned the shape in a day and a half.

There is a run before the ninth hour, when the tower takes what the carriers bring down the lane in the night, and it is heavy and it is all in the fifth column. Then the ninth hour, which is the sixth column, and which is the only hour in the day anybody comes to the table for. Then the middle, which is paper and oil and nothing a person stands in the lane for. Then the run after the eleventh, which is the crate and the stair.

Between the ninth hour and the eleventh she wrote the same kind of line forty times, and the brush got faster, and then got slower again, because a hand gets slower when it stops being surprised by itself.

The clerk's apprentice was fifteen, and his name was Pei-something, and he carried the crate up. On the second afternoon he stood at her left shoulder for eleven minutes and watched her write and said nothing at all, and at the twelfth minute she felt him looking at the shape of the sixth column instead of the fifth.

“Do not,” said Ning Yan.

“I was not going to,” said the apprentice. “I only wanted to see how you do the ninth line so it does not go crooked. Mine goes crooked.”

She showed him. It is a small thing to be taught by a woman with one arm, and he did it right twice out of three, and at the end of the day he said, “Feng says you are not to be thanked.”

“Feng says a great many things.”

“Feng says you are not to be thanked about anything,” said the apprentice, “and that I am to tell you so that you do not do it.”

She wrote for three days and learned what a clerk's page looks like from inside, which is that it looks like nothing.

Five columns and a brush and a man who is careful. The whole of what is in it is the truth and none of it is information. A thing can be entirely truthful and be worth a great deal to the man who buys it, and there is no line on any form in this town that says which.

On the second afternoon a carrier brought up bound copies in oilcloth for the crate and her hands did something they had done four hundred times at the furnace, which was to go down the outside edge and read the page before she read the middle of it, because the outside edge of a book tells you what is inside it.

Six counties' fair hand on the bound edges. The tower's own in four. One bundle in a hand she had not seen in the book before — small, hooked at the ends, brown at the edge of the strokes.

She put her hand flat on the bundle and did not open it and did not go down the middle, because a page is not a page. It is what an office decided to keep and what they will hand a man with a tube, and she had learned to read an edge in this town the way a woman reads a roofline for weather.

A hand like that on a bundle in a crate going up a stair meant something had happened somewhere and had been copied up by somebody in a room she had never been inside, and it did not mean that something had happened to her.

The crate went up at the quarter. She put her hand flat on it, briefly, the way a woman puts a hand flat on a horse she is going to leave standing, and the two clerks took it up and did not see her do it because they were looking at a sheet.

“You saw it,” said Yuan.

“I saw an edge,” said Ning Yan. “That is not the same as seeing a thing, and you know it better than I do, because you are the thing that gets read.”

## The Good Coat at the Plank

The good coat came down the lane on the third day at the tenth hour and stopped at the corner of the table and stood there, and she went on writing, because going on writing is the only thing a clerk can do with a man who has come to look at a board.

He read the whole of it. She watched him read it, and it took longer than it should have, because a man who buys boards reads them for the shape and not for the entries. Then he looked at the brush on its string, and then at her hand, and then at the sixth column.

“The fifth,” he said.

“No,” said Ning Yan. “The sixth.”

“The sixth,” said he, and put his hand in his coat and did not finish what he had put it in there for, and went back up the lane to the square.

The fifth column is a time. Everybody in Lowwater carries at the fifth hour and the fifth hour is not a description. The sixth is a body at a time, and he had come down a lane to find out which of the two a slow woman had been writing in for two months, and she had told him, in two words, standing up, in front of a fifteen-year-old with a crate on his shoulder.

He had come down a lane for a body. A lane does not have one in it. It has a column, and the column has fifty-one names in it, and every one of them belongs to somebody who is paid by the hour.

She wrote it in the docket in the lining, the way she wrote everything.

*Looked. Board. Asked fifth. Sixth. Hand went in coat. Left.*

## The Two Offices

At the county office on the fourteenth day she stood in the queue at the ninth hour with the month of it behind her and did the sum out of a clerk's habit, which is that a clerk wants the figures before he wants the signature.

The county wanted days she had stood in a room.

Sixteen county hours in a month, every one attended, her own hand on every sheet, the stamp on every one of them, and a gap in the last month where a man had stood in an ante-room with a dead man's surety-line crossing his hand and a body in a ditch at the foot of a tower four miles down the road.

The schedule wanted loads. She was at the third hundred and eleven hours of the season's labour, and the season did not close until the last week of the thaw, and the county wanted the schedule certified at the Hall's own clerk. Not by her. Not by Feng. Not by any man in a tower.

Two offices. Two rooms four days apart. One wanted her in it and the other wanted a number out of it, and the number was the only thing either of them had ever been satisfied with.

“That is a schedule,” said Yuan. “That is the schedule of your labour, and it is going to be attached to a filing, and it is being asked for because somebody in a room has understood what it does.”

“Who.”

“I do not know who.” He said it plainly. “I read what is inside the bound near you and I cannot read a room on a square and I have never once been able to read a form. I am not going to stand here and tell you I know whose hand it is when I do not.”

She looked at the two desks through the open shutter.

The first had the advancement copies on it and a young clerk with a brush to the wrist. The second had carriers' papers on it and a coin on the wood twice a day. The third had the household lines on it and the counting lane behind it, and there was nobody at that one this morning and had not been for a week, because a man does not sit at a desk he has finished buying.

“The schedule has to be certified at the Hall's clerk,” said Ning Yan. “At Qinghe. Four days up and four days down on market days, and the road is open to carts before the ninth hour and shut by the eleventh.”

“And you go up.”

“I go up.”

She had a tower and a square and a crate, and about eleven weeks of a man in her head, and a document on the square that wanted a number out of the mountain and not her.

She was going to go and get it, and there was no way on that road to do it that did not take her through a barrier where a captain who owed her nothing had written her name on a board in his own hand.
