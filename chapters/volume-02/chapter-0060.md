# The Second Bunk

The ash got into everything on the eleventh and last day of it, and by the fourth hour past midday it was in the bedding, in the wash water, in the lye, and in the cough of the whole room, which was a single weave of sound and not eleven separate ones.

They had been out in it since before the light. She had come in out of the yard with the grey going into the creases of her hands and it was the eleventh day and there would not be a twelfth, and there was a kind of animal relief in a thing that has a number attached to it.

Cao Lei had the end of her bunk. She had known that in the autumn and had not had to be told, because he was the one who had come back and put his hand on the frame and said *it is a narrow bunk* and then had gone and done something else so that it would not sit there.

“You have not asked me anything,” she said.

“No.”

“You have wanted to ask me something for two days.”

“Yes,” said Cao Lei.

“Then ask it.”

He looked at the fire, which was a fire in a quarters and not a fire at a furnace, and gave it about four seconds.

“The floor,” he said. “What it was. The four of them on it.”

“That is not a question.”

“It is the question the yard is asking.”

“Then the yard can ask me and get less.”

## What She Gave Them

She had been sitting on the bunk with her left elbow on her knee and the log four inches away under the straw, and she made the decision to do it about as fast as she could make one, which was fast, and badly.

“My arm is dead,” she said. “It comes back about a third of the time and it has come back that much since the fourth day past and it is not going to come back further this winter.”

Nobody said anything, which was the right thing, and one of the older men — Xu An, who was twenty-six and had been on the floor eleven years and had a way of standing with his weight on the back of his heels like a man waiting for a floor to come to him — looked at her right arm and then deliberately at her face.

“It is not sleeping,” said Ning Yan. “It is not weakness. There is nothing on it and it has not fallen off.”

“What happened to it.”

“Five things came out of a sky that building had not seen in three hundred years,” she said, “and one of them was a beam and one of them was the roof and it was not luck. It was the room.”

That was the first time she had said it out loud to anybody who was not on a floor with a witness list. It did not get any smaller.

“The crack,” she said. “Here.” She put her left hand flat against her own breastbone. “It opened through and it has closed in the shape it opened, and it opens and shuts, and that is the whole of what a Condensing one is now. It is a door that does not shut properly. I hold heat through it and I hold qi through it and it leaks at the rim, and it does not get better, and nothing in this sect or any other will make it better.”

“Is that why you cannot lift.”

“I can lift. I can lift with one arm and I am slower than Cao Lei by about half, and by next winter I will be slower than everybody in this room by more than half, and in three years I will be lifting things that a stage four could not lift, with one arm, and I will not be able to carry any of them far.”

The room had gone the way a room goes when nobody wants to be the one who breaks the silence, and she looked round it deliberately, and said the last of it.

“And there is eight hundred jin on my name,” said Ning Yan. “Stand in the layer. It goes to whoever brings a body and a place and a name. Not withdrawn by anything I do. Nobody has come for it yet and when one comes he will not knock.”

Eleven men and a fire.

“You have been sitting in this room for six days with a price on you,” said Xu An, “and you did not tell us.”

“No.”

“Why not.”

“Because you would have looked at me differently,” said Ning Yan, “and I do not have four months of looking at me differently in me, and I am trying to get through them.”

Somebody made a sound. It was not a laugh exactly. It was the sound eleven furnace-men make at the end of a sentence they do not have a word for.

Cao Lei got up, went to his bunk, came back with a jar of oil about the size of two fists and put it on the frame of hers without saying anything at all.

It was the same jar, from the same source, from the same boy with a lamp that ran dry in the autumn of her first month. He had kept it. In a crew's bunk. For four months.

“All right,” said Ning Yan, and picked it up, and did not say thank you, because they were all watching to see whether she would.

## What She Kept

She did not tell them about the plate.

That was a decision and she made it in front of eleven people in about a second, and the thing she thought while she made it was: I have a plate against my chest that I lied about in a room with no roof, and I have four county originals in the lining of this coat, and the wording of that oath, and if I tell this room then I have told a room of eleven in a building with a stove in it.

Not because they would sell her. Because eleven men is not a lock. A thing said in a room of eleven is in eleven chests by morning and there is no procedure for getting it back out of any of them.

So she kept the plate and she kept the log and she kept the county originals, and she let them have the arm and the door in her chest and the eight hundred jin, and it was the first time in four months she had told a set of people a true thing and kept a false one in the same breath.

She noticed herself doing it.

“There is more,” said Xu An.

“There is always more.”

“Will you tell us when you can.”

“No,” said Ning Yan. “Not that. I will tell you when I can and that is a real promise and it is about the middle of the things.”

Xu An nodded at the fire, and did not press it, and she understood that this room was going to be a problem for her for the rest of the winter in a way she had not budgeted for.

## The Roster

It was Cao Lei who found the actual shape of it, about an hour later, when the light had gone out of the door and the ash had stopped — all at once, without any decision, the way it does, and she had been waiting four months for a thing to stop and had not expected it to be that.

“The roster,” he said. “For the shifts. It is on the board at the foot of the stair and the shift leader writes it up on the second evening and it goes to Shen Xiu’s clerk on the third.”

“I know what the roster is.”

“You know it is a board,” said Cao Lei. “You are a maintaining member of this sect and you have been in the Hall for six days and you are going to be there more and not less, because that is where the paper is. And you cannot be in two places.”

“I can be on the floor. I have held a night shift one-handed since the seventh day past.”

“You held one night shift,” said Xu An, “on a furnace that had four men spare and a drill-master who kept telling you to go and sit down. In three weeks you will be at the Hall four days out of seven and the floor will have three instead of six and somebody short will be stood on the settling work to make the count.”

“Who says the count has to be made.”

“The foreman says the count has to be made.”

“Then the foreman can be wrong.”

“The foreman is right about the count and he is wrong about you,” said Xu An, “and he will not be wrong about it out loud, and in February he will be short and he will take it out of the first two men he can find, and neither of us is going to be the first two men he finds.”

She sat on the bunk and did the arithmetic, and the arithmetic took about four seconds, and there was no good answer in it.

Then Cao Lei said the thing.

“The roster takes a signature,” he said. “Not a name. A signature. Any man of the crew can sign for a shift, and when a shift is signed it is a legal fact, and what the floor did at the hour is not checked against a body. It is checked against a mark.”

“Which is how it works.”

“Which is how it works,” he said. “I have known that since my first winter. You have not asked me what I have known since my first winter.”

“No,” said Ning Yan. “I have not.”

He put his thumb on the board of his own tally, the one he kept on the frame of his bunk with the notches he had cut for every shift he had ever stood, and he did not look at her while he said it.

“Me and him. Every shift you are at the Hall. We sign it and we do the shift and the board says what the board says.”

## The Part She Took

She could have said no.

That is the thing that has to be in this and she did not soften it later and she did not soften it now: they offered, and she was a maintaining member of Qinghe Sect with an iron token in her coat and the standing she had bought four days ago at the second hour and thirty-one minutes, and the rules of this sect were extremely clear about who signed for what, and she could have said no and gone and been correct.

She thought about the four rooms she had refused. She thought about the relief of the second bunk and how it had felt like a hand on her, and how that was the worst thing she had felt in six days.

She thought about how a document is a document, and about how much of her life had been spent in rooms with paper in them, and how nobody in this room could read her hand.

“All right,” she said.

Cao Lei did not move.

“I am saying yes. And I want you both to hear the whole of it, now, in this room, so that neither of you can say later that you did not know it.”

They waited.

“A signed shift is a legal fact,” said Ning Yan, “and a legal fact is a piece of paper, and a piece of paper is the only thing in nine counties that has ever moved me. I know what a roster is. I have been on the wrong end of one since I was eleven years old and I have watched it take my father off the floor and my brother out of a yard and my mother into a bed in a cold house. That is what those are.”

The fire went over.

“And I am going to let you sign for me, because I have four months of tithe hours to work and there is no way to work them if I am on a floor that has three men instead of six. And I am telling you what a roster is now, tonight, before it is on a board, so that if it ever goes wrong you both know that I told you, and so that I cannot tell myself afterward that I did not.”

Nobody in that room said anything at all for about ten seconds, and Xu An's face did something at the end of it, and she turned away from it because she had enough of it.

“I know what a roster is,” he said, “and you have told us, and we will do it.”

He put out his hand, flat, the way you put a hand down on a board.

She did not take it. She put her left hand on the frame of the bunk and left it there, and looked at the two of them instead, which was as close as she was going to get in a room with a stove in it.

“When the shift leader asks who is on the furnace at the second hour,” she said, “you answer for each other, and you do not look at me, and if anybody asks where Ning Yan is you say at the Hall, because that is where she will be.”

“Asleep,” said Cao Lei. “You are going to be asleep half the time in the winter, you are a fool.”

“Asleep,” said Ning Yan, “is the answer you give, because I will be, and because you are both going to be exhausted, and I am not going to have either of you caught out on a board because one of us was too tired to lie properly.”

They laughed, and it was the first sound in that room in six days that had not been a cough or a fire, and she sat on a narrow bunk in a crew's quarters with an old straw mattress and a jar of oil on the frame beside her and a dead arm inside her coat and understood, about nine seconds later than she would have liked, that she was being kind to herself and calling it strategy.

They were being kind to her.

That was the trouble. They were being kind to her, and she had taken it, and it was warm, and it did not cost anything at all until it did.

“It is done, then,” said Xu An, and got up, and the room went back to the ordinary noise of eleven men at the end of a short day.

Outside, the sky over the mountain was a hard clean black with stars in it for the first time in eleven days. Somewhere down the hill a lamp-mender was already reading four different sentences about her into a market square, and the thing she had bought in that room in the morning was going to cost two men their lives inside a month.

She did not know that yet.

She lay in the second bunk with her coat under her and the log in the lining and the plate against her chest and let a warmth she did not trust come all the way in.

## The Folded Sheet

She was not asleep at the second night hour, and she went up.

The Hall has no roof and has not had one since the fourth day past, and at night there were stars over the floor and the braziers at the north end and the long smell of cold stone that would be in that building until spring.

Nobody was on the floor. There was one clerk at the table at the west end with a lamp and a cough of his own, and he did not look up, because he had been the clerk at that table for eleven days and looking up was the thing he had decided not to do.

There was a sheet folded in three on the table in front of the chair she had used every day for a week, and on the outside of the fold, in a hand that was not hers and not the clerk's and not the hand on the fourth day past either, were three characters.

*Nin Yan.*

She stood there and looked at it for a long time, because a clerk who cannot read her hand and writes her name from something else has to have gone and found out what to write it as.

Nobody in nine counties could write her name correctly. The lamp-menders had made four sentences about her and every one of them had got *servant-born* wrong, and the copyist at the Bureau had asked her on the sixth asking whether the third word was *furnace* or *funeral*.

Three characters, in the wrong order, in a clerk's careful hand, on a folded sheet waiting for her in the dark at the second night hour.

Her name, written down by an office.
