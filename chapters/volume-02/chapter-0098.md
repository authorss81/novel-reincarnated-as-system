# The Cheap Door

The pending filing held the way a wall holds, by being stood on every day.

Ning Yan attended the stamp day with Du Hong at the tenth hour, entered and in daylight, with the queue running out the door into market noise. Sun He sat at the far table with the county book open and the stamp at his hand. When her name came she stood before him with the filing copy, stages recorded, hours continuous, defect in her own hand. Du Hong gave his name aloud for the attendance line.

The registrar read the void-and-transfer terms the way clerks read weather. Du Hong said he knew. The brush moved. The mark went down.

She had begun to know the room the way a body knows a floor. Where the boards gave. Which queue ran out of the door when the weather turned. Which clerk would look up if a man paid and which clerk would look up if a man only looked. She had attended nine times and she could have got through the whole of it with her eyes shut, which frightened her more than the claim did, because a room you can do with your eyes shut is a room you are not watching.

They walked out into the square with the paper excellent and the claim standing over it, side by side, one refusing the other and both hers. The man in the good coat paid at the second desk. The younger man watched from the lane mouth. The counting room stacked paper by household behind both.

The queue at the third desk had a woman in it she had seen once before, a woman in a good coat of a plainer cut, who was going through the household book out loud with a clerk while a man in a good coat stood at her elbow and paid. The woman read a household, and the clerk wrote it, and when the woman stopped the clerk stopped, and when the woman started again he started again in a different part of the book.

Ning Yan watched for the length of four households.

It was not new. It was the whole of what the counting room was for, and she had known it since the autumn, and she had never seen it run at speed with a woman inside it before. The woman walked out with nothing in her hands, which was what you walk out with when the clerk has written what you asked him to write, and she did not look back at the desk.

Nobody had put a hand on her. That was the part that took Ning Yan a moment. Four households read, a page filled, a woman walking out into market noise, and no hand anywhere on her arm.

She stood there and worked out, standing in the sun in front of the third desk with her filing copy under her coat, that she had been afraid of the wrong thing for four months. She had been afraid of a body taken off a lane. What the bound actually did, at least this year, in this room, was put a price on a household and let the household find out later, and a body was the last thing in the process and the easiest one to refuse.

“You stand it,” said Yuan. “You bear the reading and you walk still.”

“I stand it,” said Ning Yan. “He stands with me. You watch with us.”

“I watch with you,” said Yuan. “I stay where I can see both ends.”

Du Hong was cheerful under it the way he is cheerful under everything, badly hidden and entirely steady. He carried tower letters through the lanes in daylight, flour on his sleeves, file on the table, and nodded to the younger man once the way carriers nod at weather.

“He knows my name now,” he said in the loft over soup. “The younger one. He asked a carrier what the tower hand carries in his coat. The carrier told him sheets. He paid for the hour and the lane.”

“Walk with the water cart after dark,” said Ning Yan. “Walk entered. Carry nothing with my hand on it unentered.”

“My coat carries your copies still,” said Du Hong. “Four sheets with no name on them, down the road weeks ago. And the file carries your hand twice. I walk entered and I am still the cheap door. You cannot paper that away.”

She looked at him across the mat. Eighteen, ambitious, generous, bad at lying. His name in a county book for her attendance. Her hand twice in his file. Her copies in his coat down the road. Every paper that made him worth stopping had her hand on it, and he had put himself there on purpose, in daylight, before a queue, so that no man could afterward say he had not been told what it costs.

“I know,” she said. It came out plain, without tally in it. “I let you stand and I carry what you decide. That is all I have.”

“It is much,” said Du Hong. “My name is the whole line of a county book. Everything since autumn was filed under you. This line is mine.”

He put the spoon down and looked at the boards.

“Say the rest of it,” said Ning Yan. “You have been carrying it since the mountain and it comes out in pieces.”

Du Hong was quiet long enough that she thought he would not do it.

“The clerk at the second desk has my hours,” he said. “He has them because you told him to set them beside my name in the autumn, and I said nothing at the time because it was nothing. Eleven weeks of tower letters. Every morning since. A man could buy that page for the price of a good dinner and read off what hour I leave and what hour I come back and whether I am carrying.”

“I know,” said Ning Yan.

“You know and you did not stop it.” He said it without heat, which was worse. “I want you to know that I would have let it happen anyway. I would have stood at that desk and paid a man to write down every hour of my life, because those hours were standing next to yours on the same page, and I was not going to let a clerk in Lowwater be the only one who thought that mattered.”

She did not have an answer to that and she sat with it for as long as it took him to look up.

“You should have asked me first,” she said.

“I know,” said Du Hong. “I am telling you now, in daylight, at the day hour, before I do anything with it. That is the part I can still give you.”

She did not thank him. Thanks between people who know the cost is done by not saying it.

In the evening she drilled the left-hand sequence once in the yard and stopped early to save breath, because breath is hours and hours are the county's. The sequence ran hook, turn, settle — cleaner than winter, hers and unshared. She sat with the lath across her knees till the chest settled, and felt Settlement thin beside her the way a blanket goes thin, still covering and less warm.

She ran it again anyway, and got four minutes further than the first time, and that was all she had for the evening.

Stamp days, tower readings, carrying work. The paper used, not admired. Attendance, visibility, and a name beside hers.

That was what the pending filing cost under pursuit, and it was not a price she could pay smaller. A licence would end it. A refusal had not ended it either, and a person who believed a refusal had settled a thing had not read the schedule.

She climbed once to the loft and lay on the mat with her coat over her. Below, Feng coughed and copied. On the square the desks closed. At the lane mouth the younger man waited till the tower lamp lit, then went toward the counting lane with his report.

“You fear for him,” said Yuan. “You love him plainly and you do not say it.”

“I fear,” said Ning Yan. “I work. You stay with me.”

“I stay,” said Yuan. “I do not leave this and neither do you.”

She lay with the filing copy in her sleeve and the docket in her lining and the word ticking behind her eyes where no paper held it, and a boy's name in a county book below her, read aloud that day and every stamp day, the price of being legal to five while hunted.

The cheap door walked openly in daylight, entered, watched. That was the whole of it, and it was enough for any man buying bodies to mark.
