# Nine Days Unfed

Feng brought it up at the eighth hour and set it on the floor by the mat the way a man sets down a tool he does not expect to be picked up.

Boiled grain, and a piece of salted fish, and the small cabbage he ate himself when there was any, which was twice a winter and neither time good.

“Eat,” said Feng.

“I will eat when I can,” said Ning Yan.

“You can now. That is the whole of what can means.”

She looked at it. She had carried loads down the tower stair that morning with her left hand and set them on the intake table and had felt, somewhere between the fifth step and the ground, a want for it that she had not asked for and could not account for.

She took a mouthful and chewed it and her throat closed round it like a hand.

Feng did not watch her do it. He picked the bowl up again at the fourth minute, when she had got down four mouthfuls and put it down, and he carried it out, and he did not say anything on the way past, and the door of the loft is a curtain, and a curtain is not a door a man shuts with an opinion in it.

He was not being kind. She understood the shape of it, because she had grown up inside it.

A furnace crew does not ask a woman twice about the pot. It puts the pot in front of her and it puts it in front of her again on the next shift, and nobody in the building ever remarks on what is in it, and a woman who is not eating either loses the pot or is worked somewhere she can be watched, and nobody in the building ever remarks on that either.

He was being the building. That was the kindness and it cost him nothing she could see.

## The Third Day

The third day was the worst, and not for the reason she expected. She had expected the strength to go. The strength did not go; the legs carried four hours at the intake table and eight hundred pounds of paper off a cart and up the tower stair, and they would carry them for another month. What went was the willingness to do anything at all that was not on the schedule, and by the third morning there was not a great deal on the schedule that she wanted.

She washed at the trough at the fourth hour and the water was the temperature it had been all winter and she stood in it and found that she could not make herself lift her hands out of it.

That was the thing she had not known about herself. She had known at eleven that she could work through a broken hand and a fever and a funeral, because she had done it. She had not known that the thing that would stop her was not pain and was not weight and was not cold, and was standing in nine inches of water in the fourth hour of the morning with nothing to carry.

The girl of twenty-four came down the lane at the seventh hour with a bowl under a cloth and put it on the trough stone.

“Willow tea,” she said, “and there is grain in it because you are not going to drink willow tea nine days running.”

“I will drink it.”

“You drank it yesterday and you poured half of it into the ditch while I was carrying the fourth bucket and I watched you do it,” said the girl. “I am not going to argue about it. I am going to put it down at this stone at the seventh hour and the eighth hour and the ninth hour for as long as you want it here.”

“And then?”

“And then I am going to stop,” said the girl, “because I am charged to the eighth estate and the eighth estate does not pay me to stand at a trough.”

She went up the lane. The bowl sat on the trough stone for eleven minutes and then the cold got into it and it was the coldest thing in Lowwater, and she put it in the ditch herself at the ninth hour and did not feel anything about that at the time, and thought about it for three days afterward.

## Four, Five, Six

On the fourth day she went back out onto the flats.

She did not decide to. She got up at the fifth hour because the light came through the shutter and lay there in the ordinary way, and she was putting her boots on with her left hand before she had decided anything, and she was on the ash by the seventh hour with a stone in her hand and thirty yards of mud in front of her, and it was the first thing her body had wanted in nine days that was not a bowl.

She carried the stone for three hours. She did not do the form. The sequence that had been built out of the winter in her legs was gone and what was left was four years of a furnace floor that had taught her to move before she thought, and there was nothing in a mud flat for any of that.

At the tenth hour she stopped and stood in the wind and understood that she had not come out here for the cold and that she was not going to be able to say what she had come out here for, and that it was not a thing a woman of seventeen should have to carry on her own on the tenth day after putting a man in the ground.

The fifth and sixth days she carried loads. That was the whole of them and there is nothing to say about the fifth and sixth days.

On the sixth night Feng came up with the bowl again and set it down and stood there while she ate most of it, and he did not say anything about that either, and when she had finished he took the bowl and the cloth and went down the ladder, and she lay on the mat in the dark with her stomach in a knot and her mouth tasting of salt and thought that it had been eleven weeks since anybody had seen her eat.

## The Signing

She went down at the ninth hour and worked. Intake at the table, loads in, loads out, the crate going up the stair at the quarter. Four days of that, and on the fourth day the county hours came round and she stood in the queue at the office on the square with her signing arm in the lining of her coat, because the lining is where a sheet goes that has to be kept clean and flat.

The clerk put the sheet on the table and turned the brush and she took it with her left hand and it came out of her in a slant that no clerk in nine counties has ever seen.

The brush went through the first figure. Her hand was not steady enough to make the two strokes that close a character, and the mark she made for her own name had a hole in the middle of it where the tremor had gone.

“You can have it read back,” said the clerk.

“No,” said Ning Yan. “A signature is a signature and I cannot do it better at the second try.”

He looked at the sheet for a while. He did not say anything about the hole, and he did not ask her to do it again, and he stamped the day with the county mark and turned the book.

That was the whole of what a clerk gave her and it was worth more than the week. In nineteen years at that table a man learns that a signature in a bad hand is still a signature, and that a holder who signs badly and does not ask for a second time is not a holder who doubts, and that this is the only thing in the room which cannot be improved by asking.

## The Ninth Day

“I cannot feed you,” said Yuan.

She was on the tower stair with the filing copy in her sleeve and the light going out of the sky over the roofs.

“No,” said Ning Yan. “You cannot.”

“You know why I say it. You know what I am and what is on me, and I am telling you the shape of it plain, because I have watched you this week, and a thing that cannot lift food should not go unsaid.”

“You cannot lift,” said Ning Yan. “You can stand in a room and read me and warn me and be wrong at me about four counts of breath, and you have never once lifted anything, and I have never once forgotten it and I have never once said it out loud before, and I am saying it now because you have cost me a stage and a stage is worth saying it over.”

The stair was dark above her and dark below and the weight with no hands said nothing at all for a while.

“Say it again,” said Yuan.

“No. Once.”

She got to the loft and lay on the mat with the coat for a blanket and the arm out to the side. The stage sat in her with nothing going out of it anywhere, and she was hungry, and she did not know how long the not-eating had gone on, which is how it had gone on for nine.

On the ninth day she was at the trough at the end of the carrying, and a carrier's woman she had carried water for twice a month put a bowl down on the trough stone without a word and went off with the empty one.

Ning Yan ate half of it standing up and had to stop and take hold of the trough edge with her left hand, and then she carried water for an hour and a half with the half of it in her.

Feng asked her at the second night hour, the way he asked about the arm, without any particular way about it.

“Will you eat tonight.”

“I will eat tonight,” said Ning Yan.

“You will eat tonight,” said Feng, and went down to the floor, and did not say that he would look at it in the morning, and would.

She ate. It was not much and it was not good and she did not stop.

That was what the fourth stage cost her, and it was not in a book anywhere, and she wrote nothing about it, because the fourth stage itself was in no book anywhere either, and for nine days in the spring there was not one clerk in nine counties who could have told anybody what that woman was doing in a tower loft at the eighth hour.
