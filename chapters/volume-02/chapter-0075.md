# The Wash House Splint

The wash house at Qinghe is a low building at the bottom of the quarters stair with a stone floor that never dries and a row of troughs that steam all winter.

Ning Yan went down there on the twenty-sixth day at the seventh hour of the evening because the slate the girl was copying had an error in it that would cost a household four hours if it went up uncorrected. She carried the tithe sheet in her sleeve and a brush in her pocket, and she told herself on the stair that this was business and not visiting, because visiting is a thing you do for comfort and she had no comfort to spend.

The girl of twenty-four sat against the wall with her leg out straight on a stool. The splint was a lath tied with cord above and below the break, packed with cloth where the cord would cut. It was the work of a healer with nothing to work with, and it was holding. A bowl of wash water steamed beside her and a pile of copied slates stood on the floor at her good side.

“You add wrong on the third line,” said Ning Yan from the doorway.

“I add right,” said the girl. “The carrier marked two trips as one. I copied what he marked.”

“Then the carrier is short four hours and the household pays it.” Ning Yan came in and set the sheet on the stool. “Show me.”

The girl turned the slate. Her hands were chapped to the wrist and her face was thin in the way of people who lie down for weeks and do not eat enough to knit. She was twenty-four and she had been on the cold end for three years, and the beam had taken her leg sideways and left her the rest.

They worked the figures together for twenty minutes, Ning Yan reading aloud in the tally voice and the girl correcting in a low one. The error was the carrier's, as the girl had said, and Ning Yan marked it in wash code on the edge of the sheet so the tithe clerk would see it without a household seeing it explained.

“There,” she said. “Four hours stay where they belong.”

The girl set the brush down. “They charge my season to a household that is not mine. Did you know that.”

“I know,” said Ning Yan. “The difference comes out of the eighth estate's line. Shen Xiu's book carries it.”

“It should come out of mine. I was on the floor.”

“You were on a shift standing in for mine,” said Ning Yan. “If the charge came to me it would sit on the same line as the burial entry. The book keeps us apart on purpose.”

The girl looked at her leg and then at the steam coming off the troughs.

“I heard him again,” she said. “The man in the good coat. At the gate yesterday at the third hour. He asked the clerk for the place where you sleep and the name on the carriers' papers. He put a coin on the table and said he pays for the writing as well as the answer.”

“What did the clerk do.”

“He wrote that he was asked and that he did not know. The man thanked him and paid for the writing.” The girl picked at the cord on the splint without loosening it. “He did not ask the third thing. Everyone on the woodpile knows what the third thing is and nobody says it.”

Ning Yan sat on the edge of the trough wall with her left hand on her knee. The steam got into her lungs and sat there, and she coughed once and swallowed the second one.

“You sleep under this roof now,” she said. “Off the floor for the season. That is the healer's order and the book's order together.”

“I sleep under this roof and I copy slates and I hear everything the woodpile hears.” The girl met her eyes. “I know which bunk is yours. I have known since the autumn. No man has got it from me.”

“I know that,” said Ning Yan.

“Do not thank me.”

“I was not going to.”

They sat a moment in the steam. Outside, the frost was coming on and the channel water made its half-skinned noise behind the furnace wall, and nine men moved on the floor above them with the steady tread of a crew that has learned to work short.

## The Difference

“You hate me,” said Ning Yan.

It was not a question and she did not make it one. She had said both causes aloud at the burial in front of nine men, and the girl had not been there, and the girl had heard them anyway, because a burial ground above the road carries voices down to the wash house when the wind is right.

The girl considered it the way she had considered the slate, carefully and without hurry.

“My leg is broken and my season is gone and the eighth estate pays for me to lie here,” she said. “Two men are dead. You signed nothing that put them there and you refused something that did. I have had three weeks to work out whether those are the same thing.”

“Are they.”

“They are the same entry,” said the girl. “The register says so in your own hand. That is enough for the book. It is not enough for me to hate you on, because hate takes hours and I spend mine copying.”

Ning Yan nodded once. Xu An's mother had told her she would not be at her door and had not said it as a kindness. This was different and not kinder, and she took it the way she takes hours, without arguing.

“I work the cold end at the ninth hour,” she said. “Two blocks in the week. The clerk writes me slow.”

“I know,” said the girl. “The whole floor knows. You cough and you hold the wall straight and you sign left-handed. The new hands think it is a lesson. The old hands know it is a debt.”

Ning Yan stood. The stripe along her ribs burned cold where the steam had got to it, and her right arm hung dead at her side and she did not test it, because she had tested it twice already that day.

“Copy nothing after the ninth hour,” she said again. “The healer's order holds over the household's.”

“The healer's order holds,” said the girl. “Go and hold your wall.”

Ning Yan went up to the floor and worked the channel for four hours with her left hand on the rail. The heat leaned and she took it and put it down into the plates, and the leak ran sideways and the cough came and she swallowed it. At the second night hour she signed again at the table, four and a half hours in the ugly slant, and the clerk entered it against the twenty-sixth day.

Two hundred and sixty-five worked in the winter book. Six hundred and seventy-five standing. Twenty-two and a half in her own hand on the running page.

She walked back past the wash house and saw the lamp still burning and the girl asleep against the wall with the slate on her knee. She took the slate gently and set it on the floor and drew the cloth up over the splint where the night air got in.

“You see her,” said Yuan. “You see her and you carry her.”

“I see her,” said Ning Yan. “I carry the hours. There is a difference and I keep it.”

“I see the difference,” said Yuan. “I hold it with you and I do not move it.”

She climbed the stair and lay in the second bunk with nine men breathing round her. The arithmetic sat beside her, plain and ungiving. Two hundred and sixty-five. Six hundred and seventy-five. The girl asleep below with a lath and a cord for a season, charged to a household that was not hers, holding the place where Ning Yan sleeps behind her teeth.

She did not thank her. She did not need to. The ledger knew.
