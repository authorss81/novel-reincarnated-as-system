# The Escort She Does Not Have

The road opens on cleared days at the ninth hour for carts and at the tenth for feet, and the Watch writes down every name that goes through in both directions.

Ning Yan learned this standing at the terrace rail on the twenty-ninth day with Old Gao's letter in her sleeve. The barrier at the fourth bridge sat four miles below in a cut full of morning shadow, a trestle and a board and two men, and beyond it the Lowwater road ran white and rutted down toward the town. Market smoke stood over it in a flat layer.

The thaw had taken the middle of the road and the frost gave it back every night, and every cleared day the carts went down loaded and came up loaded differently.

She had to get down that road. The tower stood at the bottom of it with four of her county copies walled behind a fifth item, and the county office stood on the square with the only stamp that could make her hours into a licence, and between her and both of them stood eight hundred jin and a man in a good coat who paid for writing as well as answers.

“You go down alone and you die on paper first,” said Yuan. “You know this and you hate it.”

“I go down alone and I die on paper first,” said Ning Yan. “I know it and I plan round it. That is what the rail is for.”

She went down the nine steps at the eighth hour with the letter in her sleeve and her coat shut over the tablet. The outer yard held about thirty people in the morning cold, and the gate clerk sat at his table with his blotter and his bell, and the man of about fifty with grey at the temple stood ten feet from the table with his hat in his hands, talking to a carrier in a low voice.

He did not look at her. He had looked at her once on the eighteenth day for four seconds and used three, and he had not looked since, because a man who does a thing the same way every time does not look at the body. She felt his not-looking the way you feel a wall standing straight, and she went past him to the clerk and set her slate down.

“I want the cart sheet,” she said.

The clerk turned the sheet toward her. It was a carriers' list like the one the girl copied at the wash house, names and marks and loads, and at the bottom of it the water cart stood entered for the tenth hour with Old Gao's mark.

“I want to go down with the water,” said Ning Yan. “Entered. In daylight. On the sheet.”

The clerk looked past her at the barrier roster. “The Watch holds you for your own safety. A cart is not a guard.”

“I do not want a guard,” said Ning Yan. “A guard is a place. I want a cart with a sheet and witnesses. Old Gao carries letters for free and water for the town. Put me on his sheet as a carrier's hand and I ride the boards down.”

The clerk wrote for a moment. Then he shook his head.

“Old Gao says yes or it is no,” he said. “He is at the trestle.”

## The Trestle

Old Gao stood at the trestle below the gate with his hands on the cart rail and his breath going white. He had carried water for Ning Xue twenty years past, and he had put a hand on a young Watchman's arm in the roundhouse in the autumn, and he remembered everything the way old men on a mountain remember, which is to say by weight.

“You want to ride my water down,” he said.

“I want to ride your sheet down,” said Ning Yan. “Entered as a hand. In daylight. With the Watch watching.”

“You are the most watched person on this mountain,” said Old Gao. “If you ride my cart the man in the good coat watches my cart. If you walk, he watches you. There is no way down that he does not watch, because watching is what he is paid for.”

“Then let him watch a sheet,” said Ning Yan. “A sheet with my name on it, going down at the tenth hour with water. He can buy the sheet. He can buy the clerk who keeps it. What he cannot buy is me off it, because I am on it.”

Old Gao looked at her for a long time. Then he looked at the barrier board, where the captain's line stood in its roundhouse hand, and at the grey-templed man up the slope who was still not looking down.

“The captain holds you,” he said. “Held, in his own hand, no order on it. You know what that means.”

“It means I am not ordered to stay,” said Ning Yan. “It means one man decided I stay alive. Ask him if a cart at the tenth hour breaks his holding.”

Old Gao laughed once, short. “I carried your mother up this road when she was too tired to walk. I will ask him. If he says yes, you ride the boards with the barrels. If he says no, you walk back up nine steps and wait for a better day.”

He went up to the barrier and spoke to the Watchman there for two minutes. The Watchman looked down the road, looked up at the gate, and nodded once. Old Gao came back with water slopping over the boards.

“Tenth hour,” he said. “On the sheet. The Watch watches the cart from the cut to the town. That is all it is. It is not a guard and it is not a favour. It is water going down.”

## The Letter

She rode the rail down at the tenth hour with her feet braced against the barrels and the whole mountain falling away below her.

The letter in her sleeve was from Du Hong, brought up by the carts two days past. She had read it four times in the quarters and knew it by heart, and she read it again on the boards with the wind taking the edge of the paper, because reading it again was work.

It said the four copies sat behind the fifth item and none had gone upward. It said Feng coughed worse and copied fairly and ate little. It said a man had been to three county desks on the square asking which clerk keeps the copies of the Qinghe advancement, and had paid, and had asked after carriers' papers by name. It said the tower loft had a spare mat and the town had ears, and to come down entered and in daylight or not at all.

She folded it and put it back. The cart went through the cut with the Watchman walking the rail above it, and the barrier board fell behind, and the town came up to meet them with its smoke and its noise and its square full of carts.

Two hundred and eighty-three worked in the winter book. Six hundred and fifty-seven standing. Twenty-seven in her own hand.

She did the numbers on the boards with the barrels knocking against her knees. Two blocks in the week, nine hours, against a household that moved thirty-odd with eleven hands. The tithe did not wait for her to travel. It moved while she rode, and she would have to earn her part of it on the ground.

“You fear it,” said Yuan. “You want the ground and you fear it.”

“I fear it,” said Ning Yan. “I want it more. That is why I ride a water cart instead of walking.”

“You ride it well,” said Yuan. “I see you and I stay with you.”

The cart came into the square at the eleventh hour. The county office stood on the far side of it with its shutters open and its queue already forming. The Lantern Register tower stood above the roofs with its lamp unlit at midday. Between them the market ran its noise, and through the noise she saw a man in a good coat standing at a county desk with a coin on the wood, polite and unhurried, paying for records in daylight.

She got off the boards with her feet in Lowwater mud for the first time in four months, entered on a sheet, watched, and alive. The Watchman turned the cart and went back up without looking at her again, which was correct. The guard was water. The water was gone.

The man in the good coat did not look at her either. He did not need to. She was a body on a square, and a body is the one proof that cannot be bought at a desk.
