# The Left Hand's Work

It was the ninth hour of the night on the twenty-first day and the melt had got into the channel.

That was the whole of it and it was ordinary, and the ordinariness is the part that has to be understood first.

There are nine hundred tons of slag sitting above the settling floor because the slag is cold now, and slag is a thing you take out of a channel with bars and buckets. You take it out because when the melt comes it makes a river out of the hole the slag came from, and a river at night in a building with one exit is how eleven people drown in ninety seconds.

So it was a job, and it was the worst job of the winter, and everybody in that building knew it and everybody in that building had done it eleven times.

There were four men in the hole instead of six, because four was what was on the foreman's board, and the board had been made up on the sixteenth day by a man who was short of hands for the third time in a fortnight and who had gone up to the shoring and looked at the four posts over the cold end and signed for the line at about the seventh hour on the day before.

He had signed it, and his name was on it, and the man who inspects forty-one shoring posts had inspected it at the first hour of the week, and it was in a book.

At the ninth hour of the night Ning Yan went down on the cold end with a plate hook and her left hand on the rail, because the cold end was the only place on that floor where one arm was worth anything, and she had learned that in nine days.

## What the Melt Does

The channel had been a cut nine inches deep in the autumn. On the second night of the thaw it was a cut with two inches of running water in it and it was not running, because the thing you were supposed to do about running water in a channel is clear it, and the thing that was on the board was not clearing, it was shoring, and the shoring is overhead and the water is underfoot.

The heat coming off the wall was in the mist now. That is what a melt does to a furnace floor: the air fills with steam off warm water and cold stone, and everything at head height disappears about twelve feet out, and a man at the far end of the lip is a shape.

She worked the channel from the ninth hour to the first night hour with her hand on the rail and the crack open and the water moving off the lip at the rate it was moving.

Her right arm came in at about the second hour. It did that for about eleven minutes and then went out.

She did not look up for nine minutes after that, because looking up on a channel in steam is what a body does instead of looking at its own feet, and she had two men on that lip and there was nothing she could do for them and looking at them was a thing she was doing in place of work.

At the ninth minute she looked up.

The near shoring post on the cold end was not leaning. The line in it had not opened. But the beam above it — nine feet of cast slag, held on four posts, holding up the lip that two men were standing on — had come down about two inches at the near end and had not come down at the far end, and it had not come down with a noise, it had come down with the sound a thing makes when it stops being held in one place.

She saw it.

She did not shout, which she has never been able to explain and has not tried to explain. What she did was put both feet and say the only word that was in her, which was Xu An's name, once, at the top of her lungs, in a room full of steam.

## Nineteen Seconds

She was a hundred and forty feet down the floor with one arm and eleven inches of water in a channel and nine feet of lift.

The lip was twenty feet off. There were men between her and it. It is a channel floor; you do not run on a channel floor, you run along it, and she got eleven feet before she had to put a hand out.

Cao Lei was on the near side of the beam and Xu An was on the far side with his back to it, and the beam went across the lip between them and came down onto the near shoring, and the near shoring went.

It took about four seconds.

Cao Lei went eleven feet sideways and off the edge of the settling floor into the nine hundred tons and Xu An went down on the near side under the middle of it, and a girl of twenty-four who was on the cold end on the other side of the lip went off the lip on the same movement without knowing she had moved and was sitting on the stone twelve feet away with her leg gone one way.

Ning Yan got to the settling floor edge in about nineteen seconds.

She had counted them later, off the wall of the furnace, by the length of the water coming out of the channel, and she was not going to be asked about them again.

She was on the edge of nine hundred tons of slag with the beam lying across it and a man under the middle of it, and she went down onto it on her left arm and her right hand — which did not work — and she got both hands under the beam at a place four feet from his chest, and she lifted it.

It came up about two inches.

She has the arms of a furnace-crew's daughter who has carried a broken boy two miles, and a Condensing stage one with a crack in her chest that leaks at the rim, and she got a nine-foot slag beam up about two inches off a body, on her hands, in the steam, alone.

Nobody was coming. There was a boy at the top of the stairs with a lamp who had gone up at the sound of it, and eleven men on the channel floor, and a heap that had to be cleared with bars and buckets by four men of whom two were down, and the nearest man to the edge was ninety feet away and coming on his hands and knees through two inches of melt water.

She held it at two inches for about eleven seconds and it was going, and she felt it go, and she had to decide what she was going to do with the next four seconds.

## The Channel

The channel was filling.

She heard it before she saw it. The noise under the beam, which had been the ordinary noise of a running channel, had become the noise of a channel with nothing in it and too much in it at the far end.

In about forty seconds the lip she was standing on would have two feet of slag-heated water standing on it, and from there it would go over the channel floor, and there were eleven men on the channel floor in the dark at the far end of a building with one stair.

She let go of the beam.

Not because she decided to. Because her hands stopped, and she went off the edge of the settling floor on her back and down nine feet of slag into the melt water and came up on the channel floor on her hands and knees with her mouth full of it.

She got to the channel.

She had been on the channel floor for nineteen seconds in the whole of it, which is a thing she will be told is nothing, nineteen seconds against eight hours, and it is nothing, and she is the one who has to hold the number in both hands at once for the rest of her life.

The channel was on the lip. She got her left hand into the rail and put the crack open all the way and took the heat out of the water in front of her and sent it down into the plates and the plates took it and the water stopped climbing.

“Up,” she said. “Get up. All of you, off the floor, up the stair, now, all of you, and count at the top and say the number out loud.”

They went. Eleven men went up a stair in the dark because a woman on the channel floor told them to in a voice they had heard for two months, and at the top of the stair a boy with a lamp counted them out loud twice and got fourteen, and the fourteen was the right number, and that was the whole of what she did that night.

It took her from the first night hour to the fourth to hold it.

The water did not come over. That is the part she cannot say to anybody.

It is not that she was brave and it is not that she was strong and it is not even that she is a Condensing stage one. It is that she is a furnace-crew's daughter, and she was taught a procedure at fifteen by a shift leader who put himself slightly downhill of whatever he was looking at, and the procedure is *hold the channel*.

There is no version of a channel coming over where the person on it is anybody else.

It is not a discovery. It is a habit, and the habit is older than the crack, and the crack only means that she does not have to spend anything extra to hold it.

## What Was Under It

The heap came down at about the second night hour, with bars, in less than a quarter of an hour, and the healer got up the stairs forty minutes after the beam went.

Forty minutes is a very long time if you have been under a thing.

They had Cao Lei's body on the floor of the settling hall before they had moved the beam off Xu An. Xu An was alive when they came to him and he was not alive when they got him out, and the healer — a grey woman of about sixty who had been on this mountain thirty years and had said a sentence once, in the autumn, about the wet lungs in a boy — got down off the stair with them and looked at him on the stone.

Then she looked at Ning Yan, who was sitting against the wall of the channel with her right arm in her lap and her hands white.

“He was under it forty minutes,” said the healer.

“I know how long he was under it.”

“You do not.”

“No,” said Ning Yan. “I know how long it was.”

## The Other One

The girl of twenty-four was on the stone against the wall with her leg out straight in front of her and a folded coat under her shoulders, because there had been nothing else to put under her.

She had not gone off the lip slowly. Everybody within thirty feet of a thing that size moves.

“Can you stand on it,” said Ning Yan.

“Ask the healer.”

The healer had not come down to her yet.

Ning Yan put her left hand flat on the girl’s knee above the break and held it there and tried nothing else, and after a while the girl stopped making a noise and opened her eyes.

“You are the one from the gate,” she said. “He asked where you sleep.”

“Yes.”

“He did not get it.”

“No.”

“Good,” said the girl, and shut her eyes again.

## The Thing in Her Head

He was very quiet.

She had been expecting something. She had been expecting him to be useful, because for four months he had been the only thing in her life that had ever offered her a choice, and at some point between the ninth hour of the night and the fourth hour she had heard herself wait for him, out loud, on a channel floor, in the voice she used for tallies.

“Bring the healer.”

“I cannot do that,” said Yuan.

“You can do that.”

“No. Not this. There is nothing here to reach.” He was quiet for about a second. “I cannot read a man on that floor and I cannot read past you and I have been sitting in your head for four months being a voice, and I could not have lifted that beam by an inch, and if I could I would have had to take it out of you, and you would not have had a left hand left.”

“Then get up the stair and bring the healer yourself.”

“No,” he said. “That is the whole of it. I am sorry. There is no version of tonight where I am any use at all.”

So she held the channel for three more hours on her own and she was not a god in it and there was nothing in her head but a woman who knew a procedure.

That is what she has never been able to put down afterward, and she has put down almost everything else: that the only time in her life she wanted him to be something other than a voice, he told her no, correctly, in the first second, without hesitating.

They came at about the ninth hour of the morning. Nine of them, up and down, in daylight, on a floor.

She had been awake for two days and she was on her feet when they came, and she had washed in the trough at the fifth hour and she was in a clean coat and her hair was wet and her right arm was out of its own weight.

She had washed. Nobody had told her to. It was the only thing she could do that was not about the beam, and it was the reason the two men in that hall looked at her and then away.

She washed their faces. There was not a facility in that building for washing two dead men and she did it in the trough at the cold end with a rag and cold water because it was what her mother had done for her father twice in her life, and she did it with her left hand, one at a time, and she got the ground off them, and she was not sick, and afterwards she was sick for two days.

Then she said it.

The layer had said it. It had been inside her chest since the fourth hour, and she had felt it arrive in the same flat way it had arrived in the autumn when eight hundred jin had been entered against the Chosen One, and she had not been able to work for the rest of that day then either.

[The furnace at Qinghe. Two of the crew are ended beneath a fallen beam.]

She stood up off the cold end of the channel in front of nine men with wet hair and a clean coat, and they all heard it, because everything in the bound hears it, and every one of them had heard a sentence in their chest on the eighth day of the winter that said her name.

## What She Said to Yuan

“You are going to tell me now what happened.”

He did not answer for a while.

“I am not going to tell you anything that makes it smaller.”

“Fine.”

“I want to be clear,” he said. “I have had four hours and I have got it in the order it happened, and you are not going to be able to put one piece of it somewhere else.”

“You chose to be at the Hall on the sixteenth day, and the two men stood up on the sixteenth day, and you filed on the twentieth. All of those are yours.”

“I know they are mine.”

“The beam was wrong because a foreman was short of hands, and that is an ordinary thing and it is not yours and it never was.”

“I know that as well.”

“And you could not have reached it.”

“I know that better than you do.”

“You did the right thing on the channel.”

“Don’t.”

“You did the only thing in that building that could have been done, and nine men are alive because of it, and it does not touch the other thing, and you are not allowed to put the two of them in the same place, and that is not me being kind to you. That is me telling you that a document that puts them in the same place is a false document, and you have spent your whole life on not writing those.”

She stood in a furnace hall at the ninth hour of the morning with two men laid out on a plank and a boiler of water going on the fire behind her.

“I am not going to put them in the same place,” said Ning Yan.

“You are going to say both of them out loud.”

“I am going to say both of them out loud.”

“To nine men, and then in a register, and then at the burial, and nobody in this sect is going to let you do that twice.”

“No,” she said. “Nobody is going to let me do that twice.”
