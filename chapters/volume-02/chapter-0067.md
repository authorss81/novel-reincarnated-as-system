# Ash and Hours

The seventh hour of the evening on the seventeenth day, the table by the furnace mouth, four feet from heat that came out of the opening in a wall you could not put a hand through.

She had come to be entered and she had been entered, and she was waiting for the second half of it, which was that a clerk who enters an hour is a clerk who reads it back.

“Four and a half,” said the clerk, “and the last night.”

“Read it back.”

He did. “Seventeenth day, ninth hour to the second night hour. Four and a half hours.”

“Enter it.”

“Entered.”

She had learned in two nights that he would not enter a number twice and would not enter a number that was not also on the floor's board, and that both of those were habits in a man of twenty-three and not rules, and that rules could be argued with and habits could only be waited out.

So she waited.

## Above Her Head

The settling work had started on the third day past, underneath the slag heaps, which is a hole in the floor at the cold end where the channel is taken up to be relined and where four men work with the heat coming down on them from nine tons of cooling slag.

Two men of the eleven who did it were above her head.

She had not been able to stop it and had not tried, and it had turned out that the not trying was the correct action, because two men on a settling floor doing a double are doing a double and there is no version of the furnace floor where anybody looks up, and she was on the channel at the cold end with her left hand on the rail and her right arm in and out of about eleven minutes an hour.

They were cheerful. That was the thing she was not ready for.

They had come down yesterday and worked, and they had worked every shift since, and at the end of the fourth hour they had argued about the second verse of the weighing song at the lip with about forty words of it left out for decency, and they had done it on purpose so that the floor could hear, and she had heard it from four feet below with her hand on cold iron.

Cao Lei had laughed so hard on the sixth night that he had to put his forehead on the slag heap for a minute. Xu An had kept the time.

It was the first full day of the doubles. There were about twenty-three days of thaw left.

## The Counting

The clerk did the counting the way he did everything, which was slowly, and he had got to the part of it where he was enjoying himself.

That was new and she did not like it. On the first night it had been duty, and on the fourth it had been care, and on the seventh there was something in the rhythm of the man that she recognised from the tally-singing she had been taught at eleven, the thing where counting out loud in a room gets into your hands and your hands keep going after your mouth has stopped.

He had a stack of four small sheets on the left of the book, and he took one off the top, and he squared it, and he read the name.

“Cao Lei. Ninth to the second night. Four and a half. Standing against a member's shift, certified.”

He looked up.

“I am required to note the standing of it,” he said. “Not to change it.”

“Noted.”

“Xu An. Ninth to the second night. Four and a half. Standing against a member's shift, certified.”

“Noted.”

He squared the second sheet. Then he picked up the third.

“Eight hours of settling work each, by the two, from the seventeenth to the close of the thaw, on the foreman's board, entered by the foreman and not by me, which means it does not come to my book until he brings me the sheet and I would say a month, if you want to know how the arithmetic works.”

He said it without any weight at all, which was how he said everything.

She looked up at the slag heaps. There was a man on the far lip with a bar in both hands and his back to her and a boy of fifteen carrying a bucket past him, and the heat coming down off nine tons of cooling slag in a sheet you could see if you wanted to.

Eight hours of settling work each, a day, in addition to four and a half on the floor. Through a thaw. In a hole under a heap.

“Keep going,” said Ning Yan.

## The Tray

The tray was on the end of the table at her right hand, four inches away, and it had been there on the four nights she had come to this table, and she had not once looked in it because you do not look in a clerk's tray.

The clerk put the fifth sheet down and picked up the sixth and squared it, and while he was squaring it he had both hands busy and his head down and the tray was open.

There were nine papers in it. Eight of them were the size of a thumb and folded twice and had the look of something that comes in every day and goes out every day. The ninth was bigger and it was unfolded and it had been on the top for a while, because the top is where a clerk puts the thing he has not finished.

She did not move her head. She had been a laundry-hand's daughter for eight years and her family's trade was reading a room with eleven people in it without any of them knowing she was doing it, and she read a debt paper upside down and backwards in about nine seconds.

It was a paper on a house.

It was not a household account. It was a schedule of debts with dates against them and a column headed by a name, and the name at the top of the column, in a fair round hand with a brush held well, was **Hou Sanliang**.

She read the dates.

There were three lines with household names in them and a date at the end of each, and the dates were eleven months and eleven days apart.

Eleven months and eleven days apart. Eleven of them.

She had seen that spacing once before in her life. She had seen it in the second week of the autumn, in a corridor off the north end of the Hall, in the second half of a document with three men's seal on it, in a schedule of nine households struck in one brush stroke over eleven years, with Qiu standing there having never seen it.

Nine households. Eleven years. The same spacing, to the day.

The three household names on the debt paper were not in that schedule. She knew two of them and had never once been told the other seven and had not gone looking, and these three were a subset of the nine.

And the column at the top of the paper said that a man called Hou Sanliang, of Lowwater, was the creditor on all three, and under the three dates there was a fourth line that was not a household at all.

It said: *standing against the ledger of the maintaining members of Qinghe, per their schedule of the eleven years.*

Per their schedule.

A man down at the bottom of that hill holding paper on three of the nine households that three men on the top of it had struck in one brush stroke, and the striking dates the same to the day, and his name written on it as the creditor in a hand that had been taught letters and had not been surprised by them for thirty years.

## What She Did

She looked at the tray long enough to read it three times and then she looked at the clerk, and the clerk was counting.

He was going along the ninth sheet with his brush, saying the numbers out, and he would be at the end of them in about two minutes, and then he would square the stack and put it on the left, and then he would put the tray back under the desk, and the paper on top of the tray would go with the tray to wherever a paper goes at the end of a day, and he would not know that it had been read.

There was no bell. There was no sentence coming round inside her chest, and she had spent four months being told there would be.

There was a piece of paper four inches from her left hand and a clerk counting out loud and about two minutes of the room's attention in the wrong place. She could have taken it. She could have asked the young man at the next table to carry a message up to a captain of the Watch on the barrier of the fourth bridge, and it would have been in the square at Lowwater by the noon hour tomorrow.

She did not do any of those things.

“You are ahead of me,” she said.

“I am not, I am on your sheet.”

“You were on the fifth and now you are on the ninth and you skipped three.”

“The three are Xu An and Cao Lei on the settling, and one is the floor leader’s own sheet.” He went back. “Nine. Fourth hour past midday to the second night hour. Two and a half. Working a plate hook with one arm.”

“That is me.”

“That is you,” said the clerk. “Did you want it entered under a note? Because a hook with one arm is still a hook.”

“No note.”

“It will look like anybody else's sheet.”

“It is anybody else's sheet,” said Ning Yan. “That is the point of it.”

He entered it and squared the stack and put it on the left, and then he put the tray back under the desk with the top of it showing, because the top of it always shows, and he said, “Nine hours this week. The sheet goes to the quota office on the third evening and the clerk up there is slower than me.”

“Thank you,” said Ning Yan.

“You have said that twice in three days.”

“I have. It is not going to stop.”

## What She Took Up the Stair

She went up the stair to the crew quarters with the cold on her and nine hours in a week in a book a hundred feet away that she had not asked for and could not stop, and above her for the rest of the night there were two men on a settling floor being cheerful about a song.

She did not write it in the log. There was a line she could have put in — *the creditor on three of the nine is Hou Sanliang of Lowwater and his paper cites the maintaining members' schedule by name* — and she looked at the coat lining and did not write it, because she had read a paper upside down over a clerk's tray while he counted, and that is not a thing you can defend in four years with a pen.

But she did not need the log. That was the thing that came up the stairs with her.

The eight hundred jin had a name.

Not a place and not a shape and not four hundred lamps. A man, with a fair round hand and a brush held well, in a counting room eleven days down a road, holding paper on three households that three men four streets away had struck in one brush stroke eleven years ago and had never once been asked to account for.

She had known the price existed. She had known it was priced at a furnace-crew's year, and she had known that meant somebody had entered it knowing who could afford it. She had carried that for eleven days without once needing to look at it again.

Now there was a name, and the name was on the page of a clerk four feet from a furnace, and the name was in the same book as the thing she had sworn about in a room with no roof.

That was two weeks ago. It had taken eleven days.

She stood on the fourth step of nine with her hand on the cold rail and her right arm out of its own weight and two men above her being twenty-three days from the end of a double shift.

It was going to be a name at the end of it, and she had known for eleven days what the end of a name like that looks like, and she had not once let herself finish the sentence about who would be standing nearest it.

Four and a half hours. Nine hours. The spring.

She went up the rest of the stairs and did not say one word to either of them about any of it.
