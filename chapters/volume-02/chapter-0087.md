# The Name for Attendance

Du Hong told her what the county wants the way he tells everything he has decided, badly hidden and entirely steady.

They sat in the tower loft at the ninth hour of the evening with the shutter open to the cold and the square noise thin below. He had brought soup and a slate with figures on it, and oil on his sleeves from tower work. Feng coughed on the copying floor beneath them, steady through the boards.

“The county clerk read me the surety terms,” he said. “A living name for attendance at every county hour. My name in the county book for your attendance. I was told what it is.”

“What is it,” said Ning Yan.

“If I live, you attend and the book holds. If I die, it voids and the debt comes to you.” He set the slate down. “Every county hour. Not furnace hours. County hours. The office on the square, the tower readings, the stamp days. I stand for you being where the book says.”

She looked at him across the mat. Eighteen, ambitious, generous, bad at lying. His file carried her hand in two places, the advancement and the report order unfiled, and with a claim standing that made him the cheap door and her the expensive one. He had said that before she did, on the sixth step, and he had carried her copies down the road in his coat with no name on them, and been noticed for it.

“I have not asked you,” she said.

“You have not,” said Du Hong. “I ask to stand. That is different.”

“You were told what it costs.”

“The clerk read it twice and wrote it out for me on a slip,” said Du Hong. “That is what a man does when he means to sign and does not want to have been lied to.”

She thought of the burial register in her own left hand, the first document naming her as the cause of anything. She thought of two names the clerk wrote slow and a mother who will not be at her door. She thought of a girl with a lath splint copying slates, charged to a household not hers. Taking names is what she does. It had never yet been free.

“Why,” she said.

He laughed once, short. “Because your hand is in my file twice and my name is nowhere that matters. Because you put yourself expensive to keep me cheap and it did not work. Because somebody has to stand and I am standing here.”

“What do you want,” said Ning Yan.

He looked at the shutter instead of at her, and for a while the only sound was Feng's brush two floors down through the boards.

“My name in a book where it is the whole line of it,” he said. “Everything since the autumn has been filed under you, because your hand is the hand that put me where I am, and a file does not care which of the two of us did the work. A county book has my name and nothing else in it. That is all I want out of it, and it costs you something, and I would rather be told than not.”

“You die and it comes to me,” said Ning Yan. “You live and every county hour owns you.”

“I live,” said Du Hong. “I attend with you, hour by hour, and the book says my name on it every time.”

## The Sponsors' Silence

In the morning she went up with the water cart to hear what the three sponsors say about a ceiling.

They said nothing. Lu Kang received her in the corridor off the roofless Hall with Deng Chao and Pei An beside him, courteous and unhurried, and listened while she laid the clearance and the application before them. The seals stood on the paper above the old refusal. The queue had seen them. The county would see them again.

“The county countersigns,” said Lu Kang. “Quota clearance produced. The seal stands.”

“The Foundation writ,” said Ning Yan. “Within two years, outside the schedule, on your signature. Where does it stand under a pending filing.”

Silence. Three men who have never once asked her a question looked at the paper and not at her. Deng Chao's brush stopped. Pei An folded his hands.

“It stands where the licence puts it,” said Lu Kang at last. “Foundation on that writ reaches only through a licence. Without one, Foundation on that writ is practice the schedule does not carry.”

Unregistered practice under a schedule it does not appear on. Breach of the sponsorship that issued it. She worked it out standing in the corridor with the cold coming through the roofless beams, the way she works tallies, without moving her face. The writ was never a shortcut. It was a leash with a better room in it, and the pending page kills it dead.

“You know it,” she said.

“We know it,” said Lu Kang. “We have known it since the seal.”

“You say nothing.”

“We say nothing,” said Deng Chao. “The county reads paper. We hold paper.”

She left them with the clearance in her sleeve and the writ dead between them unsaid. Three men knew and had said nothing. That was the shape of sponsors. They hold her rank, the schedule, the nine-household book, and now a dead shortcut, and they say nothing because a servant-born who advances inside their schedule is the only thing on it that ever worked.

“You see it,” said Yuan. “You know the writ dies and you carry it.”

“I see it,” said Ning Yan. “I carry it to the square. The county writes it dead. Not me.”

## The Entry

At the third hour past midday they went to the county office on the square together, entered and in daylight.

Sun He sat at the far table with the county book open. The queue moved. When her name came she stood before him with the sponsors' seal, the quota clearance signed looking past her, and Du Hong beside her with his hands at his sides.

“I stand surety,” said Du Hong. “For her attendance at every county hour. Write my name.”

The registrar looked at him once, the way a man looks at a line.

“You were told what it is.”

“I was told,” said Du Hong.

Sun He entered it. The brush moved in the county book, fair and final. A living name for attendance, void on death, debt to her. Du Hong watched his name go down without flinching, because flinching is for rooms where somebody might save you and this was not one.

Ning Yan stood beside him with the clearance in her sleeve and the application in her hand, complete at last: seal, clearance, surety. Three legs and the county's stamp to go. She did not thank him. Thanks between people who know the cost is a thing done by not saying it, the way Feng does it.

They walked out into the square with the stamp days ahead and the tower lamp behind. The man in the good coat paid at the second desk with a coin on the wood. The counting room stacked paper by household. The claim stood over all of it, unanswered, waiting to be read before any paper.

“You let him,” said Yuan. “You let him stand and you carry it.”

“I let him,” said Ning Yan. “He decides. I carry what he decides. That is the whole of what I have.”

“It is much,” said Yuan. “I see it and I hold it with you.”

The application was complete. The county would read the claim before the paper. That was next, and they both knew it.
