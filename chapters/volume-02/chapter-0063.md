# The Lowwater Market

The first market after the ice, and two days after the road opened, and the terrace rail at the third hour past midday, and the whole of the Lowwater road going past below her like a river with the ice still on both sides of it.

She had not seen a market since the eighth day of the autumn. It was smaller than she remembered, and it took her a moment to understand why, and then to be ashamed of noticing.

Snow comes down and goes to ice and takes the mud with it. Eleven days of ash had put a grey skin on everything that had stood outside for a week. The carts were all in one channel because the rest of the road was bad, and the whole of the visible world of her sect had shrunk into about forty feet of churned white between the fourth bridge and the cut.

The light off it was throwable. She had to put her head slightly to one side to be able to look down at it, and she did that for about a minute and a half, and then she went to find out whether anybody down there knew where she slept.

## The Barrier, Again

She got as far as the trestle.

Old Gao was on the trestle this time, with the water cart and a rope he was coiling, and he looked at her coming and did not stop coiling it.

“Morning,” he said.

“Morning. I want to go down to the square for an hour.”

“No.”

“I am not going into the Verge and I am not going to the Broken Kiln and I am not leaving the road. One hour, to the square and back.”

“No,” said Old Gao.

She had known it would be no before she got there. What she had not expected was the sentence that came with it.

“You have a claim on you,” said Old Gao, “and a man on the gate has written your name in the barrier book as *held*, and if you go down there today the Captain is a Watchman who did not stop a woman walking off a mountain, and there is no explaining a Watchman afterwards. So go home.”

It was said kindly. It was said in the voice of a man who had carried a water cart round this district for thirty years and had never once in his life been thanked for it by the sect.

“I am not going to murder anybody, Old Gao.”

“No,” he agreed. “You are going to look at a letter. And I have been sixty-one years in this district and I have watched a great many people go down that hill for less than a letter.”

She stood at the trestle in the throwable light and could not answer that, because it was true, and because she had spent six days deciding what she would do if she could get down there and the list had one item on it and the item was *be somewhere else*.

“Send a letter up for me,” she said.

“That I can do. It costs you nothing and you will not get it today. The carts go up at the fourth hour this afternoon and whatever is on them is on the Lowwater side by morning, and that is the fastest thing there is.”

“Who is at the bottom of that road who would take a letter for money.”

“Half of Lowwater,” said Old Gao, “and the tower, and I will tell you which of them will not: the county desk on the square will take it and will read it before they send it and will copy it, because that is what a county desk is for.”

“The tower.”

“The tower boy takes a thing up for nothing if you ask him at the door in a plain voice, and that boy has been up this mountain forty times since the eighth day past with somebody else’s paper, and I do not say that to a person who might want it made difficult.”

She thanked him once. He waved it away, and she went back up nine steps.

## The Letter

It came up at the eighth hour of the following morning with the carts, folded twice in oiled paper and given to the gate by a boy of about thirteen who had walked from the bottom of the road because he had been paid eleven coppers and had opinions about it.

There was a wax on it, thin, pressed with the plain die of the Lantern Register and nothing else.

She took it to the Hall because the Hall had a table and a brazier and no roof and nobody in it at that hour, and she broke it standing.

Four sheets. One of them was not paper from her own log.

*Ning Yan—*

The hand was the wrong shape of writing, the hand of a man who has taught himself letters late and holds the brush too hard and is frightened of a page. It went down the left side of the paper and it crowded at the bottom, and it said:

*Four sheets delivered to the tower, second day of the market, at the fourth hour. Nobody's name on them. The Keeper read all four and asked me which county wrote each and I said you would know.*

*He put them behind the fifth thing on the shelf in the copying room and he entered none of them upward. He asked me to tell you he did not enter them, and that it is the first month in twenty-two years he has entered none of a thing, and he says you are not to thank him for it.*

She read that three times.

Below it, in a different hand — Du Hong's, faster, and not frightened of anything:

*Ning Yan. There is a man in the square. He has been to the county desk on the square, which is not the tower. He asked three different clerks which of them keeps the copies of the Qinghe advancement, and which of them keeps the copies that go up.*

*He is not carrying anything with anybody's name on it. He is paying. They are telling him. I do not know who he is sending it to.*

*I would go up but I have been at the tower three days running and I think they have started to notice me, and if I leave they will say I left. Write me one line and I will do what it says.*

*— D.*

She read the whole thing twice and then sat on the floor of a hall with no roof with her back against the north brazier and her coat open and the plate against her ribs and four county copies of her own handwriting eleven days down a mountain in a tower in a room behind a fifth item.

Feng had read all four. Feng had asked which county wrote each one. Feng had entered none of them.

Eight days ago that man had four unindexed copies upward and a Bureau order telling him not to. Now he had five things on a shelf in a room and a twenty-two-year habit holding, and he had told a boy to write it down so that she would know, and he had said *do not thank me for it*, and she was not going to.

## The Proof-Hunt

The thing that was in the Lowwater square was not new.

That was the first cold thing that arrived in her chest, and it arrived whole, the moment after she had finished being glad.

On the third day past there had been two men at the gate who had not been there before, and on the eighth day a lamp-mender had come up out of Wanzhou and a woman off a cart out of Hanhui, and neither of them would come up the steps. On the fifth day a man had walked eleven days out of Hanhui to have her written down. A copyist had asked her six times whether the third word was *furnace* or *funeral*.

All of that was people hearing about a sentence.

Du Hong’s letter was a man at a county desk buying a fact about a record.

Those are not the same work. One of them repeats what a body has already said out loud and gets it wrong, and the whole of the bound can do that for nothing, forever, because the right words have never once been written down anywhere. The other one asks a clerk, in a building, for the *name of the clerk*.

The bounty had not found a new way to be frightening. It had hired a clerk.

And it had gone to three of them, and paid for two answers, in two days, on a county desk, eleven days of road from a man in a house with four hundred volumes in it — and Du Hong, who had been at the tower three days running and had noticed being noticed, who had been a lamp-mender’s apprentice in Wanzhou before he was anything and knew exactly what a man who buys records looks like.

She put the letter down on the floor of the Hall and got the docket and the charcoal out and wrote one line in her own hand, in the scrawl, at the rate she was able to write for a courier who would read it in about forty seconds and no faster.

*Copy nothing for him.*

Then she wrote four more lines under it, which were for herself, in the wash code, with the little hooked strokes that make a letter into a mark nobody can read quickly:

*They are at the county desk and not at the tower. They are paying for a name and a record, and they have gone for the record twice and stopped short both times, so the third thing is not in an office and is not on paper.*

*Third is not in an office. Third is somebody standing in a room with me in it, and I do not yet know what a man buys to get one.*

## The Part That Is Not Enough

She went up the terrace at the fourth hour and stood at the rail, and the market had gone, and the road below was two channels of white and a line of ruts and nothing else, and it was under two miles of snow to a town where a man in a grey coat she had not seen was sitting in somebody's counting room with the answer to one third of a question.

Below her on the terrace were nine outer disciples and two washing women and a hundred yards away a man with a board coming off shift, and forty people on a mountain knew what she was and eleven of them slept twelve feet from where she slept, and the third proof was a body in a room.

Her name in the barrier book was the only thing between that man and her, and a line in a ledger in the roundhouse, and a captain's decision that had not been asked for by anybody.

“Tell me about the claim,” she said.

“You know about the claim.”

“Tell me what is in it.”

“Eight hundred jin,” said Yuan, “and you have had that for four days and you have written it down.”

“Then tell me the part you have not said.”

“No.”

“You have not said it because you have not got it, or because you have got it and it is worse.”

“Both,” said Yuan.

“That is not an answer to the question I asked.”

“No. It is an answer to the question you are actually asking, which is whether a voice in your head can tell you what a man in Lowwater is doing at this hour. It cannot. I have been sitting with it since the eighth day and it has not improved.”

She looked down the road. Two miles of snow to a town where a man in a grey coat she had not seen was sitting in somebody’s counting room with one third of a question.

“Then I will find it out in a room.”

“You cannot find that out in a room either. I have thought about it for four days and that is the one thing I am certain of. It does not come out of a ledger and it does not come out of a list.”

“It comes out of a man who has been asked in daylight and has had to answer in front of people who will remember it. And there is exactly one place on this mountain where a man will do that, and it is not the Hall and it is not the stairs.”

She had not thought about that.

“Say more about it.”

“Not from a step above it,” said Yuan. “I have been wrong in a room before and I have never once been wrong from above a crowd. Ask me when there is one.”

She stayed where she was.

“Why now and not then.”

“Because you will not be able to use it,” he said, “and a thing you cannot use is only a thing to carry.”

Below her the whole of Qinghe went on being a mountain with a road at the bottom of it that she could not walk down, and behind her in the low building at the back of the furnace a man with a brush that counted slowly was working through a winter’s books, and she was on a terrace with a coat full of paper and one leg of three on a thing she had never seen before yesterday.

In the morning she was going to ask Shen Xiu for the second leg.
