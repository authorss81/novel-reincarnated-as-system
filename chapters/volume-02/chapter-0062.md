# The Price of Paper

The snow had stopped falling and had gone to ice in the shaded half of the terrace, and by the sixth hour of the evening the whole of Qinghe was a building with a crust on it, and the wind came over the parapet and went along the flags and found every gap.

The corridor off the north end of the Hall is eleven paces long and open to the sky, because the Hall it serves has no roof any more and nobody rebuilt the corridor either. Three braziers burned in it in iron stands. Every breath she had taken since the stair came out white and hung and was gone.

She had asked for the hour at the fifth and the message had come back at the sixth, and eleven paces is a very short walk to take in order to ask three men for a seal.

Deng Chao was at the far end with the brazier nearest him, because Deng Chao had been the one who had drafted the clause in the first place, the one nobody else had seen. Pei An had the hour-board, which was a plain piece of wood with the twelve hours cut into it, and Lu Kang had nothing at all in his hands, which she had learned in the autumn was how to tell which of the three men was doing the actual business.

“Ning Yan,” said Lu Kang. “You have not been to this corridor in six days.”

“You did not send for me.”

“No,” he said. “The clerk sent for you. That is a different thing and you have been careful about it since the fifth day and I have noticed.”

## What She Asked For

“The licence application,” she said. “The three parts of it. The first part is a sponsors’ seal and you have one and I would like it on a sheet before the month turns.”

Pei An marked the hour-board with his thumb and said nothing.

Deng Chao said, “Where is the schedule.”

“Unattached. It has to be certified, and the clerk says today I had nothing to attach, and I have had four days to go and get a piece of paper certified and I spent the four days finding out what the piece of paper was.”

“That is not a bad answer,” said Lu Kang. “That is the first straight thing anyone has said to us in a corridor since you signed.”

“That is not a straight thing. That is an excuse and you have all been letting me use it.”

He looked at her for a moment, and the corridor was cold, and two braziers and a wind.

“Yes,” he said. “We have. Sit down, Ning Yan.”

There was a stool. She had not seen it before and neither had he, and she understood in about a second that it had come out of the anteroom in the four days since she had stopped coming to the anteroom, and that it had been put there for her, and that this was the cheapest and hardest thing they had done yet.

She did not sit on it.

## The Clause Came Due at Noon

“The sponsorship carries an article,” said Lu Kang, “and the article fell due at the noon hour today.”

She had known it was going to come. She had known it since the fifth day and had been carrying it about under everything else for seven days, the way you carry a plate under your shirt. She had not expected it to come out in this corridor at the sixth hour of the evening with the braziers going and three men watching her face.

“The article,” she said.

“A single fired plate, the size of a hand, taken from the sealed ground and returned to the maintaining members within the week of signature.”

“You have read the schedule of recovered articles.”

“We drafted it,” said Deng Chao. “In the second week of the autumn, before the trial, before you knew there was going to be a hearing. We drafted it because in four hundred years there has not been one and we did not know what else to write.”

“You drafted a demand for an object without knowing whether the object existed.”

“We drafted it because a schedule that does not say what became of a thing is a schedule that gets read four ways, and you said so yourself, in front of forty-one witnesses, on the fifth day, and you were right and we took the lesson.” Deng Chao had not moved once during the entire conversation and now he moved. “Where is the plate.”

Nowhere near cold, in a building with no roof, at the sixth hour of the evening, in front of three men.

“You have my sworn entry,” said Ning Yan.

“We do.”

“It is on your paper and it is in my hand and it says the article is not in the possession of the maintaining members, is not in the possession of this Hall, and has not been in the possession of the undersigned since the hour she left the sealed ground. And it says the undersigned cannot produce it and does not undertake to.”

“Yes,” said Lu Kang.

“So the article is not late,” said Ning Yan. “The article cannot be late. It was never going to be produced and I told you that on the fifth day, out loud, and asked for it to be entered, and you entered it.”

“I entered it,” said Lu Kang, “in a fair hand, with the hour, at the second hour and thirty-one minutes of the fifth day, in a building with forty-one witnesses in it.”

“Then what has come due is not an article.” She heard her own voice do the thing it did and she let it. “What has come due is the clause. And the clause is on your paper. And the denial is on your paper. And both of them are in the same file in the same building, and you have built that, and I have helped you build it, and I did it in the same hour.”

Nobody said anything for about four seconds, and one of the braziers moved in the wind.

## The Bargain She Watched Herself Do

“Sit down,” said Lu Kang again.

She sat on the stool.

The point of a stool is that a person on it is a person who has come to be dealt with. She had been on her feet for the entire corridor with her weight on her good side and her bad arm in its coat, and she had been managing it, and she had been managing it for four months, and it had run out about nine seconds ago.

“What you want is on the schedule,” she said.

“What we want,” said Pei An, and he had not spoken until now and his voice was mild, “is that the furnace schedule is worked, on the furnace, by you, in your registered hours, for four months.”

“Why.”

“Because the county counts those hours,” said Lu Kang. “At the countersignature. And a licence application with a furnace schedule attached is an application the county already reads every week for its own reasons, and there is nothing on that schedule about the person on it. It is labour. It is counted. It is the one thing in a sect that the county is obliged to have an opinion about every single week of the winter.”

“So my name does not move.”

“Your name does not move at all,” said Lu Kang. “You will be a shift. The schedule has forty names on it and you are one of them and by the second week of the thaw you will be the eleventh name and by March you will be the reason four other men are not standing on the settling work to make the count, and that will be the whole of what you have done for four months.”

“And the second half.”

“The second half is not on this sheet,” said Lu Kang, “and you signed it on the fifth day with a bad brush, and I am not going to say it back to you in a corridor in the cold.”

They had wanted the schedule on their account and her mouth shut about the voice, in that order, and both of them had been on the paper since the second week of the autumn before she had ever heard of either, and she had walked up the nine steps and taken the trial and let a roof come off a building on top of herself and had never once been asked what she wanted to do with any of it in order.

She said no to nothing, because there was nothing to say no to. That was the discovery she made on the stool, and she made it without any help at all from anybody in the corridor.

“You will seal it,” she said.

## The Seal

Pei An took a sheet out of inside his coat. It had been folded in three and it had been folded before this hour, and the fold was old, and the corner of it was soft from being in a coat for weeks, which meant it had been made a long time ago and carried in and out of rooms waiting for a good day.

Which it had been, and the day before which it had been made, was the day the schedule attached.

Lu Kang took out the seal. He did it without hurry and without ceremony, the way a man picks up a thing he has used ten thousand times, and he pressed it on the lower right of the sheet, and he held it there for a count of four because that is how long a good ink takes, and lifted it, and set the seal beside the ink block.

She watched the whole thing. She had wanted that document for four days and it was eleven paces long and it had taken four seconds to make and it was now a thing in the world that could not be un-made.

“You have your part,” he said.

“I have your promise of my part. You have not read the clause. The first part is a paper about whether anybody will stand behind me. Everybody can see whether three men have put a seal on a thing.”

“Then it will be visible,” said Lu Kang, “and I have never claimed otherwise. And you have stood in a Hall with no roof in front of forty-one witnesses and made four men write down exactly what was said to them, so you know better than any person on this mountain that a visible document is the only kind there is.”

He was right. That was the part that went in.

“Read it back,” said Ning Yan.

“Read what back.”

“Whatever that paper says when you are done with it. I want it read out in a room and I want the hour entered.”

“It is an application for the Licence to Practise Past Registration, made by Ning Yan of the Qinghe furnace crew, servant-born, maintaining member, under Clause Four of the Settlement, and it is sealed by the maintaining members of this sect this evening.”

“Enter the hour.”

“It is the sixth hour of the evening.”

“And read me the part where you say what you want.”

There was a pause of a length she counted.

“Read me the part,” she said, “or I will stand up and take this stool back to the anteroom and put my hand on the schedule and let the county find out in the spring what a sponsoring sect does when its member withdraws an application.”

Pei An looked at Deng Chao. Deng Chao looked at Lu Kang.

“The schedule is attached in the same filing,” said Lu Kang, “and the schedule is worked, and that is all of it, and there is nothing else on it, and if there is anything else on it then you may take it back and we will learn.”

She took the sheet.

It was paper. It was very good paper. There was nothing else on it.

She said thank you and meant it, and it cost her about half of something, and she noticed herself saying it and noticed herself not being sorry, and both of those went into the room and neither of them was a lie.

## What It Bought

She stood at the parapet of the corridor in the cold with the application folded inside her coat against her ribs, on the other side from the plate, and made herself count what she had.

Three men had sealed a piece of paper that said a servant-born could ask the county office at Lowwater for the right to practise past her stage. Her name was now in a county queue that nine other people would see. There was a sheet on a board in this building that said how many hours her household owed.

One leg.

And under her arm the whole time, against her ribs, the other thing.

“Sixth hour of the evening,” said Yuan, from a long way off, and for the first time in a fortnight he sounded exactly like a nineteen-year-old who is furious on somebody’s behalf. “They have sealed it and they have not asked you one single question in three days, and they have put a demand on you that only you can pay and they have got your promise of it in your own handwriting, and you said thank you.”

“Yes.”

“You said thank you to them.”

“I said thank you to a man for reading a paper correctly,” said Ning Yan, “which is a different thing, and if you spend the next three days being furious on my behalf in my own head I will go to the Hall in the morning and file under your name.”

He went away, which he did about twice a month.

She stood at the parapet for another minute, and then went down to the terrace rail and looked at the four bridges of the road coming up out of the dark, with cart-noise still on it from the market that had been open for nine hours.

There was no way down that road. There was a document in her coat with three men's seal on it, one leg of three, and a hole in her chest the size of a hand where the other thing was.
