# The Inspectors' Paper

“No,” said Ning Yan.

She said it standing in the doorway with her coat still on and frost in her hair, into a room with eleven men in it and two officers of the maintaining members in it, at about ten minutes past the tenth hour, and she said it the way she said tallies.

“I withhold consent. I gave it to you at the eighth hour and you entered it at the eighth hour and I am giving it again now in front of these eleven people so that there is one hour in which the whole of this happened in front of witnesses instead of in a corridor. Write the hour again.”

The older inspector did not move at all.

“Ning Yan.”

“Write it.”

He wrote it. Two lines, small neat hand, and he took his time about it in a way she understood, because he was giving her the shape of the moment and there was no reason on earth for him to do that.

“Entered at the second hour of the morning of the sixteenth day, refused in the doorway before eleven witnesses, by Ning Yan, maintaining member,” said the inspector, reading it back. “Is that correct.”

“That is correct.”

“You understand what that does.”

“No,” said Ning Yan, because she did not, and because being honest in front of eleven people had been the cheapest thing she had ever done and she was not going to give it up for free.

## The Courtesy

“You have never once been difficult,” said the inspector.

He said it without any weight at all, which was worse than weight. He said it the way Lu Kang said things: flat, unhurried, absolutely without malice, and about eight beats ahead of her, and she had worked out in the corridor off the north end of the Hall three days ago that there was no version of these three men where anything they did was a surprise to them and no version where it was a surprise to her.

“I have not been difficult because I have not been able to afford it,” she said. “That is not a character. That is a bill.”

“The maintaining members have never asked you for anything,” said the inspector.

“No,” said Ning Yan. “You have sealed a piece of paper for me in a corridor and you have not asked me one question and I have been in this sect four months and you are the first three men in it who have never asked me a single question about anything, and I want you to understand that I have noticed and that I have counted.”

The inspector inclined his head about two degrees.

“I will report the refusal this evening,” he said. “It will be in the minute book before the close of the day, and it will be retrievable, and it will say what you said and the hour and the eleven names. I am telling you that so that you know the shape of what you have just done and not the other shape, which is the shape most people imagine.”

“You are being kind.”

“I am being exact,” said the inspector. “There is a difference and it has saved a great many men their lives.”

## The Roster Instead

He put the sheet into the satchel and closed it, and then he stood there with his hand on the satchel for about four seconds, which she could read perfectly well, because he was a man deciding between two clauses and both of them were in the document in his hand.

“The crew roster,” he said.

The room went somewhere else.

“The board at the foot of the stair,” said the inspector. “I have taken four bunks and there is timber out of a bag in the fourth, and I will take the rest of the room, and I will enter what I take. That is the instrument and it does what the instrument says.”

“That is not what the instrument says,” said Ning Yan.

“No,” he said. “You are right. The instrument says your effects. It does not say a board that belongs to the sect and is read by everybody who comes up a stair.”

He looked at the floor.

“It also does not say a crew roster. It is silent on a crew roster. And when an instrument is silent on a thing, the thing is taken, because the instrument has no opinion and the officer on the ground has a duty to leave nothing unexamined that he had the opportunity to examine.”

He said *duty* the way Lu Kang said *courtesy*: as though it belonged to a line of work.

Nobody in the room said one word.

“You are taking the roster,” said Ning Yan.

“I am taking the roster,” said the inspector, “because it is sect property and you are not, and because you have made a very careful, very correct refusal and I have honoured it, and it has left me standing in a room with a member's name on a board and no line on my sheet for her name at all.”

She did not have an answer to that. She would go back over it for four months afterwards, on worse nights than this one, and never find one.

## What the Board Said

The shift leader was at the foot of the stair and he did not stop them, because a man who reads the paper himself has done his part and has a duty besides to read the second paper.

The younger inspector had the board down off the nail and was reading it, and he read it the way a clerk reads, which is to say aloud, because a roster read silently is a roster that cannot be contradicted.

“Eleventh to the seventeenth,” he said. “Ninth hour to the second night. Eleven names.”

“Eleven names and twelve men,” said the shift leader.

“Eleven names and twelve men,” the inspector agreed. “And I will read the whole week, because that is what it is for.”

He read the week.

He read it in a room with eleven men in it and a foreman at the foot of the stair who had come up at the tenth hour with a board of his own because the crew's hours are the sect's labour and the sect's labour is the foreman's to account for.

He got to the ninth hour of the fifteenth day and he read two names where the floor's board had one.

“Xu An and Cao Lei,” he read. “Against a shift belonging to Ning Yan.”

The foreman's own board made a sound in his hands.

“Say that again,” said the foreman.

The inspector said it again.

That was the whole of it. That was the mechanism, and it was not a conspiracy and nobody in the room had agreed to anything, and there was no speech and no villain, and it took about eleven seconds.

The foreman did not look at the two of them. He looked at the board, and then at the number of men he had standing on a floor in the middle of a frost, and she watched him do the subtraction.

“This is wrong,” he said.

“It is what the board says,” said the inspector.

“It is what the board says and it is what the schedule says as well, because the schedule was certified at the ninth hour this morning out of the same two books.”

He said it slowly.

“Two men of a crew against a shift of a member. The member's hours are the member's. The other hours have to be somewhere by the first of the month, and they are not, and I have a settling floor to open in eleven days and I am short of four.”

Nobody said anything.

“Doubled,” said the foreman. “You two. Both of you. Every shift, the ninth hour to the second night, from tomorrow to the close of the thaw, on the settling work.”

Cao Lei said, “Sir.”

Xu An said nothing at all.

## The Part She Did Not Say

She had until the twelfth hour to say something and she did not say it, and she wants that written down in the same way she wants the hour written down.

There were four things available to her at that moment and she knows all four of them and she had all four of them in her hand.

She could have said that the entry was made by a crew member's lawful mark, which was true, and she could have put her hand on her own schedule certification, which was also true, and the schedule and the board are the same book read twice, and a foreman who will not reconcile two documents that agree cannot reconcile anything at all.

She could have used the sentence she had been building since the eighth hour, which was that a maintaining member's hours are the maintaining member's hours and the crew's cover of them is a matter between the crew and her and is not entered anywhere and therefore cannot be found in a document, and that a document is the only thing in nine counties that has ever moved her, and she had built her entire winter on that and could have used it now.

She could have said nothing and let it stand, which is what she did.

Or she could have stood in a doorway and said *take me instead*.

She has been asked since whether there was a fifth thing. There was not. There were four and she had all four and she used none of them, and what she used instead was the plate.

Because the reason she could not say *take me instead* was a fired plate the size of a hand against her ribs with the assessment of a child of thirteen written into it, four county originals folded into the back of a stolen laundry ledger inside her coat, and a false oath in her own hand with the hour on it in a minute book in a building with no roof.

She chose four lives that were not hers over one life that was. That is the arithmetic. It is not complicated and it does not improve on being read.

## The Plate

They were packing the satchel at the eleventh hour when the younger one said it, without looking up, in the voice of a man who is being helpful.

“We did not come for anything she is wearing,” he said. “For what it is worth, I would not have touched it if we had.”

Ning Yan stood in the doorway.

“The maintaining members already hold the description of the article in their own hand,” he said. “Size, material, ground of removal, and the hour it came out of it. They have held it since the second week of the autumn. There is nothing we could have found this morning that they do not already have in writing.”

He buckled the satchel.

“So the instrument is about the debris. It was never about her.”

He said it kindly. He said it the way a clerk explains a queue to somebody who has never been in one.

Then the two of them went down the four steps and across the yard and out, in frost, with eleven men watching them go and a board gone off the wall, and one of the two of them had, twenty minutes earlier, in a room with no roof, in a fair hand, with the hour entered, written down a denial of the very thing he was telling her they already had described.

That was what the clause was for.

She stood in the doorway of the crew quarters and understood the corridor of three days ago in about four seconds, standing up, on the sixteenth day of her winter.

The three men had not wanted the plate. They had wanted her to have sworn she did not have it, and they had got her to swear it, and they had drafted the schedule of recovered articles themselves in the second week of the autumn — before the trial, before any of this — and then they had let a girl of seventeen walk into a room with forty-one witnesses in it and deny an article on their own paper in her own handwriting.

They had built the trap out of her oath.

She had built half of it, and she had known she was doing it at the time, on the fifth day, standing up, in daylight, and she had done it because a word in four county books was worth more that day than the object.

## What Was on Her Coat

She went down the four steps and across the yard at the eleventh hour with the frost going off her, and there was nothing on her.

That was the sentence. There was nothing at all on her. There was a plate against her ribs and a log in a coat lining and four county originals in the back of the log and a word of hers in her hand saying she could not produce the article, and she had spent the whole morning in a doorway saying *no* in front of eleven witnesses, and it had held, and it had cost two men a double shift on a settling floor for the length of a thaw.

Cao Lei came past her on the stair.

She did not say sorry. She had thought about it on the fourth step of the nine on the way down, in about the time it took to get to the fourth step, and she had worked out that an apology said in that stairwell would be a thing that two men would have to carry for the rest of the winter, and that what they would do with it is decide whether she was going to keep asking them to pay.

So she said the true thing instead.

“I knew what the taking would do and I said no.”

“Yes,” said Cao Lei.

“I said no anyway.”

“Yes,” he said, and went down.

Then he stopped on the third step from the bottom and said, without turning round, “Do not thank us for standing up there.”

“I was not going to.”

“Good,” said Xu An.
