# The Counting Room's Employee

She wrote the reading down the way she writes everything that matters, in wash code on the back of the docket that holds Clause Four.

The quarters lamp was the only light. Nine men breathed round her and the roof dripped where the snow came off the ridge in the morning and froze in the drip's own place overnight.

She sat on the edge of the second bunk with the brush in her left hand and made the hooked strokes that turn a letter into a mark no clerk reads quickly: a broker keeps a counting room, the man in the good coat works for him, the trade is records, the place unbought, the carriers' names unanswered.

Then she wrote a sixth mark and looked at it for a while before she wrote it again. Body not asked for. Body waited on.

It was the only part of the reading that had not been an account of things the man had done. Everything else was a man spending money at desks, and he would carry out an order with his own two hands and go home. That part was the absence of an order, and an absence is the expensive kind to buy, because somebody has to supply it.

She did not write the cost. The channel stood thin at about six and a half weeks and her foundation held the hole where the spike had gone, and both facts sat in the room without needing ink. Yuan had held Ledger less than a day and put it down the same day and refused it permanently, and the refusal was the shape of the evening.

“You sleep little,” said Yuan. “You eat little and you write late.”

“I write what you bought,” said Ning Yan. “I eat what the quarters have. You rest and you watch.”

“I rest,” said Yuan. “I watch. I touch nothing.”

In the morning she folded the docket back into the lining and burned nothing, because burning is a statement, and went down to the floor with the shift leader's finger already pointed at the bench.

“Bench,” he said.

“Channel,” said Ning Yan. “Four hours. If the wall leans, bench me yourself.”

“You breathe short.”

“I breathe two counts,” said Ning Yan. “Two counts is enough to hold a wall, and I am not the reason it stands short.”

## The Week

The household passed four hundred and twelve worked by the week's close. Five hundred and twenty-eight standing. Fifty-eight in her own hand, continuous from the day the book opened, every block signed and no gap the county could point to.

The clerk wrote her blocks slow, four and a half and four and a half, and read the week back, because the reading is part of the slow. The foreman signed six on the board and posted six in the hole, name on the line, timber sound.

Her arm came back eleven minutes in the middle of a block and served and went. The leak ran cold at the rim and the stripe burned through her coat twice a day and did not get worse, because it had nowhere left to go that she had not already spent.

She sent a letter down the road by Old Gao's boy for the carts, and signed it, and the boy put his mark under hers, because a cart letter is carried by whoever is going that day and towers go free.

*Copy nothing for him,* she wrote. Four words that Feng already lives by, with the column at five and none upward.

## What Du Hong Wrote

His answer came up with the water two days later in his own hand, which is a hand that writes too fast and reads itself better than other people do.

He wrote that a clerk on the square had asked him a question he had not been asked before, which was what the end of a licence like hers costs in a county office, and he had asked the clerk to read the answer twice and write it out so that a carrier who cannot hold a brush in his head could hold it in his coat.

A living name for attendance at every county hour. The name in the county book. If the name dies before the hours are done, the paper voids and the debt comes to the woman holding it.

He wrote that he had not given the name. He wrote that nobody had asked him for it. He wrote that the same clerk, afterward, asked what he carried down the road in his coat, and wrote down that he was asked, and has a file to keep it in.

And at the foot of the page, in a hand that had got worse rather than better over the winter, one line under three more that said the same thing the long way round: a man who has written his own name onto a piece of paper on somebody else's account ought to be the one who writes it down first, and first is before you are in the room.

She read it twice at the table while the clerk waited for her block, and the clerk did not look at the paper, because clerks read what a person is holding and not what it says, and a clerk who had read it would remember it.

He was eighteen and he could not lie, and he had not tried, and he had put the price of it in her hands so that she could not hold it against him later: *you will not be able to say afterwards that nobody told you what it costs.*

That was not nothing. It was also not the same thing as stopping him, because telling a man the cost of a thing is not the same as taking the thing away from him, and she knew the difference, and she knew which one she was holding.

The first thing that had cost her anything in a month was a clerk's question, asked at a desk for nothing, about what a boy carries down a road in his coat.

“Will he say it,” said Yuan.

“He has not been asked,” said Ning Yan. “That is the whole of what is in the letter. He has not been asked and he has written down what it costs to say it, which is a thing a man does when he is thinking about saying it.”

“You want him refused.”

“I want to be standing in that office when the name is spoken.”

“You are four miles up a frozen pass,” said Yuan.

“That is the whole of the trouble,” said Ning Yan. “A man who has not been asked cannot be stopped by a letter from a furnace floor, because the letter is not the same as the office. He will choose the hour. He will walk in on a morning with the sun on the square and give his name in front of a queue, and I will be here holding a wall, and I cannot get down there in time to say no.”

“You could go tomorrow.”

“I could,” said Ning Yan. “The cart goes at the tenth hour and Old Gao would put my name on the sheet before the barrels are roped.”

She sat with it longer than the breath wanted to sit with anything.

“No,” she said. “The week is not signed. A week does not get carried in halves, and the county does not weigh a person by the days she spent on the road. I go at the week's close, on a cleared day, entered, in daylight, with the Watch on the rail, and I stand in that office before anybody stands in it without me.”

She wrote him one line back, and the line was *do not give them your name*, and she read it twice before she sealed it, because a letter that says do not is a letter that asks, and he was the sort of man who would hear the asking as an order and answer it with the whole of himself.

## The Trestle

At the end of the week the frost took the shaded turns again and the carts came up loaded and went down loaded differently, and she stood at the trestle at the ninth hour in daylight with her coat buttoned and the docket in the lining and the sheet folded on the rail.

Old Gao had the barrels loaded and his own mark ready beside hers, because he had held her off the cart four times and had understood, in his slow way, that the fifth time was a decision and not a habit.

Four hundred and twelve. Five hundred and twenty-eight. Fifty-eight in her own hand, signed four and a half at a time, and behind all of it a name written in a hand that writes too fast, carrying paper she had put in his coat.

Old Gao wrote her on the sheet before the barrels were roped and said not one word about the four times he had not, and the Watchman at the bridge put his hand flat on the board and looked up the cut at her the way the Watch looks at everything it is not going to write down.

She rode the boards at the tenth hour with her feet braced against the barrels and the whole mountain falling away below her, going down to find out what a county office wants before it asks her.
