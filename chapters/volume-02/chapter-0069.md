# The Quota Table

The quota office is a long room at the back of the furnace building with one window and four tables in it, and the anteroom outside it has four benches and a smell of wet wool that does not leave in nine years.

She was on the second bench at the sixth hour of the morning of the nineteenth day and she was fourth in the order.

The first was an old woman with a basket that had been repaired in a way she was going to complain about and was going to be right about. The second was a man in outer rank who wanted a stage cleared for his wife's household before the thaw so that a girl of sixteen could take an assessment.

The third was the clerk who wrote the tithe book, holding his own sheet, which was not a joke, because clerks send their families through the same queue everybody else does.

Nobody had asked her anything in three hours.

That was the first fact about the room. Not that she was made to wait — she had been made to wait in better rooms by better men with better reasons. The fact was that nobody had come out and told her she was waiting, and nobody had come out and told her she was not, and the bench did not know her name and the man at the inner table did not know she was on it.

She had a piece of paper in her coat with three men's seal on it and a certificate of a schedule and the whole of the next four months in a clerk's ledger, and she was fourth in a queue of twelve names for the winter, and the man who could move her in that queue was at the table at the far end with a brush.

## The Man With the Brush

Shen Xiu had a brush and he had a list, and she had watched him use both of those things to decide who died in the autumn, and then had watched him carry half of a boy two miles without being asked, and had been shut behind a barred door on the third morning of the trial on his own authority and inside his own orders, and had put a report in three days early.

He did not look up when she came in. He had looked up in the autumn at a rail by lamplight, and in the autumn on the terrace steps, and he had looked at the middle of her head four times at the trial and once in the street, and he did not look up now.

He was working. That was not an insult and she had known for about ninety seconds that it was not, and she had known it before she had got through the door, and she had come anyway.

The room filled up the way a room fills: a boy at the fourth table with a bad cold, a woman from the washing crew with a folded paper, a man of about sixty who put his hands flat on the table to stand up. They did not look at her. That was a thing she had not expected, and sitting in a room where nothing was being done to her turned out to be the single most useful half hour she had had that winter.

She had spent four months being looked at by rooms. Eleven hundred disciples, forty in a yard, nine hundred and eleven at a hearing, and every single time the looking had been the work: a thing done to her, or a thing being asked of her, or a thing she was standing in the middle of.

Nobody looked at the servant-born girl on the second bench, because the quota office is a room where you look at your own household and everybody's household is in the same condition and there is nothing to see.

She sat with her left hand on her knee and watched a brush move for two and a half hours.

He did it in a sequence. He took a sheet from the left, read it, said a number out loud to the clerk at the next table, and the clerk entered it. Then he took the same sheet back and put it in one of three piles.

Every sheet went into exactly one pile. The piles did not mean good or bad, or she could not tell; they meant *asked*, *worked*, and *owed*. He moved about forty sheets an hour, did not appear to hurry, and never appeared to make an error.

The whole of that building's function was to move paper from the left to one of three places.

## The Frame

There were twelve sheets on the wall behind him in a frame.

She had heard about them from a clerk with a broken finger at a table four feet from a furnace mouth, on the fifteenth day, and she had not written them down because she had not seen them.

The sixth one was the one about hours. She read it again from four feet away in the bad light through the window and it said exactly what the clerk had said and not one word more.

The other eleven she could not read. Not because of the light. Two of them were in a hand that had been taught letters in a roundhouse and nine were in four different hands and none of them had a signature on them, and one of them was about something she did not have a name for, and one appeared to be a list of names.

Four of the twelve were about households she had never heard of, and the dates on two of those were eleven years apart.

She looked at it for a long time and then she took the docket out of her left sleeve, the one with Clause Four written on it in her own scrawl, and she put the other half of what the clerk had told her underneath it, in the wash code, in the hooked strokes that make a letter into a mark nobody can read quickly.

She wrote it standing up, at the back of a bench, in grey close weather with the melt running off the eaves and no sun anywhere in it.

*Twelve sheets in a frame at the back of the quota office. Unsigned all twelve. One of them is the hours sheet and eleven are not. Two are in the roundhouse hand of the captain at the fourth bridge and nine are in other hands. Two have eleven-year spacing on households I do not know. I have now seen this.*

## The Figure

She was called at about the ninth hour of the morning.

“Clearance,” said Shen Xiu.

He said the word and did not look up, and then he read a line off the sheet in front of him, which she understood afterwards he had read the night before.

“Ning household, of the ninth estate, of this sect. Furnace column struck whole on the fifth day at the second hour and thirty-one minutes. Account closed. No balance.”

“Yes.”

“Quota office certification is separate from the column and does not read the column. I am telling you that so that you do not stand there waiting for me to congratulate you.” He turned the sheet round a quarter. “Against the year. Hours asked, hours worked, standing.”

He put his brush down, which he had not done for any of the forty other people in that room.

“Hours asked for this year for a household of your size is nine hundred and forty. Hours worked, entered and counted to the eighteenth, is two hundred and eleven.”

She did the arithmetic.

Seven hundred and twenty-nine.

“No,” said Shen Xiu.

She had not asked.

“You did not ask,” he said. “You had it before you opened your mouth. Everyone does. Sit down, you are standing on my light.”

She sat on the corner of the bench behind him, which was not a seat for the public and which he permitted.

“That is a furnace crew's year,” she said. “Nine hundred and forty is a crew's year to the last ten hours. I have seen the number on a claim that stands in the layer against my name and it is not this number, and it is close enough that somebody chose it.”

“Yes,” said Shen Xiu.

He did not say anything else about it. He picked the brush up again.

“Quota clearance certifies that a household owes nothing to the Bureau for the year and that the winter book closes square. It is two things and both of them are numbers. The first is your line: your household’s worked hours level with your household’s asked hours at the close of the year. The second is the book, and the book closes square when every estate standing on it is level or accounted for, and that half is mine and has nothing whatever to do with your arm.”

“And the winter book is not square.”

“It is out by six hundred and eleven hours,” said Shen Xiu. “Not on the ninth estate. On five estates that are not yours and that I am not going to discuss with you on a bench. Those five are the reason I have not had a free hand on the closing since the sixth day, and they will be level or accounted for before the year turns, because that is the entire of what I am for.”

He squared the sheet.

“I will tell you what you can put a date on and then I will tell you what it is not, and then I will not say anything else about it, because I have a book to close.”

“Go on.”

“Clearance is arithmetic,” said Shen Xiu. “It is not a favour and it is not a decision. It is not mine. I could want you certified and it would not make it so. A sponsor could come up this mountain with a sheet and a seal and it would not make it so.”

He turned the sheet a quarter.

“The only thing that makes it so is nine hundred and forty hours. They have to be worked and they have to be in my book, and my book gets a sheet from the floor every third evening, and the clerk who writes it writes it slowly on purpose.”

“I know he writes it slowly.”

“Then you know more than most people who have sat on that bench.”

## Spring

“How long,” said Ning Yan.

“I do not know.”

“That is not an answer.”

“It is the only answer I have,” said Shen Xiu, “and I have given it to about eleven people this winter and every one of them asked me the same thing in the same words, and not one of them was asking about you.”

He turned the sheet a half.

“I will tell you what I can put a date on. The hours have to be worked. That is the furnace and the settling work and whatever else is on a crew’s schedule, and this is the sum of it: seven hundred and twenty-nine hours.”

“Twenty-three days of thaw will not do it. There is the spring behind the thaw and that is where the certificate is, and a stage one with a dead arm on a furnace floor is not the man who gets through those hours fastest. And I have not adjusted the asked figure for your arm and neither can you and I will not.”

“Then adjust it.”

“No.”

“Then somebody will,” said Ning Yan, and heard how it sounded, and did not take it back.

He looked up.

It was the first time in three hours and it lasted about a second and a half, and it was the same look he had given her on the boundary bank in the autumn with the fourth line open on his board.

“Your household,” he said, “is a household. I do not do arithmetic on the shape of the people in it. A furnace crew with six men and a furnace crew with six men and one of them is a stage one with a dead arm are the same line in that book, because the day I start weighting it by what a person can do, the book is a different book, and the book is the only thing in this sect that anybody can check.”

She sat on the corner of a desk a mile from where she was entitled to be standing and did not have an answer and did not want one, because he was right and being right at her was the single most expensive thing he had ever done to her.

“Then spring,” said Ning Yan.

“Spring,” said Shen Xiu. “If the hours are worked between now and the close of the thaw and the rest of them worked after it, and my five estates are level or accounted for before the year turns, it is the middle of spring for the certificate and the first week of the summer for the county to receive it, and the county is at Lowwater and eleven days of road, and I have never in nineteen years known a county to move faster than the season.”

He put the brush in the rack.

“I will not hurry it,” he said. “I have said to four Elders of this sect this winter that I would not hurry it, and two of them have asked me what happens if I do not, and I have told them. I have not told you and I will not, and I want it understood that I have told them.”

“What happens if you do not.”

“The hours do not count,” said Shen Xiu. “That is all. That is the entire of what happens and there is no other consequence in this building and no other consequence anywhere that I am able to arrange.”

## What She Did Not Do

She did not plead.

That has to be said because every single version of this conversation she had constructed on the walk down the mountain — and she had constructed five — had a part in it where she said something to Shen Xiu that made him see her.

She stood in the room with a certificate of a schedule in one hand and the whole of the winter in the other.

She did not say one single thing that made him see her. There was nothing she could have said. He had told her what the room was for in about forty seconds and she had believed him, and she had already spent the one thing she had in that direction in the autumn at a rail by lamplight, and a man had refused it and been right to.

“Thank you for the figure,” she said.

“It was in the book.”

“Thank you for it anyway.”

He did not look up.

She went out through the anteroom and past four benches and a basket that had been repaired in a way the old woman was going to be right about, and out into a day that was grey and close and melting without any sun in it at all.

## The Walk Back

It took her eleven minutes to get back to the furnace floor and she spent the whole of it working out what had just happened, and the working-out took about nine seconds, and then she had nine minutes of walking with nothing to do.

He had not refused her. That was the thing. In the autumn she had asked Qiu for a seal and had been offered a thing with a room in it, and in the corridor three days ago she had asked three men for a seal and got four seconds and a stool, and she had been ready for both of those, and had come up the mountain ready for both of those.

She had come up the mountain ready for a man who said no in a corridor, and what she had got was a man with a figure.

Seven hundred and twenty-nine hours. Middle of spring. Nobody's favour. A number that moved when she moved it and when nothing else did.

She had spent four months learning that being counted was the thing worth having, and she had it now, and it was not a person at all. It was a man at a table with a brush who would read her line off the book and would not read her face, and it would be there in the spring if she worked and it would not be there if she did not, and it would make no difference to him at all whether she knew what it cost her.

And he had told her the whole of it and had not been cruel, and had looked up once in three hours, and had said *I have told two Elders what happens if I do not hurry it* and had refused to tell her what it was, and that was the part she would carry into the winter.

She went down onto the furnace floor at the ninth hour of the morning and picked up a plate hook and worked a channel for four hours with her left hand on the rail.

Above her, eight feet up, at the slag heap, Cao Lei put his head round and said, “There is a woman at the gate asking about you.”

“I know.”

“She asked about where you sleep.”

“I know that as well.”

Cao Lei looked down at her for a second longer than the joke needed.

“You are going to have to do something about that,” he said.

“Yes,” said Ning Yan. “About seven hundred hours of it.”
