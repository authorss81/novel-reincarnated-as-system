# The Barrier Escort

She went down to stay on the fifty-eighth day, with the tithe sheet signed through the week and the winter book at four hundred and sixty-eight worked.

The barrier at the fourth bridge stood at the bottom of the cut with the sun not yet over the far side and frost white on the trestle. The board carried the captain's line in its roundhouse hand, no order on it, no clerk's name against it. The Watchman at the rail knew her by sight the way the whole mountain knew her, and nodded her to the water cart without ceremony.

“On the sheet,” he said. “Tenth hour. Daylight. The Watch watches the cart to the town.”

“On the sheet,” said Ning Yan. “I ride the boards.”

Old Gao had the barrels loaded and the sheet open on the rail. He entered her as a carrier's hand with his own mark beside it, in daylight, before the Watchman. He said nothing about the cleared days she had stood at his trestle and not boarded, and she said nothing either, and the ink went on the sheet the way ink goes on every sheet.

The grey-templed captain's man stood up the slope with his hat on, not looking, the way he never looks. She felt his not-looking and set it aside, because a sheet is the only part of this that is on paper, and paper is a thing he can pay for and a thing he cannot get her off.

“You ride down and you stay down,” said Old Gao quietly. “The town has ears and the tower has a mat. Come up for crew days entered, go down entered. Nothing unrecorded.”

“Nothing unrecorded,” said Ning Yan. “I learned that in a burial register.”

The cart went through the cut with the Watchman walking the rail above it to the town boundary. Four miles of white rutted road, ice at the shaded turns, mud where the sun hit. She rode the boards with her feet braced and her coat shut, breathing two counts against the cold, and watched the mountain fall away from her in pieces, the ash flats, the furnace roof, the Nine Steps with nobody on them.

“You fear it still,” said Yuan. “You want it and you fear it.”

“I fear it,” said Ning Yan. “I ride it. That is the difference between fear and stopping.”

“You ride it well,” said Yuan. “I see you and I stay with you.”

## Lowwater Ground

The town came up with its smoke and its noise, and the noise was the first thing that was different, because a sect makes noise like a machine and a town makes noise like people.

The square ran full with market carts, carriers shouting, a knife grinder, children underfoot, three desks under an east awning with clerks at them and coin on the wood. The county office stood on the north side with its shutters open, a queue forming before the ninth hour and the stamp going down steady as a hammer. The Lantern Register tower stood above the roofs with its lamp cage dark at midday, copying floor inside where Feng writes.

She got off the boards in mud for the first time to stay, entered on the cart sheet, watched but not guarded. The Watchman turned the cart without looking at her again. Water had gone down. Water went back up. That was all it was, and it was the whole of what she had bought with a sheet and a witness.

Her feet went in up to the ankle on the square and she stood in it longer than she needed to, the way a person stands in water they have been told is warm.

Du Hong met her at the tower door with a spare mat under his arm and flour on his sleeves.

“Loft,” he said. “Feng says the stair costs breath. Climb once.”

“I climb once,” said Ning Yan.

He looked at her while he said it, and his face did the thing it does when he has worked something out. Then he would not carry the mat up without her, because he had seen what she could carry up a stair and had decided about it himself, and he took the sealed paper in his other arm instead and went up ahead of her.

Below, the copying floor ran quiet: brushes, paper, Feng's cough. Four of her county copies sat walled behind the fifth item in that room, entered none upward, column at five. The county office on the square kept a different book in a different hand, where a registrar named Sun He stamps. She held the two apart in her head the way she holds two lines apart, because a number merged into a number it does not belong to is a number that stops meaning anything at all.

She climbed once, with stops, and lay on the mat till the chest settled. The loft held a pallet, a shutter that did not shut, and a view of the square through the gap, which was the price of the mat: from that gap she could see the queue and the desks and the tower door, and every hand that came up the stair.

“How does the household stand,” Du Hong asked over soup at the eighth hour.

“Four hundred and sixty-eight worked,” said Ning Yan. “Four hundred and seventy-two standing. Seventy-two in my own hand.”

He whistled low. “Spring, then. As he said.”

“Spring,” said Ning Yan. “If the hours hold and the book squares. The book squares on estates not mine.”

## What She Came Down For

She washed the road off at the trough behind the tower and went down and stood in the square at the ninth hour of the evening, on the edge of the lamp light, where a person can stand a long time and be counted as a person waiting for something ordinary.

The county office was shut and shuttered and the queue was a scuff of mud in front of it. The three desks under the east awning were bare except for one clerk putting his covers on. The man in the good coat was not at them, and would be at them again at the eleventh hour tomorrow, and every day after that with his tube on his belt and a coin out before the clerk finished asking.

She had come four miles down a frozen pass to find out what a stamp was worth, and she stood in the dark and worked it out the way she worked everything out, standing up, without moving her face.

The stamp was on the far side of a shuttered room with a queue in front of it. There were three legs to carry into that room and she owned one of them, and the other two were hours on a mountain and a name in a boy's mouth, and neither of them could be made in a square in an evening.

There were four hundred and seventy-two hours standing in her household and six hundred and eleven more standing on five estates that were not hers, and the number the county would read was the one that said none of it mattered.

And there was a claim over her that no county would countersign while it stood, which she had learned from a man at a desk and had not yet learned any other way.

So she stood in the square in the cold and the answer was: the county wants a name.

Not her name. Her name was on every carrier's sheet and every tithe page and the refusal in the Hall's minute book, and a name like hers is not what a clerk asks a person to write down. A county that wants a name wants one that dies before she does.

Du Hong was in the tower under her, eating her soup, with the file open on the table in a hand that writes too fast.

She took the stair up once and lay on the mat in the cold air because close air makes the chest worse, with the docket in her lining and the shutter not shut.

She had come to say no to a thing that had not happened yet. She had been four miles away when it did.
