# Nine Men

They knew at the fourth bridge. Old Gao had the cart sheets and Old Gao's cart had been going up and down since the market opened and Old Gao told her at the trestle, with his hand on the tailboard, in the flat voice of a man who carries water and other people's business both.

“They had it from Lowwater before the snow went off the Verge,” he said. “The whole lane had it by the week. The tower foot, the road, the ditch. Not your name on the second half of it yet. Not yet.”

She got on. The cart went up four miles at the pace of a cart with water on it and she sat on the tailboard with her hands on the frame and her arm in her coat and watched the pass come down behind her in the first grey that was not rain.

Nine men on the crew and the quarters sleeping ten, and the knowing had come up the road ahead of her, which was a thing she had expected and had not expected, and she found on the cart that she had expected it.

The barrier board stood where it had stood all winter. `Ning Yan. Held.` In the captain's own hand, with no order on it and no clerk's name against it, and the frost had got into it and taken a line of it and he had gone over the line in the spring, because a man who writes a thing like that on a board is a man who goes over it, and there was a second wet streak in the letters and no second name.

Old Gao did not say anything about it. He put the cart through and took his water up to the roundhouse and did not look back at her.

The road above the bridge runs four miles through timber to the terrace, and the timber was letting the light through in a way that the last time she had walked it in December it had not, and the road was running with water at the edges where a month ago it had been frozen nine feet deep.

## The Floor

The foreman put her on the settling floor and then put her on the cold end of it, and did it in front of the shift and without a reason, and the nine men watched her go down.

Nine men do not say nothing. Two of them, going down the stair behind her, said a thing at the bottom of it in the low voice that men use when they are being honest at each other rather than at you, and the words were *she did it* and *she buried him herself*, and neither of them was wrong, and both of them said it to the other one and not to her, which is the most a furnace crew can offer.

“Say it to me,” said Ning Yan.

Nine men looked at the wall.

“Say it to me,” said Ning Yan, “and I will write it down, and I will put it in the book the way I put the other two in the book, and you can all read it.”

The foreman took the pipe out of his mouth and looked at her.

“You have got a sheet,” he said. “You have got the county's mark on it and you are capped at five and you cannot own a furnace in your life, and you have come down here to tell us what to say about it. Write your hours. Sign your own hours. Nobody is going to sign for you.”

“Nobody is going to sign for you,” one of the men repeated, not to anybody.

It went on like that for six days.

She worked the cold end for six days, at the ninth hour and after, and she carried for nine hours a day into a book that the clerk entered slow with a broken finger set an inch too long, and nobody put a mark against her shift, because there was nobody left in that crew who wanted his name on a line of hers, and the rule that let any man of the crew do it had never once been a rule about liking anybody.

The crew was nine. The quarters slept ten.

The cold end was over the place where a beam had come down in a winter, and it had been shored in the thaw with five men posted against a strength of six and the foreman's name on the line, and it had not been looked at again since, and she did not look up at it either. Twice a day she tested the right arm. It was open a third of the time.

## The Quota Office

Shen Xiu had his back window open. It was the second hour and he had four tables and the winter book out on one of them and an anteroom of four benches in front of him, and she stood at the front bench the way a woman stands at a bench.

He read the sheet she put down and then he read the schedule beside it, and then he read the sheet again, and he did not look at her while he did it.

“Three hundred and eleven hours entered in the season,” he said. “The last month is short by the days you were not on the floor.”

Eleven days. Nine of them she had not eaten and two of them had been the burial and a killing and a road.

“I will make them up in the last week.”

“You will,” said Shen Xiu. “The book closes on the last day of the thaw and the schedule goes up the quarter and the county reads it, and what the county reads is what you worked and not what you intended to work. You are three hundred and eleven short of the number you signed for and eleven short of what a woman who does not miss hours signs for, and I am going to write three hundred and eleven because that is what there is.”

That was the whole of it. He did not ask about the road. He did not ask about the ditch. He did not say a single word about a man lying in thaw water under two boards at the foot of a tower in a town four miles down the road, and his back window was open, and it was the fourteenth of the thaw and the sky over the terrace was the colour of wet ash.

“Read it back,” said Ning Yan, “and let me sign it.”

He read it back. She signed it. She noticed when his eye went to the signature and stayed a half-count longer than the numbers warranted, because the numbers were numbers and a signature is a person, and he has read this woman's signature on three hundred and eleven lines since the ninth day of the winter and knows it the way a man knows a gate he has opened every morning of his life.

He did not say what he thought of it.

When she had gone to the door he spoke to the back of her.

“The schedule goes up the quarter,” he said. “The hours have to be on it before the book closes, not after. If they are on it after, they are not in the book, and a schedule that is not in the book is a schedule with a gap in it, and a gap is not a breach but it is a thing somebody will want to look at twice.”

“I have eleven days to make them up.”

“You have the last week of the thaw to make them up,” said Shen Xiu, “because the last week is when the book closes, and I am telling you the number because I am a clerk and you are the line, and I have never once in my life weighted a book for anybody's arm, including your own, including now.”

She carried eleven days of labour up to the last week of the thaw and paid it in, and the book closed on the last day with her hours whole and the schedule whole and the frost out of the ground, and the return went up the quarter with the Hall's mark beside her hours and no gap anywhere in it.

That was what she had gone up for, and she had gone up for it through four days of a road and a cart and nine men who would not sign her shift, and she had it in her hands, and there was nothing else on the mountain she had come back for.

Eleven days to the ceiling. The stage was in her at the fourth and had been since the day after the burial, and the fourth was not on any paper in nine counties, and the fifth was where her own hand in the defect line said the practice stopped.

She went up the stair after the ninth hour with the schedule certified in her lining and put her hand on the cold rail of the ninth step, where she had stood with frost on the treads in the winter and had been furious at a man for four seconds.

The Hall had no roof. It had not had a roof since a strike in the autumn, and it was raining into it, and the water was standing in the low corner by the west end exactly where it had stood since the twelfth day, and the Hall clerk was under a wet awning at the west table with three sheets in front of him and a coat he clearly did not own.
