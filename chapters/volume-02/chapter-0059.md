# The Closed Pass

She had a plan at the second hour of the tenth day and the plan was good, and the whole of it depended on being on the Lowwater side of the fourth bridge before the light went.

Du Hong was already down there with four sheets of county paper in a coat that was not his. Feng Lushi was in Lowwater with a lamp and a cough and four hundred volumes and no idea what was about to walk into his tower. And somewhere between those two places was a stretch of iced road with the name of a claim standing on it, and the only way past that was to be on the far side of it before anybody thought to look.

The ash was coming down thin over the top of the grey snow that had packed into the stone on the ninth night, and the whole of the Nine Steps Terrace was the colour of a wet flue. It was the tenth day of eleven, and one day of it was left after today.

The road opens for market on the third day after the fall. Every day from here to then was a day she was spending on a mountain with eight hundred jin standing against her name in four county books.

She went down. Nine steps on a left hand that had held a rail since the sixth day of the winter, and her right arm out of its own weight inside her coat because the cold made it worse and the cold was most of what there was now.

The yard was doing the ordinary morning. Coal at the furnace mouth. The washing crew out at the trough before the light because they were always out at the trough before the light. Forty feet of dressed oak lying in three pieces along the street where the Hall's roof used to be, squared and counted and stacked, because timber is timber.

Nobody stopped her. That was the thing she noticed and did not enjoy noticing: eleven hundred people in this sect went on with the morning around her as though she were weather, and every one of them turned a fraction to watch her do it.

## The Barrier

The fourth bridge is a mile and a half below the terrace and the ice on it came up out of the cut in the night, and the Watch had a rope and two lamps and a trestle across the low end of it, because the Watch does not close a road for its own sake. The Watch closes a road so that somebody else has written down why.

Captain Lu Tao was sitting on a crate at the sheltered end with a board on his knees.

He was forty-three or forty-four and had been doing it nineteen years and he had the specific stillness of a man who spends a long day not moving a muscle, and he looked up when she came down onto the bridge head and he looked at her for about two seconds and then at the board.

“Morning,” he said.

“Morning.”

“The pass iced at about the second hour after midnight,” he said. “I walked it at the fourth. It is ice from the parapet to the water and it will be ice for a week and it will be ice longer than a week.”

“I can see that.”

“You can,” he agreed. “I am telling you because you came down here to be told.”

She had come down here to be *not* told, and she had come down here anyway, and it took about a second and a half for her to understand what that said about her.

“When does it open,” she said.

“Market days. The third day after the fall, which is the day after tomorrow. Then the days the road is cleared below the cut, which at this rate is the sixth day and then every fourth day after. Cart traffic before the ninth hour. Foot traffic after.”

“I am not cart traffic.”

“No,” said Captain Lu Tao.

The lamps threw two long shapes on the ice and the trestle made a small noise in the wind, and she stood at the barrier with her hands in her coat and thought about the four things she had come down here to do and how three of them were on the other side of sixty feet of bad ice, and how one of them was Du Hong, who was also on the other side of it and had been since yesterday.

“Captain.”

“I have a line.”

He said it before she asked, which meant he had decided to say it, which meant the deciding had been done a long time ago and in private.

## The Line

He turned the board round.

It was the Watch's own board. Paper, a nail through the top corner, and eleven days of entries in a hard slanting hand that got worse as it went down the page, and about two thirds of the way down there was a line that had been written later than the rest because the ink was a different black.

*Ning Yan. Held.*

Nothing else. No hour, no order, no clerk, no seal. Two words on a board in a dead man's week, in a hand that had been taught letters in a roundhouse and had never stopped being surprised by them.

“Whose word is that,” she said.

“Mine,” said Lu Tao.

“Why.”

He took about four seconds, and she watched him decide whether to say it properly or say it short, and she had read shoulders for two years and she knew the shape of a man choosing short.

“Because the eighth day past there was a claim entered in the layer against the Chosen One, and it was priced at a furnace-crew year, and the bound will pay it to whoever brings a body, a place and a name,” he said. “And there is one person inside the wall of this sect who is that name, and she cannot walk out of the gate under nineteen men with rope and lanterns without a man somewhere deciding she is worth it.”

“That is not a reason to hold me. That is a reason to worry.”

“It is a reason to hold you,” he said. “Worry is what I have got. You have not noticed.”

She had noticed. She had been up four hours noticing it, on a mountain, in a corridor, on a step, and she had told a voice in her head about it four times in six days, and a man with a rope and a board had got to the same place without any of that and had got there on his own legs.

“Then I am held,” she said.

“You are held.”

“By you. On your own hand. With no order on it.”

“Yes,” said Captain Lu Tao.

## Turning Back

She stood there for the length of a count to forty.

Forty was enough. She had learned in four months that the things which could be done about a thing could be counted, and counted out, and then you had to use the rest of the count for what you were going to do about it, and there was a list, and the list had one item on it and the item was not about the ice.

There was a road that opens in three days for carts and then for feet. There was a claim that wanted a body, a place and a name, and she was going to supply none of the three to a man who did not already have the last one. There was a man in Lowwater with a lamp and a cough and four hundred volumes who would copy anything put in front of him and had already been told by a Bureau to leave her out and had not.

None of that needed her on the Lowwater side of sixty feet of ice at the second hour of the morning. All of it needed her alive on the mountain side.

So she walked back up nine steps on a left hand and it took her five minutes, and she did not hurry and she did not stop, and when she came off the ninth step into the yard Old Gao was at the water cart with a bucket in each hand and looked at her and then looked at the road and then said, to nobody, “Then that is that, then.”

That was the entire extent of it. It was four months ago that she had stood in a market square and read a page of her own handwriting out loud to turn a crowd, and it had worked, and it had cost a captain a place in a building with no roof, and she had walked away from it.

## Second Bunk

The crew quarters had eleven men in them at the second shift and the room went quiet in a way she could measure.

She went to the second bunk and put her hand on the frame of it and looked at the length of it, and then she took her hand off, and she said, out loud, in the flat voice she used for tallies, to eleven men who had all been thinking about it for two days.

“I am taking this bunk. Not the cell and not the rooms. This one. And I am going to be on the furnace floor and at the Hall in the same week, and I am going to be at the Hall more than I am on the floor from now on, and if you want to say anything about that you may say it now and it will not go any further than this room.”

Nobody said anything.

One of them — Cao Lei, twenty-two, who had shown her a back stair in the autumn that avoided the elder's eye — looked at the door and then at the ceiling and then said, “It is a narrow bunk.”

“It is.”

“You will be back on the floor in a week and everybody in the yard will ask you what is in there that is worth being away for.”

“They can ask me,” said Ning Yan, “and I will tell them that there is paper.”

That was not a lie. She put her coat under the bunk with the log in the lining and the iron token against her hip and she sat down on the frame, and it was a narrow bunk, and the straw was old, and there was a plate against her chest and eight hundred jin against her name and the whole of the rest of her life in a room with no roof.

“You are going to be stuck on this mountain until the thaw,” he said, from further away than usual.

“Yes.”

“For four months.”

“Yes,” she said.

“That is a very stupid thing to have chosen.”

“Yes,” said Ning Yan. “It is the first one I have chosen in six months that is not somebody else’s.”
