# Five Days of Margin

The channel went thin the way late winter goes thin, still covering and less warm.

Ning Yan felt it in the mornings first, the way you feel a blanket gone thin, reaching past her skin for breath and finding less. Yuan spoke little and plain, present as a weight with no hands, and what he spoke was short. Ordinary use, week on week, stamp days and tower readings and carrying loads in thaw mud. The spike at the gate weeks ago had cost months. What was left was weeks, and weeks had gone to days while she carried.

She did the arithmetic on the mat at night against the calendar, the way she does tallies, without flinching.

About five days of ordinary margin. Past nine-tenths of the channel and holding there, with nothing behind it.

She had done the whole of it twice and it came out the same both times, which is what arithmetic is for. He has Ledger refused permanently, on her order and his own, and there is no trial standing anywhere in the bound to clear. He orders himself nothing. The only thing left in the bound that settles a Settlement is a listed act worked and ended by her hand, and there is no way to arrange one, and there is no way to buy one, and asking him to try would be asking him to spend her on a market that may not have it.

Five days was what ordinary use cost. Ordinary use was what she had refused to stop.

She did not write the number. Numbers in ink travel upward. She held it the way she holds the tablet, against the skin.

“You count it,” said Yuan. “You see me thin and you do not look away.”

“I count it,” said Ning Yan. “Five days at this rate. I work still. You rest and you watch.”

“I rest,” said Yuan. “I am here for all of it and I do not ask you to stop.”

That was the sentence she would remember afterwards, and she remembered it because she wanted an argument and he had refused to give her one. She had come up the stair that night with a whole speech ready about her arm and the county hours and how two more weeks of stone would make her a body that could not be got up a tower stair, and he had not taken it, because he had understood that she was not asking him for permission and had never intended to.

She worked still. Stone for the wall in the morning, water at the seventh hour, paper after the fifth. The loads sat heavier in thaw mud that took the sole and gave nothing back. Breath pulled short at two counts. Her arm served eleven minutes and went, a third and no more. She drilled the left-hand sequence once and stopped early to save breath, hook and turn and settle, unwritten, hers.

Du Hong was on the second stair when she came in off the yard, and he had the soup with him, and he sat down on the step because she was going to pass him and he was not going to let her pass him.

“Say it,” he said.

“There is a number,” said Ning Yan. “Not the number. There is one, it is mine to keep, and I am not telling you what it is.”

Du Hong sat with that on the step for a while. The tower made its ordinary noises under them.

“Right,” he said. “Then I will not ask again and I will not pretend I do not know you said it, and if a day comes when you are standing in a lane and you are calculating instead of looking, I am going to put my hand on your shoulder and you are going to answer me.”

“Answer you how.”

“With the word *walk*,” said Du Hong. “That is all. I will say *walk* and you say *entered* and I go home. I have practised it on the way up the stair.”

Ning Yan sat down on the step opposite him with her back against the wall, which she had not meant to do and which cost her nothing to let happen.

“You are eighteen,” she said.

“I am,” said Du Hong. “I have been eighteen since the autumn and you have been counting it as a reason for about four months and it has not once worked.”

She stayed on the step until the soup went cold and then she took it and ate it cold, standing up, which she had not done in two days and only noticed halfway through.

On the second morning she carried two loads in the hour she usually carried one, because one of the men at the intake shed had gone down with a bad back and the water for the kitchen was standing in a butt by the tower stair going flat, and water that stands goes bad and a bad butt of water is a thing the copying floor blames on somebody.

Her breath went to one count going up the second stair and she had to stop on the landing with the yoke across her shoulder and put her forehead on the wall, and she stood there long enough that a clerk's apprentice came round the corner, saw her, and asked if she wanted the intake clerk.

“I want the water carried,” said Ning Yan, and stood up, and carried it.

In the yard at the ninth hour she ran the sequence once and stopped early, half way through the carry, and stood in the mud with the lath across her knees until the chest settled.

Du Hong watched her do it from the tower door.

“You save breath,” he said. “Since when.”

“Since the calendar,” said Ning Yan. She did not tell him the number. Telling a man the cost of a thing is not the same as taking the thing away from him, and she knew which one she was holding. “Walk entered. Walk with the cart. No upper lane after dark.”

“The soup cools fastest there,” said Du Hong, and grinned, and walked the lower lane that night with the cart, entered, because he had heard the asking as an order and answered it with the whole of himself.

She attended the stamp day with him at the tenth hour, stood through the reading, signed, took the mark. The filing copy thickened. The buyer paid at three desks. The younger man watched the tower door. The counting room stacked paper by household.

Coming out of the county she stopped at the third desk, which was the one that had written down and dated her absence, and asked the young clerk a question that had nothing to do with the claim.

“This sheet you write. Who reads it after you?”

The clerk looked at her for the length of a breath and then at the man in the good coat, who was at the first desk and paying for nothing this morning.

“It goes up at the quarter,” said the clerk. “Same crate as the others.”

“Up to where?”

“Up,” said the clerk. He did not say to where, and he did not have to, because they were both standing in the shadow of a tower with a stair up it and a copying floor at the bottom of it.

She thanked him, which she almost never did, and went out into the market with the number five sitting behind her ribs where she could get at it.

In the evening she sat in the loft alone with the tower volumes under her and the tax sequence in her own body, and the safe-side sentence ticking behind her eyes, and the word on the Lowwater sheet in a second position, and five days of margin.

She did not ask to be forgiven. She did not forgive anybody. The middle of the pursuit held, grinding, claustrophobic, a room getting smaller every week: the terrace, the quarters, the furnace floor, the tower loft, a counting room she would never enter.

“You fear,” said Yuan. “You want more time and you have days.”

“I fear,” said Ning Yan. “I keep the hours. You stay.”

“I stay,” said Yuan. “I do not flinch at it either.”

She lay on the mat with the coat over her and the cord holding at the gap where the wind came in, and listened to the tower going to bed under her: brushes stopped, one cough and then another, the crate going up the stair on two men's shoulders with a thump against the third step where it always thumped.

Below, Feng coughed and copied, column at five, four copies walled, none upward. On the square the desks closed. In the lane the broker stacked paper, and the employed man reported, and feet waited behind coin for a body to walk unentered.

Five days. That was what was left between ordinary use and dry, and she had spent the day keeping county hours instead of spending him, which was the only mercy she had left to give.
