# Read Him Aloud

The man in the good coat came back to the Qinghe gate on the fifty-first day at the third hour past midday with a leather tube on his belt and nothing to carry.

Ning Yan saw him from the sixth step with forty people in the yard below. The thaw ran in the ditches and the eaves dripped, and the gate clerk sat at his table with his blotter and his bell, eleven weeks at the post and tired of it. The grey at the man's temple showed in the sun. His coat had been good eleven days of road ago and was good still. He set his hat on the table and spoke in the voice of a man buying lamp oil.

She came down the last six steps into the yard to hear him, and stood at the foot of them with her back against cold stone. The yard did what a yard does, which is go quiet without seeming to.

“Sir,” said the man. “I come again for the same two things, and I ask in daylight before witnesses, because I prefer a hundred ears to one whisper.”

“State it,” said the clerk.

“The place where the Chosen One sleeps. Any of them. The bunks, the terrace, a room off this court. I do not want to search it. I want to know where it is.” The man set a coin on the wood. “And the name on the carriers' papers. Any cart in eleven weeks with a packet somebody did not sign for. I pay for both and I pay for the writing, so no man wonders after whether he was paid.”

The clerk looked at the coin a long time. Then he wrote three lines in the time it takes to write three lines, and the yard said nothing for forty seconds.

Ning Yan stood at the foot of the steps with her left hand on the stair rail and her breath short. Two of three, asked aloud again, polite and unhurried. The third unasked because the third is a body, and a body walks about inside the wall and had just come down the steps to listen.

The man thanked the clerk, put his hat on, and turned to go up through the yard because the road to the square runs through it. He came ten feet and stopped, looking out through the gate at the road, ten feet from her, not looking at her. She had four seconds and used three on his face. It was the face from the eighteenth day, tired at the corner of the mouth, spending other people's money and wishing to stop and unable to.

Then he went up through the yard and out the far gate, and forty people watched him go.

## The Order

She went back up the steps to the rail above the road with the drip coming off the eaves.

“Read him,” said Ning Yan.

She said it the way she says tallies, flat and entire, with forty people below and the rail cold under her hand. It was not a request. She had made a request on the eighteenth day on the fourth step, and he had told her the price before the word, and she had not given it. Fifty-one days of tithe and a stage and a fever stood between that hour and this one. This was the word.

Yuan was quiet for a breath. She felt him there the way she feels the tablet, a weight with no hands.

“You mean it,” said Yuan. “You say it now and you mean it.”

“I mean it,” said Ning Yan. “I say it as an order. Read him through my eyes. Now.”

“I take it back,” said Yuan. “I hold it now and I read him.”

It happened the way a lamp is lit in a dark room, all at once and without noise. She felt him reach past her skin for the first time since the sixth day of the winter, out through her eyes toward the man walking down forty feet of churned white at the bottom of a hundred and forty feet of stone road. The channel pulled, not the crack but something deeper, under the foundation the way a root runs under a house. Her knees went watery on the step.

She put both hands on the rail, the dead right and the living left, and held on.

Through her eyes he read him. A name the man answers to, short and plain. A broker's counting room behind him with paper stacked by household and a brush that never stops. Instructions to buy records and not wordings: which clerk keeps the Qinghe advancement copies, what carriers carried packets unsigned, and which household in this town the Chosen One is entered against.

Coin for desks, coin for writing, nothing for force.

And one instruction standing outside the rest of them, because it carried no coin at all: a body is not to be asked for. A body is to be waited on. Polite. Patient. Paid by the week.

The reading lasted less than an hour by the sun, though it felt longer on the step. When it ended Yuan let go all at once, and she felt the letting go like a hand unclosing inside her chest. The channel thinned to weeks where months had been. About six and a half weeks of it left, thin and clear and hers to count against the calendar.

She stood on the step with the drip going off the eaves on her coat and did not sit down, because sitting down in front of forty people is a statement.

“What did you get,” she said quietly.

“A room with paper,” said Yuan. “A man who pays for records. A broker behind him who counts. I see them now and I hold them.”

“Do you hold it still.”

“No,” said Yuan. “I put it down now and I do not take it again.”

“Today.”

“Today and always,” said Yuan. “I refuse it now and I keep refusing. I do not hold it again this day and I do not hold it after. Ask me and I say no.”

She nodded once with her hands on the rail. The third term held between them, and she had paid it in the correct order: he had told her before, on the eighteenth day, what it takes and where it comes from, and she had waited fifty-one days to give the word, and now the word was given and taken and done.

“You pay it,” she said. “I see you thin and I know where it comes from.”

“I pay it,” said Yuan. “You know it and I am not hiding it. It comes from you and it does not return.”

The man in the good coat went down the road with the place unbought and the carriers' names unanswered and the body unspoken. Forty people went back to their business. The clerk put the coin in the box and the writing in the file.

She went up the rest of the steps with her breath short and her legs unsteady. At the top she stopped and looked back once at the road, the cut, the town smoke beyond. The bound had decided to move against him, and the decision was hers, spoken aloud on a step in daylight before witnesses, irreversible as a signature.

“You rest,” she said. “You do nothing now but watch.”

“I rest,” said Yuan. “I watch with you and I touch nothing.”

## Less Than a Day

He held it less than a day. By nightfall it was gone from him entirely, put down the same day and refused permanently, the way a man puts down a weight he will not lift again. He spoke little that evening, and what he spoke was plain and present, as he always speaks, with no past in it.

The gate clerk came up to the table at the second night hour with his blotter and his broken finger and said that the coin had gone into the box and the asking had gone into the file, and that a file is read by whoever the county trusts. He said it slowly, the way he says everything, and he did not look at her while he said it.

“The clerk writes what he is asked,” said Ning Yan.

“I write what I am asked,” said the clerk. “I do not write what I think.”

“Write it slow,” said Ning Yan. “Slow is right.”

That was the part she had not thought about, standing in the yard. A refusal is nothing until it is written, and then it is in a building, and then somebody reads it.

She lay in the second bunk with nine men breathing round her and felt the thinness in the channel the way you feel a blanket gone thin, still covering and less warm. About six and a half weeks. The arithmetic sat beside her with the fever gone and the breath short, and she did the new sum without flinching.

She had ordered it. That was the whole of it. No one else had, and no one else could.
