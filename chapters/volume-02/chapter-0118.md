# A Second Cough

The rain came through the Hall in four places and found the furnace floor schedule where it had been laid out flat on the stone to be copied, and the schedule is the season's labour and nine households and three hundred and eleven hours of one woman in a column of her own, and the rain came down on it from the fourth hour of the morning and was still coming down on it at the ninth.

They would have to move it. They always had to move it. In this building that had no roof since the autumn, anything anyone wanted to lay down anywhere was laying it down in weather, and the way it was done at Qinghe was that two men picked up a board of paper off a wet floor without wiping it and put it under the west table and let it be somebody else's morning.

She did it herself, left-handed, edge first, and got a corner of the season's labour wet through, and the ink on this schedule was water-soluble and had been entered by nine separate hands over five months and there were two places on it now where a household name had run a little downhill into the hour column.

That is what the Hall did. That was all it had done since the autumn. It rained on the paper and you picked it up and you did not tell anybody.

She carried it under the table with it flat, and the Hall clerk came in at the seventh hour and saw her doing it and said nothing about it and got out his own sheet and put the numbers off the copy she had brought down, because he was a copyist and the numbers were the work and not the water.

“Your hours are the whole of the third column,” he said. “I am entering them the way they stand on the certified and not the way you say they were.”

“Enter them the way they stand on the certified.”

“I am,” said the clerk, and then, with the brush still over the sheet, in the voice of a man who has been sitting at a table under a wet awning since the winter: “The name is the wrong order on this. It has been the wrong order since I came to the table.”

“I know what it is.”

“I have no other version to work from,” said the clerk, “and I have said so to a hall man once and he told me to bring a corrected sheet, and nobody has ever brought me a corrected sheet, and I am not going to go and look for one, because I am a copyist and I write what I am given.”

He wrote it in the wrong order. She watched him do it.

## The Second Bunk

She went in at the ninth hour and got up into the second bunk and lay on her coat with the arm out to the side, and the licence was still nailed over the foot of it, four feet of county paper with a stamp on it and six conditions on the back and one line of ink from the morning's rain running down the second condition and putting a small soft blur into the words *no other schedule counts*.

The defect line was on the other side of that, in the county's own transfer of her hand, and the paper had gone soft at the crease of it and the black of it had started to lift, and in about a month the crease would open and there would be a hole in the middle of the third line, and the third line was *aperture cracked through; stabilised at the crack; permanent*.

Permanent was on a piece of paper nailed crooked over a servant's bunk in the fog and rain, going soft in the damp.

That was the whole of four months.

She got up off the mat at Lowwater. She went down four days of road past a barrier with her name on a board in a captain's own hand. She worked a floor nine men would not sign her for. She took the last stage she will ever be allowed to take and told the whole of the bound about it in the middle of the afternoon.

She went down four days of road again and stood in a queue in the wet and had a piece of paper stamped. And then she carried it up four days of road and nailed it over her own bunk at a slant.

Ten slept in that room. Twelve bunks. Nine men on the crew and her on the settling floor.

A licence nailed over a bunk is not a reward and there is nothing in the arrangement that will make it one. She had bought it with a dead man's name and her own defect in her own hand and three hundred and eleven hours of a furnace's schedule, and she had put it in the only place she had ever lived in that was hers, which was over her own head.

The right hand lay out to the side. She had tested it twice, at the ninth hour and at the first night hour, which is the whole of what a day allows, and it had answered on one and not on the other, and it would go on doing that for the rest of her life and a piece of county paper nailed above it did not change one letter of it.

## The Count

“How much have you got left.”

She said it into the room, at the ninth hour, lying on her coat, to nobody, in the ordinary voice of a woman asking a question of the air, and the crew went on getting up and the stair went on taking boots off it.

Yuan answered, because that is what he does.

“A little under three-quarters,” he said. “About six weeks of ordinary use at the rate you are using me, which is the rate of a woman who talks to me out loud on an island twice a day and does not stop to do it.”

She lay with it. Six weeks. Two months and a half of the channel, and it did not go up while she lay there and it did not come down either, and it was the only number in the year that had behaved itself.

She did the arithmetic against the calendar, lying still, with the wood of the bunk frame behind her head and nine men breathing in a room that had held twelve.

Five weeks to the quarter's return. Five weeks to a crate that goes up the stair and down the road and into a county office where a registrar reads what comes in the crate in the order the crate is stacked.

Six weeks of channel. And the first return is the fifth week of summer, and there is no way to get a thing into a county book in the fifth week of summer if the book does not have it in the fourth week of summer. The return takes five weeks and is sent by a road that shuts.

Six weeks is not enough for a licence that has five weeks in it before its first return.

“There,” said Ning Yan. “That is the arithmetic.”

“That is the arithmetic,” said Yuan. “I am not going to do anything about it.”

“No,” said Ning Yan. “I am not going to have you do anything about it either.”

He was quiet for a moment.

“You are not going to ask me to stop.”

“No,” said Ning Yan. “I am not going to ask you to stop. If I ask you to stop you will not be able to and you will say so, and then it will be a thing between us that I did, and I have enough of those. And I have not been inside myself once this year without finding you at the bottom of it, and I am not going to spend the last of it on a thing that is only mine.”

She said that to the ceiling. She had it worked out in about nine seconds and she had known most of it since the mud flat, and the ceiling of the crew quarters was soot and forty winters of soot and it took it.

“You want to hear conditions,” he said.

“I want to hear conditions,” said Ning Yan, “and then I am going to tell you mine, and you do not get to argue with the list because you are the only other thing in this arrangement that can be spent.”

“You say them.”

“First,” said Ning Yan. “The channel is spent on nothing that is not hours. No merit from a thing that happens to me. No reading a man through my eyes. No Ledger, and there is no version of the rest of the winter where there is Ledger, and I do not care what it is worth, and it was once and that was once and it cost four and a half weeks that you did not tell me about.”

“It stands once,” said Yuan. “It stands behind your order and not mine, and I have not held it since the same day.”

“Second. You do not decide anything about my body, and that includes deciding how fast it goes, and that includes what you do not tell me about the rate of it. I found out the rate of it tonight by asking. I do not want to find out about the rest of it by asking.”

“I will not decide about your body.”

“Third.” She stopped. The stair had gone quiet and nine of them were asleep and one was not and she could not tell which.

“Third, there is no stage above five. The licence says it and my hand says it and the paper goes soft in the damp either way. If the ceiling ever comes into this I will know it is me that brought it and not the bound and not you.”

“And if I ever ask you to take me past it you tell me no, and I will not be angry, and that will not be a surprise to you.”

“I will tell you no,” said Yuan.

“Fourth. When it is gone, you tell me on the day. Not the morning after. The day.”

“You will have it on the day.”

“Those are mine,” said Ning Yan. “Say yours.”

There was a long silence in a coal-smelling room with ten sleepers in it.

“Mine are three,” said Yuan. “I do not decide anything about you. I tell you the cost before I spend anything. And if the designation rots, I do not hide it and I do not pretend it was always so and I do not get to ask you to hold on. Those have been mine since the ninth step and they do not change because you have said four.”

“They do not change,” said Ning Yan. “They are the ones I have checked most often.”

She lay on the coat with the lamp at the foot of the stair going down and the paper going soft over her feet and her brother's name in four registers reading *reconciled*, and four miles down the mountain the tower was lit and a fair hand was copying upward a bundle that went at the quarter.

## The Sheet

The courier came up on the second cart day with the crate and the schedule, and the crate went under the west table out of the rain, and there was a small sheet on top of it with a fair hand on it and no seal and no office mark, because a tower keeper writes to a tower keeper in a hand his own clerks can read.

*Licence stamped. Read the six on the back and write them out fair and put the copy where the county will find it and not where it will be liked.*

*One matter and then I go back to work.*

*There was a cough in my stairwell on the eleventh, at the second hour of the morning, and it was not mine. It came up from the tower door and it stopped on the fourth stair, the way a man stops to look at something, and then it went down again and out into the lane.*

*It has been up and down twice since. It has not asked me for anything. It has asked the girl on the ninth line what a schedule is, and she told him it is a sheet and she said it like that because she is her, and he thanked her.*

*He is at the first desk in the market twice a week and he buys nothing. He gives his name to nobody. He tells the carriers he is not from the Bureau, and there is a kind of man who says that who is from the Bureau and there is a kind who is not, and I have been in this trade twenty-two years and I do not know which one he is.*

*I have written it to you because I have written everything down since the twelfth day of the winter and there is nobody to write it to but you.*

*Come when you have to come. Do not come for this.*

*Lamp is lit. It is always lit.*

She read it twice on the second bunk with the sheet on her knees and the rain going through a roof four hundred yards off, and a word she had carried alone since a spring afternoon in a town four miles down that road sitting in the middle of her like a stone in a shoe, and nothing at all answering from anywhere behind her eyes except a man with no hands saying *I hear him and I do not know him*.

“Making the counting expensive,” said Yuan.

“Making the counting expensive,” said Ning Yan. She folded the sheet and put it in the lining beside a licence and a filing copy. “A man who asks what a schedule is, twice, in a market, in daylight, and buys nothing.”

“A man who is not buying a body.”

“He is not buying a body yet,” said Ning Yan. “He is reading the paper to see whether it is worth the paper.”

She got down out of the bunk and went to the door of the quarters and stood in it for a moment, in the rain smell off the Hall, with nine men asleep behind her and a licence going soft over the second bunk and a bolt of cloth about the size of a hand against her ribs, and looked up the mountain at a light in a tower.

It was burning. It was always burning.

It went down the dark in four miles of wet timber and one blaze at the bottom of it, and it was the only thing in nine counties a woman with a piece of paper in her coat could still look at and be certain of.

She had about six weeks of a man's life in her head, and a licence with six conditions on it and a claim on the back of it in a county's own hand, and a stranger had come up the road in her hearing and was standing at a desk in a market asking what a schedule was.
