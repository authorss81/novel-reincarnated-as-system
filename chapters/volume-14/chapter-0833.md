# The Morning Before

Eleven days since the ninth stage. Four hundred and forty-two days in that room. The cage out two hundred and nine days. Right arm open four hundred and forty-nine days and it is not going to close. One thousand one hundred and eighty-four days lighter at this morning and not one going back.

There was no person in the room. The lamp was standing on the wood a foot in from the near end of that table and the light went a hand’s width along it and stopped.

She said her figures out loud at the seventh hour, standing, in the ordinary voice, and then she went and stood against the leg of that table with her back to the dark and she did not do anything for about a count of ninety, and then she said the reason for that out loud as well, because she is not going to have a day in this room that cannot be read.

## Nine Years And Nineteen

She said, “This is the thirtieth of the month beginning two thousand seven hundred and fourteen and tomorrow is the first of another one, and there are thirty-one mornings between this one and the morning a bundle comes up that stair.”

She put her left hand against her fourth rib.

She said, “And I am going to spend this one getting ready, and I am not going to pretend that getting ready is a different thing from doing it. It is the same thing with less in my hand.”

She said, “Two figures and I am going to say both of them and I am not going to dress either of them.”

She said, “I have not written a sentence on anything since I was nine years old. That is nineteen years, and the last thing I put on paper was a date, in my own hand, on a leaf of a book of eight, and I have said that out loud in this room twice and I am not going to say it a third time.”

She said, “And the nib against my ribs has not touched a column in this building in nine years. Nine is not nineteen. I have been in this room four hundred and forty-two days and there has not been a column in it for me to touch. The nine years are somewhere else and I have not been back and I am not going to work out this morning what was in them.”

## The Book Out Of The Fold

And then, at about the eighth hour, she took it out of the fold of her shirt.

She said it out loud before she did it, standing, in the ordinary voice, with her right arm open at her side and her left hand going to the place low behind her fourth rib where a small open place the size of a coin has been for nineteen years.

She said, “I am taking the book out of the fold of my shirt and I am going to hold it in the light, and I am not going to open it to a blank one, and I want that said at the eighth hour before I do it, because it is the second time I have decided this and the second one is harder.”

And then she took it out.

It is a book of eight leaves, and it has been folded and carried against that rib for nineteen years, and it is soft along the fold and it is not stiff anywhere, and it comes out of the shirt a good deal flatter than a book and it is the size of her two hands laid one on top of the other.

She put it down on the top of that table in the light, a foot in from the edge, beside the four pieces of wood, and put her left hand flat on it and left it there.

She said, “That is the whole of the object. It has no cover on it and it has never had one. It is eight pieces of paper folded together and it is going to come apart in about two pulls if I let it, and I have let it twice and put it back both times.”

She said, “And it has been warm for nineteen years against that rib and it is warm now, and there is not one instrument in this building that could tell me how many leaves of it are left.”

## Counting By Touch

And then she did the thing she does with the strip of pale wood, which is that she read it with her hands, and she did it out loud, one at a time, and it took her about a count of ninety.

She said, “Eight leaves. One, two, three. Those three are the ones that are written on and the writing is in my own hand and it is not a sentence and it is not a figure and it is not a name.”

She said, “Four, five, six, seven, eight. Those five have been blank since I was nine years old.”

She stopped for about a count of ninety.

She said, “And the third one has something on it that is not a date. I said on the seventh of the month beginning two thousand seven hundred and fourteen that it was a date and that was wrong of me and it is in this room now that it was wrong of me. It is four marks in a row and they are not figures and they are not letters and I have never worked out what they are, and I am not going to today.”

She said, “So: three written on and five blank and the third of the three is four marks I cannot read. That is the whole of what is in my hand.”

## Not Opened To A Blank One

And then she held it in the light, and she said the second half of the morning out loud while she had it there, and this is where it got hard and she said so at the time.

She said, “Here is why it is harder today than it was on the seventh of the month beginning two thousand seven hundred and fourteen, and it took me about four days to work out and it is a stupid thing to have to say out loud.”

She said, “On that day I had not got the sentence. I said that out loud: a leaf is for the sentence and not for the wanting to have said it, and I believed it, and not writing on one was easy.”

She said, “I have got the sentence. I said it out loud in this room as a description of a sentence and I have carried it around in my head since and I have checked it against itself every morning since and it has not changed and it is not going to.”

She said, “And holding a sentence you are not allowed to spend is worse than not having it. That is the whole of it. Not writing today is not careful. It is the hardest thing I have done in this room in four hundred and forty-two days and I have done it once before and that time I had nothing in my hand.”

She stood there for about a count of a hundred and forty with the book lying open to nothing at all in the light on the top of that table and the lamp burning a hand’s width along the wood and twenty-two paces of dark behind the light.

She said, “And I am not going to open it to a blank one.”

She said, “Not tonight and not tomorrow and not on any morning in between this one and the morning I have said, and I am not going to say a date out loud in this room about it because every time I have said a date about that book it has moved.”

And then she folded the eight leaves together along the fold they already had and put it back against her fourth rib inside the fold of her shirt, and she did it slowly, with her right arm open the whole time, and she put her hand flat over the place afterwards and left it there for about a count of forty.

## Thirty-One Days

And then she said the last of that morning out loud, standing four feet off the near end of that table, in the ordinary voice, at about the ninth hour.

She said, “Here is what I have, out loud, standing here, so that it is in this room and not only in my head.”

She said, “A sentence. Five blank leaves against my fourth rib. A nib and a cake of ink against my ribs that have been against them for nineteen years and have not been let dry since the seventh of the month beginning one thousand eight hundred and seventy-four. And thirty-one mornings.”

She said, “And one arrangement, which I have said out loud twice and am not going to say a third time this morning: the leaf in my hand at about the first hour, the sentence said out loud standing here before the nib goes on the paper, the leaf folded, and it goes down that stair on the morning a bundle comes up and not one person is asked to carry it.”

She said, “And one thing I decided this morning at about the ninth hour and twenty minutes and I am putting it here now so that I cannot quietly not do it.”

She said, “That board is going to be lying face down on the top of this table on that morning when I say the sentence.”

She said, “Not turned over. Face down, on its own, at the near end of this table, about a foot in from the edge. I have not turned it over yet and I am not turning it over today. On that morning there is going to be a sentence going out into twenty-two paces of dark and I am not going to have it go out past a piece of wood that is already arranged.”

She said, “And the other three stay face up, because they are mine and they are his and they are a place, and I have not got a reason to put any of them face down and I am not going to invent one at the ninth hour on the thirtieth of a month to make this morning feel finished.”

She left the lamp where it was. The light went a hand’s width along the near end of that table and stopped.

And there were four pieces of wood lying face up side by side about a foot in from the edge of it, and there were five blank leaves against her fourth rib that were still blank, and there were thirty-one mornings in front of her and one of them was going to use the first of the five.
