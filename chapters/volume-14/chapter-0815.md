# What She Would Have To Stop

Thirty-five days since the eighth stage. Four hundred days in that room. The cage out a hundred and sixty-seven days. Right arm open four hundred and seven days and it is not going to close. One thousand one hundred and forty-two days lighter at this morning and not one going back.

A boy of sixteen was four feet off the near end of that table with oil on his fingers and a lamp standing on the wood between them, a foot in from the edge, the light going a hand’s width along it and stopping there.

She had been standing at the near end of that table since the seventh hour saying a figure out loud every morning for nineteen years, and on this morning she said a different one.

She said, out loud, in the ordinary voice:

“I am going to work out what a keeper is.”

## The Parts Of It

She said, “I am not going to be told. There is nothing in this building going to tell me and I am not going to go and ask, so I am going to take it apart the way I take a thing that comes up a stair, and I am going to say the parts out loud before I say what they make.”

She held up her left hand in the light, three fingers and a thumb on it, and then put it down flat on the wood.

She said, “One. There has to be a thing that is true. That is the first part and it is not optional and everything else is decoration on top of it.”

She said, “Two. There has to be a person standing where the truth is being made, at the time it is being made, not afterwards and not nearby. Because I have tried it afterwards and nearby. I have tried it for nineteen years in nineteen years of a room with one column in it and there is not one line in that column that I can account for from where I was standing when it was written.”

She said, “Three. There has to be a light to write it by. Not the room’s light. That does not matter. A light somebody else fed, at the time, in the place.”

Her breath was at nine and it stayed at nine.

She said, “Four. It has to be a thing nobody sent anybody for. And that is the part that has to be exactly true or the other three are worth nothing.”

## The Part That Cannot Be Asked For

And then she was quiet for about a count of ninety, and the lamp went on doing a hand’s width of the near end of that table, and then she said the fourth one again.

She said, “That fourth one is why the second place at the far end of this table has been empty for four hundred years.”

She said, “Not because there is no light. Not because there is no record. Because there has never once been a person in this room who was standing at the near end of this table for nothing.”

She said, “A person who came up here and was given a place would be given a job, and a person with a job can be given a second one, and the second one arrives on the strength of the first, and there is not one thing in this building above the line that could stop it, because there is not one thing above the line that knows either of us is up here.”

She put her hand flat on the top of the table between the two speeches.

She said, “I said that sentence to a boy of sixteen on the eleventh of the month beginning two thousand six hundred and fifty-four about a bundle of papers under that table and I meant every word of it and I have not taken one word of it back.”

## The Wall She Built, From The Other Side

He said, “That is what you said to me.”

She said, “Yes.”

Her breath stayed at nine for all of it.

He said, “About a bundle.”

She said, “About a bundle, and about a lamp, and about two nights, and about the whole of this building. And I built it out of one sentence, and you found it on your own in five nights without me giving it to you, and you have been standing behind it ever since.”

She waited about a count of ninety.

She said, “I have not thought about that sentence from this side of it until this morning.”

She said, “I built it so that nothing could be put in this room that I had not asked for. And it works.”

She said, “There is nothing in this room that has been put on me. There is a lamp and there are two nights and there is a boy who came up eight hundred and eighty steps on his own feet and was never asked to, and every one of the three things I say out loud about him is still true.”

It was at nine and it did not go up for him and it did not go up for her.

She said, “And I am standing here in the fourth hundred day of it working out how a keeper is put together, and I have worked out four parts, and I have got three of them.”

She said, “I have got the first part. There is a thing in this room that is true and it is in a column with a price at the head of it and it is also on a strip of pale wood two inches across with four figures of mine in it, and that is the rarest thing I have ever had.”

She said, “I have got the second part. I am standing at the near end of that table, four feet off it, at the time it is being made, every morning, and I have never once been late and I have never once done it from the far end.”

She put her hand flat on the wood again and left it there while she said the next of it.

She said, “I have got the third part, and it is a boy’s oil lamp and it came up eight hundred and eighty steps and not one person asked it to.”

She put her hand flat on the top of the table.

She said, “And I have not got the fourth part and I am never going to get the fourth part, and it is not about a boy of sixteen at all and that is what I had to find out this morning.”

She said, “The fourth part is that nothing was sent for it and nothing was given to anybody to do it and nothing in the world can be asked for it twice. And I have got three of those four things. I came up that stair on my own feet and I chose it and I would choose it again and not one person has handed me anything to be in this room for.”

She took her hand off the wood and turned it over and put it back the other way up.

She said, “What I have not got is the difference between a thing that was never given to anybody and a thing that nobody has got round to giving to anybody yet. Those are the same thing until the first time somebody thinks of it.”

She said, “And I have stood four feet off this table for four hundred days and not one of those four hundred days has been the first time, and on the thirteenth of the month beginning two thousand six hundred and fifty-four a room with a column in it made out what I am where I stand, and it has known ever since, and there is nothing in that column that has to be given to me for it to know it.”

She said, “That is the part that cannot be got. I worked it out at this table on the fourteenth of this month and I wrote nothing against it, and it is the fourth part and it is the only one of the four that costs anything.”

## The Part That Is Hers

And then she said the thing she had been standing there for, out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice, and it came out of her at about half the speed of everything else in that room.

She said, “And I have been keeping it anyway.”

She said, “I want that said in the ordinary voice where it exists and I am not going to dress it and I am not going to have it read as the fourth part being the one I have.”

She said, “For four hundred days I have stood four feet off the near end of this table and said every figure I have got out loud in the ordinary voice, and it has gone twenty-two paces into a room and hit nothing, and there is a stack of forty boards under the middle of this table with my handwriting on them and one fair hand under some of mine, and that is what a keeping looks like from the inside.”

She squared that hand up against the grain of the table and did not move it again.

She said, “The difference between what I am doing and a keeping is not the standing and it is not the voice and it is not the light. It is that no arrangement was ever written down.”

She said, “Nothing in this building was ever written down about what I do at this table, so there is nothing anybody can ask me for a second time, and a room with a column in it cannot put a job on a woman who has not got one.”

She said, “That is the sentence I have been standing behind for nineteen years and I said it out loud this morning as though it were still standing.”

She said, “And the cost of the eighth stage was named out loud in this room on the thirteenth of the month beginning two thousand six hundred and fifty-four and it is not coming off, and I am not going to say it again today.”

She said, “I worked out on the thirteenth of that month, standing here, that it does not stop me from reading and it does not cost me a thing I had. What it took was the other side.”

The count of a hundred and forty went past with nothing in it.

She said, “A wall. I built one in nineteen years out of one sentence a boy of sixteen found in five nights, and I built it to keep things out of this room, and a room with a column in it can now make out what I am where I stand and there is not one place in this building where I can stand that nothing in it can make out what I am.”

She said, “That is what it took. The wall is gone and I am not getting it back by wanting to.”

## The One Thing She Would Have To Move

And then she was quiet again, and the light went on going a hand’s width along the wood and stopping, and she looked at the two pieces of wood lying side by side there.

And she said the last of that morning out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice, at about the ninth hour, and she said it slowly on purpose.

She said, “There is one thing in this room that I would have to move, and it is not the boy, and it is not the lamp, and it is not the bundle, and it is not the bar against that wall.”

She put two fingers of her left hand on the near end of the strip of pale wood.

She said, “It is this.”

She said, “Here is the whole of it and there is no other version. A keeper stands at the near end of a table while the things that are true get said, and the things that are true get written on something, and the something is in front of the keeper’s hands while they stand there. That is the whole arrangement and there is not one part of it that is negotiable.”

She said, “And there is not one other piece of wood in this building with a figure of mine on it. Not one. It came up that stair in an armful on the first of the month beginning two thousand two hundred and ninety-four and it is a foot long and two inches across and there is no second copy of it anywhere on this mountain.”

Nine, and it held at nine, and the lamp went on doing a hand’s width of the wood while it did.

She said, “On the ninth of the month beginning two thousand six hundred and fifty-four I carried it twenty-two paces in my left hand with the lamp in that same hand because I would not leave a figure of mine in the dark. And I cannot do that every morning and also stand where the saying is done, because I have two hands and one of them has three fingers and a thumb on it and the other one does not close.”

She said, “So for it to be a keeping instead of a habit, this strip of pale wood has to stop travelling. It has to go off the top of this table and stay somewhere on this table, where a person who is not me could put a hand on it, and where a hand going along a column could reach it.”

She said, “That is what I would have to move. That is the only thing in this room that has to move and it is a foot long and it weighs nothing.”

She squared it up with two fingers of her left hand and left it exactly where it was.

And then she said the last sentence of that morning out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice, and she did not say a word for what it was.

She said, “In thirteen days a bundle comes up that stair and forty boards go on this table, and whatever is on it will go down beside this strip of pale wood, and I am going to be standing four feet off that table when it does.”
