# The Fare

He did not go out of the doorway.

She had said the last of what she had, which was that she did not have an answer that was small, and she had said it on the night before and had gone on saying nothing since.

And the room had gone back to being the room it had been for two hundred and forty days, which is to say that there was nothing in it to tell apart, and a boy of sixteen stood in the light of his own lamp with the lamp held up at about the height of his own chest because that is the height a person holds a lamp at when they are standing in a doorway of a room they have just been given the whole of.

That was the seventh hour of the eighth of the month beginning two thousand five hundred and thirty-four. It was the tenth hour of that same day.

She had been standing four feet off the near end of that table for three hours and she had counted ninety twice and a hundred and forty once, and every one of those counts had come out the way she had left it, and she had said nothing at all in any of them.

## The Thing Standing In A Doorway

She cannot let a thing stand in that room that has not been put into words where it exists, and the thing standing in the doorway of that room had not been put into words, and so she said it, out loud, in the ordinary voice, standing, in the dark:

“There is a boy of sixteen in the doorway of this room and he came up eight hundred and eighty steps at the seventh hour and it is the tenth hour and he has not gone, and he is holding a lamp up because a person holds a lamp up when they are standing in a room they have been told about.”

And he said, “My other one is out.”

She said, “Which one.”

He said, “The one on the wall on the floor I work on. It went out at some hour in the afternoon and I did not notice until I wanted to put a sheet down.”

She said, “Then you have two.”

He said, “I have three. That one has been out since the afternoon.”

She said, “And you are standing in a doorway at the tenth hour of the night with a lamp you cannot afford to be standing in a doorway with.”

He said, “That is the same lamp.”

She said, “I know it is the same lamp. I am saying it out loud because you have not said it out loud and it is standing in this room and I do not let it stand.”

## One More Try At The Small Sentence

And at about the ninth hour she tried again, because she has never in nineteen years let a thing go past without one more go at it.

She said, “The lamp is lit because there is a lamp that is lit.”

He said, “That is not a reason.”

She said, “I know that it is not a reason. I am telling you that I have got one sentence and it is that, and I have been holding it in front of me for a count of a hundred and forty and it is still that.”

He said, “Then why was it lit.”

She said, “Because it was lit.”

He said, “By who.”

And she stood there in the dark with her left hand on the near edge of that table and she found that she could get as far as the first of a month and no further, and that the rest of it was a room that had been standing at a table for four hundred years without ever once wanting to, and there was no sentence in that either.

It was a fact and it was the wrong shape for the thing he wanted, and she had said the whole arithmetic already and it had not been any use, and she was not going to say it a second time to a person who had come eight hundred and eighty steps to get it.

She said, “I did that on purpose and I am the only person in this building who could have done it, and that is the whole of what I have got for you, and I have said it once.”

He said, “All right.”

## What A Lamp Costs The Person Who Carries It

And then she worked out the thing she had been working on since the seventh hour, standing in the dark, with nothing in her hands and no light to work it out by, which is that she had spent the whole of the night before handing a boy of sixteen the arithmetic of a lamp in a cage, and had come to the end of that night with nothing in her hands, and had not once said what the lamp in his hand costs him.

She said it out loud, and it was the first time in this building that she had told anybody what a thing costs the person who brings it.

She said, “There is no oil in this building and there has not been for nine years, and there is none to be had on this mountain, and every lamp that is burning in this room tonight is a lamp that is not burning somewhere four miles down it.”

She said, “That is the fare. It is not the oil. The oil is only what it is made of. The fare is the lamp that is somewhere else while this one is here.”

She said, “There is one lamp on a wall where you work and it is the one that went out this afternoon. There are three more and none of them is on a wall, and they go where you go, and if one of them is burning in this room at the tenth hour of the night then it is not burning on that floor, and if it is not burning on that floor then there is a sheet of paper on that floor with nothing on it under it.”

And then she stopped and said the rest of it and she said it quickly, as though saying it quickly would make it smaller.

She said, “And you have to carry it up eight hundred and eighty steps and down eight hundred and eighty steps to do it, and the first four of those flights have a rail and the second four have not got one, and I have been up and down that second four every day of the two hundred and forty days I have been in this room and I have never once been able to do it with a lamp in my hand.”

She said, “And I am the reason for that spending, and I worked it out standing in the dark at about the eighth hour tonight and I had not worked it out before that, and you are not going to get a better reason than that one out of me.”

He said, “How much is it.”

She said, “I cannot tell you how much and you would not believe a woman who handed you a figure she had made up in her head in the dark.”

He said, “I did not mean how much oil. I meant how much of it is yours.”

## Four Nights Out Of Seven

And that is the question she had an answer to, and she gave it to him standing up, in the ordinary voice, at the near end of that table, and it came out with a number in it that she had worked out on the days between the seventh and tonight.

She said, “Four nights out of every seven.”

She said, “And the first, and the third, and the fifth, and the seventh of the seven. I will say them out loud on the night in question and you will come on those, and on the other three you will not come, and if you come on one of the other three I will not say anything about it, and you can decide for yourself what that is.”

He said, “That is my oil.”

She said, “I know whose oil it is. That is why I am saying the number out loud instead of asking you for it. I am not asking you for anything. I am telling you what I am going to take and letting you decide what you do about it, and those are not the same thing, and only one of them is mine to do.”

He said, “Why four.”

And she told him the reason, and the reason was a day, and she gave him the day without giving him the date.

She said, “Because there is a day coming that this room does something on, once a month, and on that day it is worked in front of a person for the only time in thirty days, and I have never seen it without light and there is no light in this building, and if there is going to be anybody standing in this room on that day then I am going to have to be able to afford them.”

She said, “And it is not a day I am having. It is a day I am going to be in and I do not know what happens in a room on that day when the only light in it belongs to somebody else.”

He was quiet for a while, and the flame stood up straight in his hand, because there is no draught in that room.

Then he said, “You have no right to give me that number.”

She said, “I know I have not.”

He said, “Then I will take it anyway, and not because you have a right to give it.”

## Down The Low Door

And he went down the low door at about the eleventh hour with his lamp and the count of what he had promised her, and she went to the first four hundred and forty with her left hand on the rail after him because a dark stair is worse going down than coming up and she had never once let a person go down that one in front of her.

He said at the fourth landing, from under her hand, “What will you do on the three.”

She said, “Stand here.”

He said, “That is not an answer.”

She said, “It is the whole of the answer I have and I am not going to make one up for a stair at the fourth landing, and you can hear me saying that I have not got one.”

And he went on down and the sound of his boots went away and got smaller and stopped.

And she went back up the first four hundred and forty alone, and stood four feet off the near end of that table in the dark, and did the one figure that mattered to her, which was that she had said four out of seven out loud to a boy of sixteen and she did not know in what world that was a kindness and she had said it anyway.

And she did not know what he would do with the three nights a week she had just told him not to come, and that was the first thing in two hundred and forty days she had not been able to put into words, because it had not happened yet.
