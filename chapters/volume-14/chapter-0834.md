# The First Of The Five

Eighteen days since the ninth stage. Four hundred and forty-nine days in that room. The cage out two hundred and sixteen days. Right arm open four hundred and fifty-six days and it is not going to close. One thousand one hundred and ninety-one days lighter at this morning and not one going back.

There was no person in the room. The lamp was standing on the wood a foot in from the near end of that table and the light went a hand’s width along it and stopped.

She turned the fourth piece of wood over at about the eighth hour and she said out loud that she was doing it before she did it, standing, in the ordinary voice, which is the rule.

## The Board Face Down

She put two fingers of her left hand on the near edge of it and turned it over onto its own face, not over the other way, and slid it about a foot along the wood so that it was not under the lamp.

She said, “I said at about the ninth hour and twenty minutes on the sixth of the month beginning two thousand seven hundred and forty-four, standing four feet off the near end of that table, that I was going to do this on the morning I said the sentence, and I am doing it at about the eighth hour this morning, and I am not going to get a date out of the fact that I did it early.”

She said, “And I want it said that I did not turn it over. It is lying on its own face. There is a board on this table with a name cut into it in a third hand and it has been face up since the first of the month beginning two thousand six hundred and eighty-four and it is staying face up and I have said out loud in this room that I am not turning that one over and I have not.”

She said, “And there is a third board on this table describing where a place is and what keeping it is, and it is face up, and there is my own strip of pale wood, and it is face up.”

The light went a hand’s width along the near end of that table and three pieces of wood and stopped, and the fourth one was lying on its other face in the dark about a foot further along.

## The Sentence, Said First

And then she took the book out of the fold of her shirt and she opened it to the first of the five, and she put it down on the wood in the light, and she squared it with her left hand twice.

And then she said the sentence out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice, four feet off the near end of that table, into twenty-two paces of dark, before the nib went anywhere near the paper.

She said, “I am going to say this once and it is going to be said out loud in this room before there is one word of it in ink, and that is the fourth of the six and I have not broken it in nineteen years and I am not breaking it now.”

She said, “Here it is. The whole of it. There is not a second half.”

She said, “I refuse the place at the far side of this table.”

She said, “I refuse a binding.”

She said, “I refuse to be named in the column at the far end of it.”

She said, “No person is going to feed that lamp in place of me.”

She said, “And I am going to keep standing four feet off the near end of this table at the seventh hour and saying every figure I have got out loud in the ordinary voice.”

She said, “And you may do what you like with the paper.”

And then she stopped, and she stood there for about a count of a hundred and forty, and she did not say it again.

## The Nib

And then she took the nib and the cake of ink out from against her fourth rib, and she said the one thing that had to be said about them out loud before she used them, because a thing gets said before it is a line anywhere.

She said, “That nib has not touched a column in this building in nine years and it has not touched this book in nineteen, and the ink has not been let dry since the seventh of the month beginning one thousand eight hundred and seventy-four, and I am going to put one of them against the other for the first time since I was nine years old.”

She said, “And I have three fingers and a thumb on the hand it is in and my forearm goes down about the width of the little finger on the other one while the nib is on the paper, and it is going to do that, and if it goes down further than that then I am going to put the nib down and stop, and I am not going to write a whole refusal in a hand I cannot feel.”

She put the nib into the ink, and then she put it against the first of the five blank leaves, about a finger in from one end of it, and she did not start writing at once.

She said, out loud, standing, with the nib touching the paper:

“I am going to write it exactly the way I said it. The same words, the same order, and no word added and none taken out, because the fourth of the six is that a thing gets said out loud before it is a line anywhere, and a version that is better written than the one that was said is not the same thing.”

## Six Sentences

And then she wrote it, and it took a count of ninety, and her right forearm went down while the nib was on the paper by the width of the little finger on her left hand and no further.

The hand she writes with has three fingers and a thumb. The ink is old. It is a small flat sound in a room with a lamp burning a hand’s width along the top of a table twenty-two paces long, and she has not made that sound in nineteen years and there is not one instrument in this building that has ever heard it.

And then she took the nib off the paper and put it back against her fourth rib, and she did not say anything for about a count of forty.

And then she said it out loud again, standing, in the ordinary voice, from four feet off the near end of that table, reading it off the paper in the light for the first and only time, all six sentences, because she cannot let a thing stand in that room that has not been put into words where it exists.

## What She Cannot Do

And then she said the rest of that morning out loud, and she said it before she touched the paper again, because she knew it about herself and she would rather say it than find it out.

She said, “I am not going to be able to find that leaf again.”

She said, “I wrote on the twentieth of the month beginning two thousand five hundred and ninety-four a paragraph of words on one board out of a stack of forty in this room, and I cannot tell that board from the other thirty-nine, and I have stopped expecting to.”

She said, “And there are now eight leaves in this book and I am going to write on one of them with something I can read in the light of a lamp on this table, and in about two years it will be a book of eight leaves with one sentence on it and I will not be able to tell which one.”

She said, “And I am not going to number them. I have had this book for nineteen years and I have never put a mark on the outside of it and I am not going to start now, because a mark is a figure and I have just written a refusal and I am not going to put a figure next to it.”

She said, “That is the price of the paper and it is a stupid price and it is the only one on offer.”

## Folded, And Nowhere

And then she folded it, and she folded it in four with her fingers and her thumb because there is nothing in that room that folds paper.

She said, out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice, at about the ninth hour:

“I have folded it in four. It is a square about two of my fingers across and it is going to go down nine hundred and four steps in a cloth with forty boards on the top of them, and it is not going in anybody’s hand, and not one person is going to be asked to carry it, and that is the whole of the arrangement and I have said it out loud twice and this is the last time I am going to say it.”

She held it in her left hand for about a count of forty.

She said, “And I am not putting it anywhere.”

She said, “It is not in the book. It is not under the table with the bundle. It is not in the stack of forty under the middle of this table with the paragraph of words on one of them. It is not in the drawer, because there is no drawer in this room and has not been one since I got here.”

She said, “It is in my left hand and it is going to be in my left hand for the next twenty-four days and it is going to be in the fold of my shirt against my fourth rib tonight, and I have not put it inside the book because the one thing I would not be able to do is lose a page in a thing I cannot find things in.”

And then she put her right arm open at her side and got the book out of the fold of her shirt with her left hand and put the eight leaves back against her fourth rib, and then she put the folded leaf in with them, outside the paper, against the rib, and she held her hand there for about a count of forty.

And then she took her hand off, and squared her own strip of pale wood up, and squared the board with the name on it, and left the third one where it lay.

And there was a piece of paper against her fourth rib with six sentences on it in her own hand, written on the seventh of the month beginning two thousand seven hundred and forty-four at about the eighth hour of the morning, and there were twenty-four mornings in front of her, and the first morning a bundle came up that stair was the first of the month beginning two thousand seven hundred and seventy-four, and nothing had gone down that stair out of that room in nineteen years.
