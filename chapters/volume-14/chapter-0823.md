# The Morning After A No

Fifty-three days since the eighth stage. Four hundred and eighteen days in that room. The cage out a hundred and eighty-five days. Right arm open four hundred and twenty-five days and it is not going to close. One thousand one hundred and sixty days lighter at this morning and not one going back.

There was no person in the room and nothing in the room was required to answer her and she had said that out loud on the seventh of the month beginning two thousand six hundred and fifty-four and had said it once since.

She got up on the sixth of the month beginning two thousand seven hundred and fourteen and stood four feet off the near end of that table at about the seventh hour with the lamp standing on the wood a foot in from the edge and the light going a hand’s width along it and stopping.

## What She Did Not Wait For

She did not wait.

That is the whole of what happened at about the seventh hour and five minutes and she said it out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice, immediately, because she has said figures before they are anything else since she was nine years old and she was not going to break the rule on the morning after.

She said, “I waited yesterday. I stood here at about the ninth hour and twenty with my hand on that wood and I let a count of a hundred and forty go past to see whether anything came back from the far end of this table.”

She said, “And I have not waited this morning and I am not going to wait tomorrow morning, and that is the first thing I did differently and I want it said at about the seventh hour on the sixth of this month before I do anything else, because I know exactly what I am frightened of and it is not that something answers.”

She said, “I am frightened of a morning when I notice that waiting has become the shape of my standing here. That has never happened in nineteen years because there has never been anything to wait for.”

## The Order She Changed

And then she did the second thing differently, and she did it on purpose, and she said out loud that she was doing it on purpose.

She said, “I am going to say why first.”

She put both her hands down at her sides.

She said, “Every morning since I was nine years old I have stood four feet off the near end of this table and said the figures first and the reason afterwards, in that order, and I have never once done it the other way round. Figures. Then why. Four hundred and eighteen mornings of that.”

She said, “And this morning I am going to say the why first and then the figures, and I am telling you at the seventh hour that I have changed it, so that you cannot work out later that I did it by accident.”

And then she said the why, standing, in the ordinary voice, and it came out short and she did not dress it up.

She said, “Because a record is only worth something if somebody else has heard it, and there has not been one other person in this room since I came into it, and I said that on the ninth of the month beginning two thousand five hundred and thirty-four and I have not taken it back since and I am not taking it back this morning.”

Then she said the figures, which is what she has always said.

## What She Was Not Sure Of

And then she stood there and waited, not for the room, which has never answered anything, but for herself, and she found that the morning was lighter than the morning before it by an amount she could not put a number on, and she did not like it.

She said, out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice:

“I am lighter this morning and I am not going to say I do not know why, because I do know why, and I said yesterday at about the ninth hour and five minutes that I was calm, and calm was the coldest thing I have been in this room in four hundred and eighteen days.”

She said, “So it is not lightness. It is the other side of the same coin, and I have not got a word for the two of them together and I am not going to make one up at the eighth hour on the sixth of this month.”

She said, “And I do not trust it. I want that said in this room this morning and I want it said plainly. A woman who has said no to a thing that works ought to feel heavy. I have watched people get light when they have done the right thing and I have never once been one of them and I do not know what to do with it.”

She put her hand flat on the top of the table.

She said, “I have said a no in this room and the room has not answered it and I have not been relieved, and I have not been sorry, and I am lighter. I am going to keep both of those.”

## What She Went Back To

And then she went back to work, and she said that out loud too, because a woman who says something at the seventh hour and then goes and stands about a count of a hundred and forty doing nothing has done the thing she did on the tenth of the month beginning two thousand six hundred and fifty-four and got a hundred and forty counts of for nothing.

She said, “I said a no yesterday and it is said and it cannot be unsaid and I said that out loud as well, and I am not going to say one more word about it today. It is said. It is in the room. That is the whole of what happened to me yesterday and it is finished.”

She said, “And I am going to do the whole of what I do every morning.”

She said, “Which is this. There is a strip of pale wood lying face up about a foot in from the near end of this table and a price at the head of it that does not date, and four figures of mine in four pairs of cuts under that, and eight pairs of cuts under them with nothing in any of them.”

She did not move while she was saying it.

She said, “And there is a board beside it with a name on it in a third hand that has not gone back down that stair. And there is a third board beside that which says where a place is and says that it is not given to anybody.”

She said, “And none of those three have moved in five days, and I did not move them, and the lamp goes a hand’s width along this table and no further, and the bar is against the wall on the left where it has been since I came up that stair with it on my shoulder, and the bundle under the far end has two turns in it and not a knot.”

She said, “That is the morning. That is all of it. That is what four hundred and eighteen days has produced and it is going to be produced again tomorrow at the seventh hour in the ordinary voice.”

## The Three Pieces Of Wood

And then, at about the eighth hour and forty minutes, she did the thing she has done every morning since the first of this month, which is to go and look at what came up that stair, and today there were three of them and a day old.

She carried the lamp no distance at all, because they were lying a foot in from the edge of the near end of that table where she had put them, and the light went a hand’s width along the wood and did all three of them.

She read the third hand’s board first and said it out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice: a name, one worn pair of cuts under it with nothing in them, and a column at the far end of the shelf with the same name in it stroke for stroke.

She read the place board second and said it out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice: between the twentieth pace and the end of it there is a place, it is not a seat, nobody has sat in it, it is kept, it has been kept for four hundred years, it is not given to anybody, and it is kept by being there when the figures are said out loud.

And then she put two fingers on her own strip of pale wood and did not read it, because she can read that one without light and has been able to for nineteen years.

And she said it out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice, before she did anything else with the morning:

“Three boards a foot long and two inches across lying side by side on the near end of this table. One of the three is mine and I have not said that out loud this morning and it is not going to surprise me that it is the one I read first every day.”

## The Two Hands

And then she stood there and let the thing she had been carrying since the tenth of that month come out, because it was going to come out eventually and she would rather it came out in the ordinary voice at the eighth hour than at the ninth hour at the end of a night.

She said, “I have not worked out whether the fair hand and the third hand are the same hand.”

She said, “I have seen the fair hand nineteen times underneath my own arithmetic on boards in a stack under the middle of this table, and once at the end of a stroke on a board that came up nine hundred and four steps in the dark on the first of this month, and that is twenty times, and twenty times is still not enough to say a thing about a hand.”

She said, “They could be the same and I am not going to find out which. The one that wrote the name is not the hand at the far end of this table and neither is the fair one and neither of them is a hand I have watched write anything else in nineteen years.”

The light went a hand’s width along the wood and stopped.

She said, “And I have decided that I am not going to work it out, and that is a decision and not a giving up, and I made it at about the eighth hour and forty-five minutes on the sixth of this month standing at this table and I am putting it in the room now.”

She said, “Because the finding would be worth nothing. A third hand that corrects my arithmetic is a hand. A third hand that writes my name and a description of a place is a hand. There is not one thing I can do with either of them and there is not one thing I would stop doing.”

## The One New Sentence

And then she said the last of that morning, and it came out at about the ninth hour and five minutes, and it was the one sentence of that day that she had not said before in nineteen years in any room, and she stopped dead after it.

She said, “I said a no in this room yesterday at about the ninth hour and ten minutes and there is not one thing in this building I can put it on.”

She stood there.

She said, “There is a strip of pale wood and it has a price at the head of it and a column with a price at the head of it is a column of amounts. And a no is not an amount. And a board that came up that stair does not go back down it and there is no board in this building that has not been put on that table by somebody who was not me.”

She said, “And a person can write on a leaf. That is the thing. A person can put a nib on a piece of paper and a thing that is not an amount can be on it, and I have not written a sentence on anything since I was nine years old.”

She put her left hand against her fourth rib, where a book of eight leaves has been against that rib for nineteen years, and five of those eight leaves are still blank, and she left her hand there for about a count of ninety and took it off again.

And then she said the figures out loud one more time in the ordinary voice, standing four feet off the near end of that table, and the light went a hand’s width along the wood and stopped, and nothing looked at her, and nothing in that room said one word.
