# The Middle Of The Table

Fifteen days since the ninth stage. Four hundred and forty-six days in that room. The cage out two hundred and thirteen days. Right arm open four hundred and fifty-three days and it is not going to close. One thousand one hundred and eighty-eight days lighter at this morning and not one going back.

There was no person in the room. The lamp was standing on the wood a foot in from the near end of that table with a trimmed wick and the light went a hand’s width along it and stopping there, and she said her figures out loud at the seventh hour, standing four feet off the near end of it, and then she went and stood about a count of ninety doing nothing at all, on purpose.

## Twenty-Two Paces And One At Eleven

She said out loud what she was doing, standing, in the ordinary voice, because she has said figures before they are anything else since she was nine years old and she was not going to break that at the eighth hour of a day she was about to break something else on.

She said, “I said no in this room on the fifth of the month beginning two thousand seven hundred and fourteen and I said out loud the next morning that I was not going to say it out loud a second time.”

She said, “And that is the rule and it is the only rule in this building I have ever set for myself, and it is nineteen years old, and I am going to break it once today, on purpose, and I am telling you at the eighth hour so that a person in this room cannot work out later that I did it by accident.”

She said, “And I am going to break it in the middle of that table.”

She put her left hand on the leg of that table at about the fourth pace and said the reason first, because that is the order she has used for nineteen years and she was not going to reverse it on the one morning she was doing something new.

She said, “Because a no that is not said again is a no that can be read as a thing that was settled. And a room that has been owed a second thing and has not been given one can take the quiet for the second thing.”

She said, “That is not because the room is listening. It is because I said out loud on the seventh of the month beginning two thousand seven hundred and fourteen that a no said in a room with one hand in it is not a no to that hand, it is a noise.”

She said, “And a noise that is not followed up is not a noise anybody has to do anything about.”

## A Room Owed A Second Thing

She said, “So here is the shape of it and it took me four days to work it out and it is short.”

She said, “I have read that board out loud. I said every line of it into twenty-two paces of dark on the first of the month beginning two thousand seven hundred and forty-four, in my own voice, and then I said what it is and what it is not and what the price is, and then I said out loud that I was not going to say the refusal out loud that day.”

She said, “That is a reading and it is not an answer. And there has been nothing since but mornings.”

She said, “A board with no name and no date on it has been lying on this table through every one of them going nowhere, and the lamp has been burning a hand’s width along this end of it, and I have stood here every morning at the seventh hour in the ordinary voice saying figures out loud about a lamp in a cage and an arm and a bar on a wall.”

She said, “And none of that is one word about a person.”

She stopped for about a count of ninety.

She said, “A room does not need me to say yes. It only needs me to stop.”

## The One Sentence

And then she went along that table, and she said it out loud at the start of it in the ordinary voice because she had said she would, and then she did not say one word else for eleven paces.

She went along the near side of that table on her feet with her left hand on the leg of it, and the light went off her at about the fifth pace and she went on in the dark, and at eleven paces she took her hand off the leg of that table and stood in the middle of it.

She was eleven paces from the near end and the same again from the far end, and there was nothing at either end of her.

There is no light at eleven paces. The lamp is nine paces back at the near end and it goes a hand’s width along the wood and no further, and she could not see her own hands or the front of her, and the flag in the floor that moves is at the nineteenth pace and she did not reach it.

And then she said one sentence, in the ordinary voice, standing up, out loud.

She said, “I am not answering that board, and this room has never once been owed a second thing by me, and it is going to have to do without one for as long as I am standing in it.”

And then she did not fill the count.

She stood there for about a count of a hundred and forty, and the lamp was nine paces behind her at the near end and there was no light on her at all and she could not see her own hands, and nothing at the far end of that table moved and nothing came back.

## What It Cost

And then she went back along that table to the near end, with her left hand on the leg of it, and when her foot came into the light again she stood four feet off the near end of that table and said out loud what it had cost her to do that, which is the rule, and the cost is not the standing in the dark.

She said, “It is not the dark. I have stood in the dark in this room four hundred and forty-six days and I did it on purpose tonight as well.”

She put two fingers of her left hand on the near end of the strip of pale wood.

She said, “The cost is that I cannot tell whether it went anywhere.”

She said, “Everything I have said in this room for nineteen years I have said from four feet off this end of that table, standing up, in the ordinary voice, one thing at a time, before it was a line anywhere. That is the fourth of the six and it is the whole of my method and it is not a habit, it is the arrangement.”

She said, “Every word out of me from this end of this table has gone one way. Twenty-two paces. At a column with a price at the head of it, in a stroke that starts in the same place on every board and does not stop on anything.”

She said, “And tonight I stood eleven paces along and I said one sentence with nothing at either end of it, and there is no instrument in this building that can tell me whether a sentence said in the middle of a table counts as a thing that went across it or is a sentence a woman said to herself at eleven paces from where she usually stands.”

She stood there for about a count of ninety.

She said, “And I did it on purpose. I wanted one sentence in this room that I could not promise anybody had heard, because I have got one sentence that nobody in this building can check and I wanted to know what that feels like before I write the other one out.”

She said, “It feels like nothing. And I am not going to tell anybody that but this room.”

## What The Middle Of Twenty-Two Paces Is

And then she said the last of it out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice, at about the ninth hour, and it came out in the flat voice she uses when she has worked out a thing and does not like it.

She said, “Here is what the middle of twenty-two paces is.”

She said, “It is eleven paces from the end of that table where a hand has been writing down whatever goes across it for four hundred years, and eleven paces from the end of that table where there is a person who has been standing four feet off it every morning since she was nine years old.”

She said, “And it is the only place in this room that a sentence cannot be accused of having been aimed.”

She said, “And there is no lamp in the middle of that table. There is no board. There is not one instrument in this building that knows I stood there, and I am the only witness to my own standing, and I have been the only witness to my own standing for nineteen years and I did not know until this morning that that was a fact about me and not a number.”

She put her hand flat on the top of that table.

She said, “That is what the middle of twenty-two paces is. It is the part of this room that has never been written on and never will be.”

## Back To The Near End

And then she squared her own strip of pale wood up and squared the board with the name on it, which are the two she squares every morning, and left the other two where they lay, and the light went a hand’s width along the near end of that table and stopped.

There were four pieces of wood lying face up side by side about a foot in from the edge of that table.

Hers. The one with the name cut into it in a third hand. The one that describes where a place is and what keeping it is.

And the one that has no name on it and no date on it and one pair of worn cuts under it with nothing in them, which has been lying there since the first of the month beginning two thousand seven hundred and forty-four, and which she has read every morning since, and which she did not answer today, and which she said out loud at about the ninth hour and twenty minutes that she was not going to answer tomorrow either.
