# The Grain Runs Up

She had been putting her hand on that strip of pale wood in the dark for two hundred and seventy days and she had never once seen it, and on the eighth of the month beginning two thousand five hundred and sixty-four at the seventh hour she said that out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice, at the near end of that table, to a boy of sixteen holding a lamp two inches in from the edge of it.

“Then get it into the light.”

“It is under the table.”

“I know where it is.”

“I know where it is as well and that is not the difficulty.”

## Two Hours

He had said at the fourth of that month that he would be in this room two hours on the nights she was talking, and that was the arrangement, and he had taken the wick down and stood with the lamp.

She said, “You have two hours and I am going to use about nine minutes of them and I want you to know that before I start.”

He said, “Why are you telling me that.”

She said, “Because I have cost you three nights out of six since the fourth and two hours out of every one of the other three, and you did that to make your own count come out right, and the least I can do is tell you what I am doing with the part of it that is left.”

And then she said the thing that had been standing behind the whole of that evening, which she had carried down that stair on the fourth and had not got out of herself in five nights.

She said, “I told you on the fourth of this month that there was one more night like the thirtieth and that I would say it when I had said it out loud in this room on my own for about nine days. It is the eighth. I have said it every day since and I am saying it tonight.”

He said, “Go on.”

She said, “The first of the month beginning two thousand five hundred and ninety-four is the day this room does what it does, and a man comes up that stair at the second hour of it with forty boards, and I am going to be standing in here when he does.”

She said, “And four days after that I am going to take the seventh stage of my own life. I gave you the first of those days on the twelfth and the reason for it on the twenty-second. What is new tonight is that I have said both of them in one breath in one room with a person standing in it.”

He said, “All right.”

## Getting Down To The Wood

She took the lamp off the near end of that table and put it on the stone, the way she had done on the twenty-sixth, and she told him she was taking it before she took it, and then she went down onto the stone on her hands and her knees and went in under that table.

A lamp held low lights the floor and the undersides of things and not the tops of anything. That is what it does, and she had worked it out on the twenty-sixth with him watching her, and it had taken her a count of ninety to go along the near side of that table and get to where the boards were.

There is a cross-beam across the shoulders of a person who goes in there, and she had had a beam across her shoulders on the second of every month since the second of the month beginning two thousand three hundred and twenty-four.

She said, out loud, on her back on the stone with the lamp on the floor beside her head, “There is a beam and there are two legs of that table and there is a strip of pale wood on the stone between them, and I am going to have to put my hand on it without being able to see my own hand, so I am going to do it out loud.”

He said, “Do it out loud.”

And she put her left hand out onto the stone and went along it until her fingers were on the wood, and she knew it by the grain of it, because the grain runs up and her fingers have run up that grain on the second of every month since she was seventeen.

She said, “That is it.”

He said, “Say how you know.”

She said, “The grain runs up. That is how I have known it for two hundred and seventy days and it is the only way I have known it, and I have just put my hand on a thing I have not been able to see and known it in about a second and a half, and I would like that said out loud in here, because I am not certain I will be able to do it once I know that it is a thing a person can do.”

## Twelve Pairs

And then he came down onto the stone beside her, on his knees, and put his own hand on the wood, and he did not ask her to move and she did not move.

She said, “It is in your way.”

He said, “I cannot see it in yours.”

And the light went along that strip of pale wood for the first time in that room, because the lamp in the cage at the far end of that table had been the only light this building had ever put on a thing in here, and it had gone out on the first of the month beginning two thousand five hundred and thirty-four, and she had stood at the near end of that table on the seventh of that month and said out loud in the dark what was cut on that wood, and the wood had been under a table in the dark for every day since.

He read it.

He read it slowly, and he did not have her mouth to get it out of, and that is the whole of what happened on the stone floor of that room on the eighth of the month beginning two thousand five hundred and sixty-four.

He said, “There is a figure at the head of it.”

She said, “There is.”

He said, “It is not one number. It is three. Days and hours and minutes.”

She said, “It is.”

He put his finger back at the top of the wood and went over the head of it again, slowly, the way a person goes over a line they have been given the wrong reading of.

He said, “And the same three are cut again in the first pair under the head.”

She said, “The same three.”

He went on down past the head of it.

He said, “And there is a five in the second pair.”

She said, “There is a five in the second pair and I put that there myself on the twenty-second of the month beginning two thousand four hundred and forty-four at the ninth hour with a nib and a cake of ink against my fourth rib, kneeling on this stone.”

He said, “I know. You told me that.”

She said, “I told you that. I am saying it out loud because it is a figure and I say figures before they are anything else, and I have one arm open and I cannot get comfortable on this floor and I am not going to leave a piece of it out.”

And then he went along the rest of it with a fingertip, and he counted, and she lay on the stone and listened to a boy of sixteen count her own record out loud in a room that had had no light in it for thirty-seven days.

He said, “Twelve pairs.”

She said, “Twelve pairs.”

He said, “And nothing in any of them.”

She said, “Nothing in any of them, and I said that out loud to you on the seventh of the month beginning two thousand five hundred and thirty-four, and you have got it right, and that is the first time in two hundred and seventy days that a figure of mine has been in this room in a voice that is not mine.”

## What An Empty Pair Is For

“What is an empty pair for,” he said, from off the stone and not close to her.

And she lay on the stone with her left hand flat on her own record and found that she had an answer, and that the answer was not a rule, and that she had built it herself out of what she has seen that hand do.

“A pair of cuts is where a figure goes when I am put up that stair in an armful. That is what it is for. That is the whole of what it has ever been for in my life, and I have been in this room two hundred and seventy days and I have watched four of them get carried past that shelf and I have never once been inside one.”

“And a pair with nothing in it is a pair that was cut and not written in. And I have known about those twelve since the seventh of that month and I have not said them out loud to anybody until tonight, because I have never had anybody in this room who was going to ask.”

“You have just said them out loud to me.”

“I have just said them out loud to you, which is the first of those two things happening and not the second.”

“What is the second.”

“Working out what they are.”

And then she said the whole of it, on her back, on the stone, with her arm open at her side and the lamp standing up off the floor.

She said, “There are two things it can be. It can be that the wood is cut for every pair a row could ever have and the hand writes in one when there is something to write, and that is the whole of it. Or it can be that a pair gets cut when something is going to go in it, and then it does not go, and those are twelve times that something was going to happen to me in this room and did not.”

She said, “I have counted them. I have counted them on the second of every month for two hundred and seventy days by putting two fingers on the edges of them. I have never worked out which of those two is true and I have never written either of them anywhere, and I am saying them to you on a stone floor in oil light because I have run out of room to keep a thing in on my own.”

## She Does Not Lift It

And he said, “Then take it out.”

She said, “No.”

He said, “It is in the dark.”

She said, “It came up that stair in an armful on the first of the month beginning two thousand two hundred and ninety-four and it has lain on that stone every day since, and that is where the room put it, and I am not taking it off the floor tonight.”

He said, “That is not an answer.”

She said, “It is the whole of the answer I have got and I am going to give you the rest of it so that it is not the whole.”

And she got herself out from under that table on her hands and her knees and sat with her back against a leg of it and the lamp on the stone in front of her, and that took her about as long as the counting had taken him.

She said, “I do not lift it because every figure on that wood is either one I put there or one the hand at the far end of this table put there, and I cannot tell which is which, and neither can anybody in this county, and a strip of pale wood with the grain running up is not a thing that two pieces of can be told apart by.”

She said, “And I have looked at four boards go up that stair with my price on them and I have not been able to see which one was which, and the last time a piece of wood of mine left this floor it went in an armful and it came back in an armful and I stood in this room and could not tell whether it had gone.”

She said, “And I am not spending oil on finding out which of two things is true about a strip of wood. I have got a stage four days past the first of that month and a boy of sixteen on a stair, and that wood stays where it is.”

She said, “Tonight. And I have said the word tonight out loud, because I have learned what happens when I decide a thing on a stone floor at the seventh hour without saying that there is a time on it.”

## Not Letters

“It is not letters,” he said, before he could have said anything about the word tonight.

She said, “No.”

He said, “There is not one letter on it.”

She said, “I know there is not one letter on it. I put two of those figures there myself and I know what I put there and it was not a letter.”

He said, “Then how do you know what is on it.”

And she stopped.

She stayed where she was, with her back against that leg of the table and the lamp standing on the stone in front of her, and the light went a hand’s width along the strip of pale wood and stopped there, and the rest of that room was twenty-two paces of dark.

She said, “I do not know how I know what is on it.”

She said, “I have said that wood out loud in this room every day since the seventh of the month beginning two thousand five hundred and thirty-four, in sentences, in the ordinary voice, standing at the near end of that table, and I have never once seen a cut on it, and tonight a boy of sixteen has been on a stone floor with a lamp and his own fingertip and he has read it off the wood and not out of my mouth, and he has got all of it right.”

She said, “And it is not letters. On the wood it is cuts. And a thing made of cuts can be read by somebody who cannot read, and I have been talking to a room in sentences for nineteen years and the room has not once written one of them down.”

And then he asked her the last question of that night, standing up off the stone with lamp oil on two fingers.

He said, “Who wrote it.”

She said, “Two hands.”

He said, “That is not an answer either.”

She said, “One of them wrote the head of it and the first pair under the head, and one of them wrote a five in the second pair, and the hand at the far end of this table has been standing at this table for four hundred years and I have never once been in light at that end of it, and I do not know what that hand looks like.”

She said, “I have described that table to you out loud in the dark and I have told you what is under it and what is on it and what is in it, and I have done all of it in the dark, for two hundred and seventy days, and I have never once put a lamp on the far end of it.”

She said, “That is the whole of what I can tell you about who wrote it, and it is the last thing I have got, and I am not going to guess in front of you.”
