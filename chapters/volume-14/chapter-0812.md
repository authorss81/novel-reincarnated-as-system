# Away From Her Hearing

On the sixth of the month beginning two thousand six hundred and eighty-four there is a room in here and he is in it.

There is no floor in this place and there is nothing to put a hand on, and he checks both of those before he lets anything arrive, because he has stood in rooms like this one before and he has never once been in a hurry about one.

The lamp in the cage at the far end of that long table is out and has been out a hundred and fifty-five days, and the wall behind it is the wall, and the line cut in the stone of that wall is a line cut in a stone wall and the only light in this building that ever came down to it went out on the first of a month a hundred and fifty-five days ago.

The only light is an oil lamp standing on the top of the table a foot in from the near end. The light goes a hand’s width along the wood and stops there. Past that it is twenty-two paces of dark, and the light has no business going further, and he does not know why and does not spend a count of a hundred on it.

## What Is In The Room

There is a bar nine feet nine flat against the wall on the left with a hold-iron welded at each end, and it is the only thing in here that came up that stair on a person’s shoulder.

There is a bundle of papers under the far end of the table corded round twice and not knotted, and nothing has moved it.

And at the near end of the top of the table there are two pieces of wood lying side by side and not touching.

One of them is pale and about a foot long and there are figures cut into it in rows, four pairs of them filled and eight pairs of them empty, and a price at the head of it. The other one is the same length and the same width and there is a name on it in a hand that is not the hand at the far end of the table, and one pair of worn cuts under the name with nothing in them.

He stands and looks at that name for about a count of a hundred and forty.

Then he stops for about a count of a hundred at the bar, because a thing nine feet nine lying flat against a wall for four hundred years is a thing he wants to be able to say he has looked at, and then he goes and stands in front of the far side of that table with nothing in his hand, and there is nothing to see.

## Twenty-Two Paces

He walks twenty-two paces along the near side of that table with his left hand on the leg of it, because there is a flag near the end of it that moves.

He is not carrying the lamp. He wants to see what is at the far end and he cannot see anything at all at the far end, so he is not carrying it, and that is a thing he decides in four seconds and does not improve on.

At the far end he stops about four feet short and stands there in the dark and there is nothing in front of him that he can put a hand on.

He knows the length of that table. He has known it since the fifth of the month beginning two thousand four hundred and forty-four, when he stood in a room like this one with the cage lamp lit and did not get into anything.

He goes back for the lamp.

He picks it up off the top of the table where it is standing a foot in from the near end, and it is heavier than it looks and there is oil in it and the wick is trimmed short, and it belongs to a person who is not in the room.

He carries it twenty-two paces with it in his right hand, and at about the eleventh pace the light comes down onto a stack of forty boards face up under the middle of the table, and he stops there.

There are rows of figures on them. Rows of figures in a small hand with three fingers and a thumb in it, running the length of the wood, going back a long way, and they are the figures that are cut into the pale strip at the near end of this table.

There is nothing under any of them. Not one row of anything, in any hand, anywhere along any of those boards, and he looks at the middle of that stack for about a count of ninety because a stack of forty boards with figures on the tops of them is a thing a person looks at the middle of, and there is nothing there.

He does not work that out either. He goes on down the table, which is the way he has put down about four hundred things in nineteen years.

He puts the lamp down on the top of the table about four feet short of the far end.

The light comes down flat onto the wood.

## What He Has Been Told

And he looks at it from about four feet back, standing, the way a person stands when they are looking at a thing they have been told about and have never been in front of.

A stretch of the far side of the table about the length of a forearm, paler than the rest of the wood, smoother, with a shape worn down into it that no tool made.

He has been told about it. He was told about it in a dark room on the seventh of the month beginning two thousand five hundred and thirty-four by a woman standing four feet off the other end of the same table, and what she told him was that it is not a chair, and that it is addressed, and that it is the right size for somebody who is not here, and that she cannot tell him what it is for.

That is all she told him. He has had it for a hundred and forty-nine days and he has been in this room and looked down that table at that end of it once, from a doorway, at a distance, on the day she told him, and that is the whole of the standing he has got.

He stands in front of it now.

He does not put his hand on it and he does not put his weight on it and he does not sit in it and he does not get into it, and every one of those four things is a decision and he makes all four of them on purpose.

And then he stops making decisions, because what is left is not a decision any more.

## What The Sentence Has To Be

He knows about the tense of a thing that is said and does not come back, because he has been in a room with a woman in it for a hundred and forty-nine days and has heard her do it most mornings of it, and there is a boy who brings the lamp up eight hundred and eighty steps and the boy was not here on the first of them.

Every figure she has ever said out loud at that table has been said in the present tense. It is a rule and it is older than him. A count said in the past tense is a count about a day that has gone and a room that copies cannot tell the difference between a day that has gone and a day that has not, and she will not have anything come back at her that she cannot date.

So the sentence has to be in the present tense or it is nothing at all.

He works that out in about four seconds and it is the only thing about the sentence that takes any working out.

He also works out that not one person in this room has asked him for it, that not one person in this room is owed anything by his saying it, that nothing in this room will be able to hold him to it and nothing in this room will be able to release him from it, and that a sentence with all four of those properties has been said, on purpose, in the present tense, to a piece of wood.

And that is what a keeper is, from the inside.

## The One Sentence

A woman has been standing four feet off the near end of that table for three hundred and eighty-eight days, saying every figure she has got out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice, to a room.

The boards under the middle of that table are the sound of it.

And there is nobody in this room to hear what they sound like, and there has not been anybody in nineteen years, and the wood goes on making the noise either way, which is the whole of what a room does with nineteen years of a woman talking to it.

He looks at the worn place for a count of a hundred and forty.

Then he says it, out loud, standing, in the light off a lamp that belongs to somebody else, to a piece of wood about the length of his own forearm:

“I will keep what she says out loud in this room.”

## Back Down The Table

That is all he says.

It is in the present tense and it is not a promise to anybody and there is no one in this room to hear it, and it has been said to a piece of furniture, and a piece of furniture does not answer and does not need to.

He stands there after it for about a count of a hundred and forty and he does not take any of it back, which is the part he would have thought was the hard one nine years ago and is not.

He picks the lamp up off the top of the table and carries it back twenty-two paces on the near side with his left hand on the leg, over the flag that moves, and puts it down on the wood a foot in from the near end where he found it.

The light goes a hand’s width along the table and stops.

And there are two things he does not do on the way back, and he notices both of them, and he does them on purpose.

He does not go and stand in front of it a second time to be sure the sentence took, because a sentence that has to be checked a second time is not a sentence.

And he does not go and stand at the near end of that table, four feet off, where a woman stands every morning, to see what it looks like from there, because that is the one place in this room that is hers and he has had no claim to it this morning and he was not going to make one by standing in it.

He leaves the lamp where he found it and the room goes on being the room.

And that is what a yes said to a chair is: a sentence in the present tense, in a room with no one in it, made by a person who cannot hear it, to a thing that has been empty for four hundred years and has just been told in words that it is not going to be left that way.
