# A Cup And A Half

He came up on the twelfth of the month beginning two thousand five hundred and thirty-four at the seventh hour and put the lamp down on the near end of that table, and she moved it two inches towards her, because the light of it was running along the edge of the wood and not onto the wood, and that was the first thing anybody had done to that table in two hundred and forty days that was not taking something off it.

He said, “You can put it back.”

She said, “I am not putting it back. Two inches in.”

## His Count And Hers

She had been working out how to start since the fourth hour. She had four questions ready and she got through none of them, because he put the lamp down and then turned round and looked at the table the way a person looks at a table, and she waited for him to finish, and he did not take long.

She said, “How much is in it.”

He said, “It was a cup and a half when I filled it.”

She said, “And it is not a cup and a half now.”

He said, “No.”

She said, “That is not an answer and you know it is not an answer, because you have been up here once before with a cup and a half in it and it is the same lamp.”

He said, “It is not the same lamp, it is the same cup, and I do not know what is in the cup, and if I tell you what is in the cup then in about a month I am going to have to remember which of us we told and that is two things to remember instead of one.”

She said, “You count.”

He said, “I count lamps.”

She said, “I count days.”

He said, “Then there is no trouble with that.”

She said, “There is no trouble with that until there is.”

And then she asked him the other thing, the one she had no right to ask and asked anyway, which is how she has got everything she has ever got.

She said, “How much is in the other two.”

He said, “That is not a figure you are getting.”

She said, “I am not asking for a figure. I am asking whether you know.”

He said, “I know there are three lamps and I know which of them is lit and I know which of them is standing with the wick turned down. That is the whole of what I carry between here and there, and if I carried a number as well I would be carrying something four miles down a mountain that does not fit on either of those floors.”

She said, “All right.”

He said, “You are angry about it.”

She said, “I am not angry about it. I am angry about having asked, and I am going to stop asking in about four seconds, and the four seconds are up.”

And then he said the number, because she had asked for it without asking, which is the only way she has ever got anything out of anybody in nineteen years.

He said, “I have carried one up five times since the seventh and I have carried one down five times, and three of the lamps have not been in this room once, and it is the fifth of the seventh that is here and it is not the one I thought it was going to be.”

She said, “Five.”

He said, “Five.”

She said, “And the fifth of the month beginning two thousand five hundred and thirty-four is the eighth, and the seventh is the day you came up the first time and the two in between are the nights you were not here, and that is five days and not five nights, and I do not know which of the two of us is counting better.”

He said, “Days.”

She said, “I know. It is a figure and not a count of anything. That came out wrong and I am leaving it.”

## The Day She Named

And then she gave him the date, which she had been carrying for four days and had not said out loud to anything, not once, not even standing in the dark at the near end of that table where she says everything else.

She said it the way she says a figure into a row, standing up, in the ordinary voice, with nothing before it and nothing after it:

“The first of the month beginning two thousand five hundred and ninety-four.”

And she did the subtraction out loud, because the fourth of the six terms is that a figure gets said before it is anything else, and because she has not got a better place to keep a number.

She said, “Two thousand five hundred and ninety-four less two thousand five hundred and forty-five is forty-nine days, and the forty-ninth day from tonight is the first of that month.”

He said, “Why that one.”

She said, “Because the light in that cage comes down two of my own hand a day and fifty-eight of my own hand is from the top of it down to the cut in the stone, and fifty-eight divided by two is twenty-nine, and twenty-nine days from the second of a month is the first of the next one. That is the whole of the arithmetic and I have given you that arithmetic once and I am not going to give it to you again.”

He said, “And the last time it came down.”

She said, “The first of the month beginning two thousand five hundred and thirty-four, at the fourth hour, and it did not stop on the cut.”

He said, “Because there was nobody in the cage.”

She said, “Because I did not put one in. And I have not put one in since, and it is not coming down to that cut again until somebody does, and there is not one thing in this building that goes into a lamp in a cage.”

And then she told him the rest of it, and this is the part of the date that is hers and not the lamp's.

She said, “And on the first of a month a man comes up that stair at the second hour with about forty boards under one arm and a bundle of papers in a cloth under the other, and he puts them down on that table, and hands go along the wood at about four to a minute, and he goes back out and he does not look at anything. You have watched that happen twice and you counted them going on and you were right about the number of minutes.”

She said, “And on the first of the month beginning two thousand five hundred and thirty-four I watched it too, and there was light, and I told him to take them back down, and he took them back down eight hundred and eighty steps, and it is the only time in four hundred years that a bundle has gone back down that stair the way it came.”

She said, “And I have never seen that room worked with nothing in it. Not once, in two hundred and forty days. It is the one day out of thirty it does anything, and there has been no light in here since the first, and on the first of that month there is going to be forty boards put down on that table in front of one lamp, and that lamp is yours.”

## Three Lamps In Order

He said, “You have not asked me what it costs.”

She said, “I have been telling you what it costs since the seventh of this month and you have not been listening, because you came up here to find out why a lamp is lit.”

He said, “Then what is left.”

She said, “I do not know and I am not going to work it out, and that is not me being proud. That is me telling you that the only figure about that oil that is true is yours and I have got nothing to add to it.”

She said, “You have three lamps and they go in order. I am saying that out loud now because I do not let a thing stand in this room that has not been put into words where it exists, and one of those three is going to be burning in this room on the first of the month beginning two thousand five hundred and ninety-four.”

He said, “Which one.”

She said, “That is the part I do not know, and neither do you, and it is the last thing about the oil that either of us is going to be able to say out loud.”

## Whose Count It Is

And then she did the thing she had come up the day for, which was the sixth sentence, and she said it standing at the near end of that table with the lamp two inches in front of her and her right arm open at her side where it has been open for two hundred and forty-seven days.

She said, “I am not going to keep your count.”

He said, “Why not.”

She said, “Because you count lamps and I count days, and if I had your count in my head then I would have to check it against mine, and I do not know which of us is right, and I am nineteen years old and I have never in my life had to hold two counts of the same thing.”

He said, “You have one now.”

She said, “Yes. That is the reason I am handing it to you and not keeping it, and I have thought about that sentence for two days and I have not found a better one.”

He said, “All right. It is mine.”

She said, “And I will not ask you for it and I will not correct it and if it is wrong on the first of the month beginning two thousand five hundred and ninety-four then it is wrong and I will know it at the time and not before.”

He said, “You will know it at the time.”

She said, “At the time, or never. There is no third one.”

And he took the lamp off the table and stood with it, and she stood four feet off the near end of the table in the dark behind him, and she did the last figure of the night out loud to a room that had one lamp in it for the first time in eight days.

She said, “Forty-nine days is forty-nine days and I have counted forty-nine days, and a lamp is a lamp and he has counted lamps, and the two of us are not going to add them together.”

And she said the last of it to herself, in the ordinary voice, standing, with nobody left in the room but the one at the far end of the table who had not looked up once in two hundred and forty-four days.

She said, “One of those two counts is going to be short on the first of the month beginning two thousand five hundred and ninety-four, and I have given mine away and I am not going to know whose it was.”
