# The Lamp Stays

She decided on the seventh of the month beginning two thousand five hundred and ninety-four at about the seventh hour that the lamp was going to stay on that table, and she decided it out loud in eleven seconds, and it took her the rest of the morning to say what it cost.

## The Oil He Carried Up

He said, “I have to take a lamp down.”

She said, “I know you have.”

He said, “I have three and I have carried one up and there are two on the floor I work on and there is not one oil in this building, and if I leave this one on that table then I am carrying an empty lamp down eight hundred and eighty steps tonight.”

She said, “Yes.”

He said, “You are not going to ask me for a fourth.”

She said, “There is not a fourth. There has not been a fourth in nine years and there is not one on this mountain and you have told me that twice and I have not asked you for it once.”

He said, “Then what.”

And she said it out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice, at the near end of that table with the lamp burning a hand’s width along the wood.

She said, “Empty it into this one.”

## The Lamp That Goes Down

He said, “There is one lamp on that table.”

She said, “There is one lamp on this table and about a cup and a half was in it when you filled it, and there is some of it left, and there is one on that.”

She said, “Put what is left in this one and take that one down, and it goes down the stair empty in your hand, and there is no oil in it and there is not one drop in it when you set it on that floor.”

He said, “That is you taking my oil.”

She said, “That is me taking your oil.”

He said, “Say it as though I am standing here.”

And she said it again, and it was worse the second time and she did it anyway, because the fourth of the six is that a thing gets said before it is a line anywhere and she has never once let a thing stand that had not been put into words where it exists.

She said, “You carried oil up eight hundred and eighty steps in your right hand at the seventh hour of the morning and about nine hours of it has been in this room and I am taking what is left of it and putting it in a lamp that is staying on this table, and you did not agree to that and I have not asked you and I am doing it in front of you.”

He said, “Why.”

She said, “Because I am nineteen years old and I have been in this room two hundred and ninety-nine days and the dark in it has been mine every night of them, and I am tired of it in a way I did not know I was tired of it until the sixth of this month.”

She said, “That is the whole reason. I am not going to dress it up as arithmetic, because it is not arithmetic, and you have had an arithmetic out of me almost every night since the twelfth of the month beginning two thousand five hundred and thirty-four and this one is not one of them.”

He said, “All right.”

## Trimming The Wick

And she did it herself, which she had not expected to do.

She took hold of the wick with two fingers of her right hand, three of them and a thumb, on an arm that is open at the elbow and has been since the first of the month beginning two thousand two hundred and ninety-four, and she pulled it up a little and she took about a quarter of an inch of it out between her fingers and she put the piece down on the top of that table beside the lamp.

She said, “That is the whole of the work. There is no tool in this room that does that and there is no tool in this room that does anything.”

And the book of eight leaves was against her fourth rib where it has been against her fourth rib, and five of those eight leaves are still blank, and she did not take it out of there to write on, because the sixth of the six terms is that a record is a record and not a hand, and she has not yet worked out which of those two things she is being at the near end of this table.

And then she took his lamp in her left hand and tipped it, and she poured what was in it into the one that was staying, slowly, with her right thumb on the rim, and the oil went across and did not go anywhere else, and there is nothing in that room to spill it on.

And when the lamp he had carried up was empty she set it down on the top of that table with the wick turned right down, and it weighed nothing, and she had been carrying it for three days.

She said, “That is yours and it goes down the stair in your hand. I have emptied it and I have not kept the vessel and I want that said out loud, because the next sensible thing for me to do would be to keep that as well and I am not going to.”

## What Cannot Be Un-Let

He said, “Then I am going down with an empty lamp.”

She said, “You are.”

He said, “And there is no oil in that building and none on this mountain.”

She said, “I know.”

He said, “So if this one goes out then there is no way to get another one up here.”

And she stood there and said the whole of it out loud, which took her about a hundred and forty counts, and it was the longest thing she has said in that room since the twenty-second of the month beginning two thousand five hundred and thirty-four.

She said, “That is the price and it is not the oil. The oil is only what it is made of.”

She said, “The price is that the light in this room from tonight is a light I cannot hold. I cannot be given a thing. There is not one road by which a thing gets handed to me and there has not been since I was nine years old, and I cannot go down four miles and buy a lamp, and there is nothing on this mountain, and there is not one instrument in this building that can make light out of anything that is not already burning.”

She said, “So the only thing that can keep that lamp lit is you coming up eight hundred and eighty steps with oil in your hand, and there is nothing in this room that ties you to it, and you are not entered and not sent and there is no line anywhere that says where you go in the evening.”

She said, “And if you stop coming up that stair then this room goes dark and it does not come back.”

He said, “You are describing a debt.”

She said, “I know what I am describing.”

He said, “I am not going to call it one.”

She said, “Then call it what you like, because there is not one word in this building that has any authority over what you do with an evening and I have said that out loud on the sixteenth of the month beginning two thousand five hundred and thirty-four and it has not been withdrawn.”

And then she said the part that was under all of it, and it came out in about four seconds.

She said, “And I cannot take it back.”

He said, “The lamp.”

She said, “The dark. That was mine. Every night of two hundred and ninety-nine days that room was a dark room that I had not had to share with anybody and that I had not had to owe, and I have given that up today in front of you for a lamp with about a cup of oil in it, and there is no version of tomorrow in which I stand at the near end of this table in the dark and it was my dark.”

She said, “And I would not have it back if it were offered.”

## The Worn Place

And at about the ninth hour he picked up the empty lamp and stood at the low door with it, and she went down the first four hundred and forty with her left hand on the rail after him because it is worse going down.

And at the fourth landing, with her hand flat on a rail that stops there, she said out loud, to the dark, at about the ninth hour of the seventh of the month beginning two thousand five hundred and ninety-four:

“Light in this room now comes up that stair and I am not going to ask him for it and I am not going to be able to say no to it if he brings it, and both of those are true and only one of them is mine.”

And then he went on down, and the sound of a lamp that had nothing in it went away and got smaller and stopped.

And she went back up the first four hundred and forty alone, and stood four feet off the near end of that table, and the lamp was standing on the wood about a foot in from the edge with the wick trimmed and the cup filled out of what he carried up and did not use.

She said out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice:

“There is a lamp on this table and it is going to be here when I get back from anywhere I go, and I did not put it here, and I did not ask for it, and it is not mine, and I cannot send it back, and that is the whole of what I have got.”

And she put her left hand flat on the top of that table about a foot in from the edge, on the place she had cleared on the twenty-second of that month, and she looked along the wood.

The light went a hand’s width and stopped.

And twenty-two paces from where she was standing, past the end of what the light could do, there was a place in the top of that table where the wood had been worn, and it had been worn by somebody sitting in it, and it is not a chair and it is not a seat and no instruction in that room ever put anybody in it, and she told a boy of sixteen all of that in the dark on the seventh of the month beginning two thousand five hundred and thirty-four and she could not tell him what it was for.

She said, out loud, to it:

“I still do not know what you are for, and I have had a lamp in this room for four days and I have been nearer to finding out than I have ever been, and I am not going to work it out tonight.”

She said, “And it is in the dark. It has been in the dark for as long as I have been in this room and a lamp standing at this end of that table does not change one thing about it, and I am going to have to have an opinion about it and I have not got one yet.”

And she stood there with the oil burning a hand’s width along the near end of that table and twenty-two paces of dark past it, and she did not go down either stair again that night, and the cage at the far end of that room was still out and had been out for sixty-six days.
