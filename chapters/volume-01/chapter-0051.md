# The Floor

The hall was fuller than it had been for the accounts, and every person in it turned when she walked onto the floor, and the turning made a sound like a hundred and forty feet of weather arriving at once.

She had thought about this for nine days. She had thought about it as a walk from the wall to the middle of a room, about forty paces, and she had not once allowed herself to think about what it would feel like to be looked at by everybody at the same time while doing the hardest thing she had ever attempted, because she did not have a model for it.

She had fought four things in the Verge in the dark with nobody watching and she had won, and she had once stood in a corridor with eleven men in it and been permitted to speak, and there was nothing in either of those that prepared her for a floor.

She was worse at it than she had expected and she was better at the other thing than she had expected, and those two facts turned out to be the same fact.

The middle of the floor was a square of plank about nine feet on a side, worn pale in the middle where forty years of somebody important's boots had gone round and round.

“Furnace crew,” said Xie Shuo, from a table at the edge of it, with the page in front of him. “The member may stand where she pleases. The hearing ends when the member says so, or at the failing of the light. Any witness may ask the clerk for the hour and for the count, and the clerk will read both.”

“Read the hour and the count now,” said somebody from the back.

“Ninth hour. The member is on the floor.”

She took off her coat.

Underneath she had a thin grey jacket and the string of the tablet between her breasts where it had sat against her skin for three months, and she did not think about that then and thought about it every day afterward. She put the coat on the bench at the wall, folded, because she was not a fool and neither was anybody in that room.

Then she walked forty paces of floor with the whole of it watching her do it, and she felt every one of them land on the back of her neck like a hand, and she got to the middle, and the light was flat pewter with no shadow in it at all, and the ash was coming down through the high windows in a slant that did not move and had not moved since the fourth morning.

## Reading the Room

“Who has decided something,” she said, quietly, in the voice she used for tallies.

“You want the count or the names.”

“The count.”

He read it. It took him about two seconds and it was the single most useful thing he had ever done for her, and she stood in the middle of a floor and received it.

“Nine hundred and fifty-one are curious,” he said. “Nine are frightened, which is ordinary, and all nine of them are at the back. Four have decided something. Two of the four have decided it about you and they are not the ones you would pick out, and one of those two is the Speaker, and he has decided to let you finish.”

“And the fourth.”

“The fourth is the clerk,” he said, “and he has decided to do his job, which is the most frightening thing anybody in that room has decided to do.”

“All right.”

“I want you to know that I cannot touch any of it,” he said. “Not one piece of the floor, not the light, not the weather. If it starts to go wrong in a way that is not you, you will be entirely alone on a plank floor in front of a hall.”

“I know,” said Ning Yan. “Do it anyway.”

## The First Form

She did not decide to start with the Nine-Step. Her body did it.

That was the thing that had happened on the terrace on the fourth morning past and had happened to her perhaps a dozen times since, and she had never told a living soul about it, including the thing in her head, and she was certainly not going to explain it in the middle of a floor in front of a hall.

She did not remember learning it. That was the whole of what she knew about it: that it had been in her legs since she was small enough to be carried, that it came off the back of a book her mother had owned about laundry, and that at some point in the last year the book had stopped mattering and the thing itself had stayed, and that when she went at a problem with it now it went whether or not she had decided to include it.

She started the first step and her left foot was already in the fourth.

“Do that again,” he said.

“No.”

“You have never moved like that before. Not once, in nine weeks, not in training, not in the ash-flats.”

“I do it every day.”

“You do not do it like that every day.”

He was quiet for a moment.

“Ning Yan.”

“Not here,” said Ning Yan, out loud, in the middle of a floor, in front of the entire sect, and she did not soften it or explain it afterwards, because if she had explained it she would have had to say where it came from, and where it came from was a laundry manual that a laundry-hand had owned, and that was not a thing a hall could be allowed to have in it.

He let it go.

She went round the worn square twice, and the second time she felt something in her legs that she had not felt in three months, and it was not strength and it was not stage.

It was *timing*.

She had been working on this for nine days the way a person works on a lock with cold hands. Condensing was the second realm and the first thing it did was take the air that a Body-Forging body breathed in and spent, and let out, and spent, forty thousand times a day, and turn it into a thing that a person owned.

Her chest knew how to do that. Her chest had been doing it since she was born, and the problem had never been her chest. The problem was the crack, which was a hole through the centre of the thing that was supposed to hold it, and everything she had poured through that hole in three months had gone out sideways into a pattern instead of staying in a place.

Mei Jiu's cloth came out from under her jacket and she put her hand against her own sternum and did the thing a woman in Beihe had taught a woman in Qinghe who had taught a fourth-rank inner disciple who had given it away for nothing on a landing in the dark.

Narrow the gate.

Not close the crack. Narrow the gate, so that the crack became a rim and a rim was still a rim, and a rim could hold.

It worked.

She felt it go in like a hand into a glove and the whole of her chest went quiet for the first time since the sixth day past, and out in the hall somebody made a small sound, and she did not have any attention left for it.

“That is not yours,” he said.

“No.”

“You cannot describe it. You have four words for it and three of them are mine.”

“It is not mine and it is not yours either and I am going to use it on this floor, and if a fourth-rank inner disciple comes out of the back of that gallery in about a minute I am going to look at her while I use it, so that the room knows where to send the bill.”

“You would not lose anything by letting them think it was yours.”

“I would lose the only thing that is hers,” said Ning Yan.

## The Fight

It took her four forms to get to the wall, and it took the wall two more.

The wall was not a wall. That was the thing that everyone in that hall believed and that she had stopped believing on a terrace step at the fourth hour of the fourth night past. There was no wall. There was a body at the top of what it could hold and the empty inside it, and the only way through was to let the top of it go before it took anything, in that order, on purpose, and she had done it four times and it had cost her four months of everything the first time.

She let go.

It went out of her through the crack and down the length of the pattern and out through her back into the air of a hall with a roof on it, and she felt her own shape leave, and the hollow came up from her sternum to her navel, and the whole of that was the third clause of a sentence she had read out of four hundred volumes in a fair hand and never understood until a man in her head showed her.

Then she took.

Her right hand went at the sixth form.

It went the way it had been going for three days, without warning, the two fingers first and then the whole of it, and she felt her own arm become an arm on somebody else's body while the pattern in her chest tried to close on a shape and hold it. There was no pain. She had always known there would not be pain.

She did not stop. She put the shape in with the left hand on her own ribs and the right one hanging, and she went round the pale square of the plank on her heels while the hall watched a girl carry an arm, and the room began to make the noise a room makes when it cannot decide whether what it is looking at is anything.

And then the fear stopped.

It did not go away. It was still there, in the front of her, and she could feel the size of it, and she could feel exactly how much attention the whole of that room was paying to a plank floor. But somewhere in the middle of the sixth form it stopped being the whole of what was happening to her, because something else had taken over the front of her chest and that thing did not care about the room at all.

It was the only thing she had ever been good at.

For nine days she had been trying to do this in cold air on a flat above the yard with nobody counting. Here she could feel the whole of them coming off the floor at her, and every one of them was afraid, and the fear of a room like that is a substance, and she took it the way you take a handle.

Her chest came in on itself like a fist.

## The Hook

She had it. She had the shape and she had the gate and she had the order of it, and the only thing between her and Condensing stage one was that Condensing stage one was bigger than anything she had ever held and she was holding it in a body that had been at stage four nine weeks ago.

“Two more,” she said, aloud. “That is all. Two more.”

And the air of the Assembly Hall fell out of the room.

Not a wind. Every loose thing in the hall came off its surface at the same instant — sixty sheets of paper off the Speaker's table, the ash off the floor, the cloth off every shoulder in the room — and hung there, and went up.

The ash stopped falling sideways. It went down straight, in every part of the room at once, like rain through a roof that is not there, and the temperature dropped nine degrees in the space of four seconds, and the high windows, which had three hundred years of flat grey light in them, went black.
