# Withheld

The anteroom was where the Hall kept people who were going to be let in later, and it had four benches and a clerk's table and a window that gave on the north side, and by the fourth hour of the third day the floor of it was the colour of the lye trough at the wash house.

That was not the ash. That was nine days of it. Boots came in from the terrace with a grey load on them and went out with less, and nobody thought about it, and by the fourth hour the middle of the anteroom floor was a grey that had been trodden into a paste, and the window light came through it and lay on the benches in a bar the colour of a bad tooth.

She came in at the second hour and they made her wait until the fourth, and the waiting was the point of it, and everyone in that corridor knew that, and nobody said so.

Lu Kang sent a girl of about nineteen to tell her that three men would see her when they had finished, and the girl said it standing in the doorway with her hands behind her back and did not come in, and Ning Yan sat on the second bench with her hands on her knees and did not look at her, because looking at her would have been a thing she had to apologise for later to somebody.

## The Ask

“Two things,” she said. “I will sign. I will sign in front of witnesses, in this room or the room next to it, and there will be a clerk and I will dictate the whole of it aloud first so that every word of it is entered exactly as spoken.”

Lu Kang nodded. “And?”

“And I will not give you an hour.”

Deng Chao, who had the cough, said, “You have until the fourth hour of the fourth day.”

“The fourth day is tomorrow. The trial is tomorrow at the ninth hour.”

“It is.”

“So the fourth hour of tomorrow is five hours before I stand on that floor.”

“It is,” said Lu Kang.

“No,” said Ning Yan. “I am not signing five hours before. I will sign after. I will sign an hour after I come off that floor, in this room, with witnesses, and if I do not come off that floor then you will never have my signature at all, and you can enter that as a finding and it will be true.”

She had it in her before she came in the room. She had worked it out on the stair at two in the morning with a cough going and the compression cloth against her ribs, and she had counted the hours in it twice, and it had held.

“No,” said Lu Kang.

“Why not.”

He put his thumb on the table. He did it the way a man does a thing he has already done many times.

“Because of what comes out of you at the ninth hour.”

“That is what I said.”

“It is half of what I said,” Lu Kang said, and he was not unkind about it, which was worse. “You have told me you will sign afterwards. Very well. Then think about what afterwards *is*.”

“At the ninth hour there is a girl on a floor and no paper anywhere in that building with your name on it. Whatever comes out of her at the ninth hour is then the property of this sect, because the sect sealed her and the sect set the trial and the sect held the floor. We get it. We hold it. There is nothing we can do about that and there is nothing you can do about it either, because you are in a room with nine hundred witnesses and you will be Condensing stage one and the advancement will have happened in Qinghe and there will not be one piece of paper in nine counties that says otherwise.”

“Now suppose instead that at the ninth hour there is a sponsorship on a table in front of you, witnessed, read aloud, entered, sealed. Then what comes out of you belongs to the men who put you on that floor. That is the entire difference between the fourth hour and the ninth. I am not being cruel to you. I am telling you what the hour is *for*.”

She stood in the middle of the anteroom with her hands at her sides and let that go all the way in, because it was true, and because true was the thing she had come here for.

“Your term holds until the fourth hour of tomorrow,” Lu Kang said. “After that the account of Ning Bo stands and the clause is dead. You may go.”

She did not go.

## The Sitting

They let her sit for eight hours because the rule said a member might wait in the anteroom and no rule said for how long, and because none of the three of them wanted to be the man who had put her out into the corridor in front of clerks.

She sat on the second bench with her coat on and the log in the lining and the tablet against her skin, and she let the eight hours happen.

The ash came in on the boots of forty or fifty people and none of it went out, and the light through the north window changed four times, and at some point in the fifth hour it began to rain, thinly, and the rain came in as grey and settled on the floor paste and made it darker.

There was a bench of outer disciples near the door who came in out of it at intervals and looked at her and looked away. A furnace-crew shift came through at the seventh hour and stopped as one body when they saw her and then went on, and one of them put a cup of water on the bench beside her without looking at her and without a word, and it was still warm.

The cough came at the fourth hour, in the middle of the room, with forty people in it, and she managed it in the way she had practised on the landing: four short pulls and then a hold, with her face turned a quarter turn toward the wall and her right hand flat on her knee, and it took eleven seconds and it made a noise and eleven people heard it and two of the clerks wrote something down.

That was the price of the ash and it was not only a cough. A girl who coughs in an anteroom is a girl with wet lungs. A girl with wet lungs is a body that will not survive Condensing. Nine hundred people will do that arithmetic by the ninth hour tomorrow without being asked to.

“You should go up and lie down,” he said.

“No.”

“You have nine hours and you are going to spend them sitting on a bench in a cold room coughing.”

“Yes.”

“Why.”

“Because at the fourth hour of tomorrow I am going to say no to them,” Ning Yan said, “and I would like to have been in the room for eight hours first, so that when I say it nobody can say I said it quickly.”

## The Offer Dies

The clerk came at the last hour of the day.

He was not the servant-rank man from the corridor. He was one of the Hall's own table, a thin man of about fifty with a bad seal hand, and he came and stood in front of her and held the sheet out at arm's length the way you hold a thing out to a dog.

“Furnace crew. The term is at an end. The account of Ning Bo is not struck and the entry made yesterday is struck out of the schedule and stands void. You are to leave the anteroom.”

She looked at the sheet. The four lines she had asked for were on the second page in a fair even hand, and beside them there was a line through the whole page in the same ink.

“Who struck it out.”

“I did,” said the clerk. “Enter it that way if you want it entered. I struck out the schedule of nine accounts as well, and I was told to.”

Nine households. Somebody in that building had pulled the rug back after one day and put it down again for them.

“Was the entry of my brother's clause struck out too.”

“No,” said the clerk. “The entry stands in the minute book and cannot be taken out, because a minute book is not a schedule. It says that on the second day, at the third hour, three members entered a undertaking touching the record of Ning Wu, and that it was asked for and not offered. That is all it says. It does not say reconciled. It does not say anything at all until somebody signs.”

He put the sheet back under his arm.

“Mind the step. The floor is greasy with the rain.”

She sat on the bench for another hour after the room emptied.

## It Comes Back

It came back at the second hour of the night.

The same servant-rank man, at the west door, with his cuffs turned back and his sleeves damp at the elbow, and he was standing in the rain under the eave and he did not come in either.

“Furnace crew.”

She got up.

“Three men are not here. I am carrying this and I did not write it and I am not told what is on it beyond what you will hear from me.”

“Say it.”

“Eleven days ago, on the ninth day past, they sent me down to the terrace steps with a document.” He said it in the flat voice of a man who had practised a sentence all the way down the mountain. “I told you that I did not write it and that I only carried it. I have thought about that on every day since and I have decided it was the only useful thing I did all winter, and I am telling you so that you know what I am when I tell you what this is.”

She waited.

“The account of Ning Bo is struck whole on the day of signature. Your brother's record is entered in the register as reconciled, with the hour and my master's name on it. Foundation in two years on their writ. Bed and stipend. The tablet is to be surrendered within the week and it is to be entered as recovered.”

“That is the whole of it.”

“No,” said the servant-rank man. “That is everything except the thing I have not been told the wording of, which is a clause of one line at the bottom, and I am not going to guess at it for you.”

She took the sheet.

It was dry. It had been in a drawer, and it had a fold in it down the middle that the other one had not had, and the second page was blank where the four lines of the entry had been, and the first page had a new line at the bottom in a different ink from the rest of it.

There was no hour on it anywhere. No term. No deadline. No line struck through anything.

“When does it die.”

“It does not die,” said the servant-rank man. “It is not in the minute book any more, so no clerk in that building can strike it, and no man in that building can say when it ends. It sits in a drawer until somebody signs it or until a piece of paper gets wet.”

“And they know that.”

“They know that,” he said. “They worked it out at about the ninth hour tonight, all three of them, in about six minutes, and then they had to decide whether to send it back in the rain on the strength of six minutes, and they decided, and they are not proud of the deciding.”

He turned to go and then stopped.

“Furnace crew. I am not a good man and I have never once in my life refused to carry a thing.”

“I know,” said Ning Yan. “That is why you are still walking.”

He went down the steps in the rain.

## What She Had Done

She got back to the quarters at the fourth hour and did not sleep, and she did not train, and she did not go up onto the flats, and she sat on the edge of the bunk with the sheet in her hands for most of two hours before she worked out what had happened in the last three days.

She had asked for mercy. Not consciously, and not in words, but there had been a shape to it: a girl in a corridor being given the one thing she wanted by people who owed her nothing and did it kindly. She had refused that shape twice.

This was not that shape.

Nobody in that room had been kind to her in the anteroom. The first man had explained, patiently and without heat, exactly why the hour he was asking for was the hour he needed. The second had been made to stand in a corridor and repeat a line. The third had walked six miles up a wet mountain in the dark to hand her a piece of paper and had told her, unprompted, that he was not a good man.

They had come back with worse terms for themselves than the ones she had refused.

She had not asked for that. She had decided it, on a bench, over eight hours, by not leaving a room — and she had gone into that corridor on the morning of the second day asking to be forgiven something, and she had come out of it at the fourth hour of the third night with a document that three men could not take back.

“Nothing on that sheet is a favour,” he said.

“No.”

“It is a thing they need, that you are able to give, at a time they have told you about.”

“Yes,” said Ning Yan. “And I am going to use it. That is the part I did not notice happening.”

“You noticed it at about the fourth hour of the third day and you have been carrying it for two.”

“No. I noticed it just now.”

“You noticed it on the bench,” he said. “You just got round to saying so.”

She put the sheet inside her coat, in the lining, on the wrong side from the log, because the log was evidence and this was a weapon and she had been keeping them apart for two months.

Then she sat with her hands on her knees and looked at the wall and noticed, with the part of her mind that never stopped counting, that she had not said please once in eight hours.

Not in the anteroom and not on the stair and not in the rain at the door. Eight hours with three men and a clerk and forty people and she had said *no* nine times, *understood* twice, and *I will sign*, and she had not said please one time, and the reason she had not was that she had not needed to.

She found that she could not remember the last time that had been true either.

The trial was in five hours. The roof of the Hall was forty feet up and she was going to stand under it in front of everybody the sect could put in a building and try to go through a wall with a hole in her, and she had made a document wait, and the document was dry, and the rain had stopped at about the first hour and the ash was coming down again now, thick, out of a sky with no stars in it.

She was not going to sleep. She had decided that at about the second hour and she had not changed her mind since, and there was nothing in the next five hours she wanted to be rested for.
