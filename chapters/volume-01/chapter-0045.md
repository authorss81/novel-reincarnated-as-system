# Nine Days

The nine days were the longest of her life and she spent most of them on her back on the ash-flats, which was not what she had planned.

[Nothing further is set against the ninth day. The region keeps counting it anyway.]

She went in with a list of four things, because a list was the only piece of technique she had left that had not been taken. She wrote it on a stave because her hand was shaking and she could not hold a brush.

One: the grip of the right hand at about nine minutes. Two: the breath count, which goes to eleven and then lies. Three: the leak, which is now a way and is also a hole. Four: the fever, which came up on the second day and did not go down.

She had gone in believing that stage nine was a floor and that she had to stand on it for three hours in front of nine hundred people. It was not a floor. It was a ceiling with a hole in it, and the hole was the only thing that was letting anything happen at all.

What she had done on the terrace step worked. That was the good part and it was also the problem: she could open herself on purpose now. She had taken the shape, emptied first, taken second, and got nine out of a body that was four with a crack through it, and she could do it again, and doing it again on the second day left her on the flats for an hour with her chest hollow and her legs full.

So she trained on the flats in the pattern with the leak sideways, and it held her at nine, and every hour she spent on the leak put a little more heat into a body that had no way of getting rid of it.

The fever came up on the second day and stayed. On the fourth she could hold the form for eleven minutes and a half and then her right hand stopped taking orders, and on the sixth she lost the sense of it in two fingers, and on the seventh she made herself do it anyway and the fingers came back about four seconds late, which was worse.

Du Hong came down to the flats twice and was turned away both times by a girl who did not stop moving. Mei Jiu came down once, at dusk on the fifth, and stood at the edge and watched her do four forms and did not offer a word, and went up. Ning Yan understood that as the closest thing to help Mei Jiu was capable of, and that both of them knew exactly what the other one was not saying.

Shen Xiu passed the flats on the sixth with his board under his arm and did not stop, and she was glad, because she could not have carried anybody that day.

## The Corridor

She was coming off the terrace steps at the seventh hour on the first day when the clerk came out of the side door with a document already folded in his hand.

He was about forty and servant-rank, which meant he carried other people's paper and had been servant-born himself, and he was so frightened that he held the folded sheet the way people hold plates of hot soup.

“Furnace crew,” he said, and could not get the rest of it out.

She stopped.

“I have to say this and then I have to go back in,” he said. “I did not write it. I carry it. Those are different things and I know which one you will believe and I know which one the Hall does, and I would like you to know which one I am.”

“Say it and go in.”

He said it. A servant-rank clerk from the Hall's own table had been sent down to the terrace steps to find a furnace-crew girl on her way to a meal, on the first of nine days, while the Elders were eating, which meant somebody upstairs had worked out exactly where she would be and exactly what time.

She took the sheet. He went, with his head down, at a speed that was not quite running.

She did not open it on the steps.

## The Document

The Hall's waiting room off the corridor had a bench, a brazier that was out, and a smell of old paper that she had known since the records room under the furnace and had not smelled since.

She sat down and opened it.

It was better than the last one. That was the first thing and it was the worst thing, because the last one had at least been open about itself.

Inner sponsorship. Full inner rank at first sitting, not at review. A bed and a stipend from the month of the hearing. Her family account written off in full on the day she signed, the six years of furnace column extinguished and the balance struck. Foundation within two years at the Hall's cost, on a writ.

And the Clay Tablet, surrendered to the Elder Hall in the same week, entered in the record as a kiln article recovered and returned to the seal.

And at the bottom, on the line where a document tells a person what it is, in a servant's hand because a servant wrote it and it was meant to look like it had come from her own side:

*I, Ning Yan, servant-rank of the furnace crew, do state that I have not entered sealed ground at the Broken Kiln or elsewhere, at any time, and that I have not carried, removed or borne any article of kiln clay.*

It was four lines long and every word in it was true except the only two that mattered.

She read it twice. She read the schedule of payments twice. She read the clause about the account being struck on the day of signature twice, because the day of signature was a day she had not chosen and it could be any day up to and including the day she stood on the floor in front of nine hundred people.

Then she did the thing she had come here to be able to do, which was that she did not destroy it.

She got the log out of the lining and turned to the last clean page and wrote down, in her own poor hand, in the wash code inside it: the date, in her mother's stain notation. The clerk, described. The corridor, the seventh hour, and the fact that he said out loud that he had not written it and had only carried it, which was the single piece of evidence in the whole document that a person had ever put anything down.

The terms, in order, word for word, because word for word was the part that could kill someone later.

The clause about the sealed ground and the kiln clay, transcribed exactly, in the clerk's hand, which was even and competent and had never trembled once, because whoever had drafted that line had believed it.

Then the fold-mark of the paper itself, the pin hole at the top left, and the fact that the sheet had been drafted somewhere with a different kind of ink than the fill.

She wrote it all out and it took forty minutes because she could not hold the brush for more than a minute at a time, and when she had finished she sat in the cold waiting room with a document worth a life in her hand and a log full of it against her ribs.

The first offer had said: stop asking about your brother.

The second offer said: sign that you never asked.

The second one was worse, and it was worse in a way she had not expected. The first one had been Qiu's, and Qiu had been telling her the truth about the offer for a month while she refused it, and a man who tells you the price is a man you can argue with.

This one came from underneath Qiu, out of the same building, from three elders who wanted his furnace and did not care whether she lived, and it had been drafted by somebody who believed the sentence about the sealed ground. Somebody in that building had sat down and written a false statement about a place she had stood in for forty minutes and had not believed themselves to be lying.

She put the document back in its fold. She did not tear it. She carried it down to the furnace floor and put it in the log whole, because a torn document is a rumour and a dated document is a witness.

And then she went up to the flats and did nine forms.

## The Eighth Day

The seal came on the eighth day, at the fourth hour, with three witnesses, because three witnesses was what the Hall's own finding required and not one more.

The seal-clerk was an inner disciple of about sixty who had been putting seals on paper for thirty-four years and who did the whole thing in ninety seconds without looking up.

She stood in the furnace room, not the Hall, because furnace crew were sealed on the furnace floor and she had not been moved up to any room with a better name. She had a clean coat on. She had the tablet against her skin. She had the log against her ribs. She had not slept in five days and she was standing up out of what was left of a fever and a thing that leaked.

The clerk read the finding. He read the words about the objection being the writ and he read them with the flat delivery of a man who has read a strange document and is going to do exactly what it says.

Then he put the seal down.

It was not a flourish. It was a wooden block with a furnace mark cut into the base, and it came down onto red paste on rice paper with a sound like a lid being shut, and it was the smallest thing that had ever happened to her.

“Ning Yan,” the clerk said, past tense, and then stopped.

He looked up. He looked at the ring of furnace crew standing along the wall who had come to watch because there was nothing else to do on the eighth day of the ninth, and at the shift leader, and at Du Hong by the stair with his face open.

“No servant-born has been sealed for Condensing in this sect for three hundred years,” he said. “Not since the fourth Elder Biyu, whose name is not on your sheet because it is not in the current book.”

Nobody in the furnace room said anything at all.

“The sheet is entered,” he said, and went away, because he had thirty-four years of practice at leaving a room before it becomes a thing.

She stood there with the red paste on the corner of a sheet she did not have to carry because the clerk took it, and she waited for it to feel like something.

It did not feel like anything. What she felt, standing in the furnace room with three witnesses and a four-hundred-year-old fact hanging in the air above her head, was the place where the seal had gone in. It sat on her like a collar that somebody else had put on her, in a building she was fighting, on an instrument drawn up by a man who did not know her, and it bound her to attempt Condensing within a year or be recorded as having refused.

She had spent two months wanting a document. She had gone into a sealed room on the chance of one. She had carried a book up a mountain to a dying clerk to get four hundred volumes of somebody else's weather.

She had wanted a door and what she had got was a door with somebody else's lock on it, and there was not one person in nine counties she could have swapped it with.

That night she went back to the terrace and did nine forms and did not sleep, and at fourth hour on the ninth day she was standing on the Assembly floor with nine hundred people in the room and a paper in her hand asking them to look at her on purpose.

Two men had offered to arrange her life in exchange for her giving it up. The first had been the man who fired her father and remembered four other names out of pure decency. The second had been a corridor and a document and a clause about a place she had actually stood in.

They had been the same offer. They had arrived in the same shape, in the same week, from the same building, and neither of them had cost the giver anything they would miss.

She had been given her life twice by two different people and it had come to her in an envelope both times, and she had signed nothing, and she had a seal she did not want and a hearing she had asked for, and the floor was nine hundred people and a weather event, and she had never once in her life been on the side of the weather.
