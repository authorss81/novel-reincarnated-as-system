# The Thirty-Third

Dawn of the fourth day came grey and windless, ash hanging motionless in the air like dust in a closed room. Ning Yan stood at the water's edge washing blood from the binding cloth, working carefully around the scar that pulled hot from sternum to hip, when the layer spoke again.

This time there was no breakthrough. No diligence recorded.

[Harvest thirty-three is gathered. The servant Lin Shou is taken. His diligence is recorded.]

Silence rushed back. The creek steamed. Somewhere a disciple dropped a cup.

Ning Yan went still with her hands in the black water.

Thirty-three. The quota said twenty-eight.

Every outer disciple at Qinghe knew the winter number. Twenty-eight this year, posted on the yard board in the Bureau's hand, read aloud by the Ledger Reader, believed by everyone in the yard because there was nothing else to believe. Twenty-eight hands, beasts or substitutes, to fill the sect's obligation before the thaw.

Shen Xiu carried it on his list-board. The furnace crew planned by it. The infirmary kept linens for it.

Thirty-three was not twenty-eight.

She straightened slowly, water dripping from her fingers, cold biting through the numbness. A servant in a county she had never visited, taken in a harvest that was not on any quota she had ever seen. The layer reports, she understood. It does not editorialize. Whatever is in the arithmetic gets said out loud, even if no mortal wrote it on any board.

Du Hong came to the bank, face pale. “You heard the number?”

“I heard it,” she said.

“It miscounted,” he said quickly. “It must have miscounted. Quota was twenty-eight. Everyone knew twenty-eight.”

“Then somebody is counting.”

Du Hong put both hands in the water and stood there with them, which she understood to be the only way he had of not shouting.

It was Lu Mei who found the thing they were all about to find, half an hour later, standing at the water with her hood down in the ash.

“Lin Shou,” she said. “I know that name.”

Du Hong turned. Ning Yan did not.

“He was a laundry-hand,” Lu Mei said. “Not ours. Beicounty. There was a girl from Beicounty married into the sixth street in Lowwater and she used to say her cousin had been at the furnace six years and never came back and had written once.” She looked at the water. “That is his name. That is the whole of what I know about him and I am telling you it in front of witnesses.”

A boy two feet away said, “So he is nothing.”

“He is the thirty-third,” Lu Mei said, and went away, which for Lu Mei was the whole of an argument.

On the far bank, the arithmetic had already started and it had already gone wrong. Four boys were saying twenty-eight at once, and one of them was saying it louder, and another one said, “Thirty-three is the whole bound. It is not ours.”

“It is ours,” said a fifth boy. “Twenty-eight is what Qinghe posts. Everything else belongs to Beihe and Canglan.”

“Then why is it saying it to us at four in the morning?”

Nobody had an answer, and the boys moved on to the next thing, which was whether there would be time to break camp before the light.

Then one of the second-rank boys, who was seventeen and liked to be right in front of witnesses, said the thing that put the knife on the table.

“It is ours, though,” he said. “The layer does not announce our numbers. It announces the bound’s. So the bound’s number is thirty-three. And the board at the gate says twenty-eight. And the run is not finished until the eighth day.”

Every boy on the bank turned to look at the same thing, which was Shen Xiu standing by the fire with his list-board in his hands, staring at nothing.

“So we are five short,” said Xie Qiao, from the lee of a trunk, his bound wrist held against his chest. “We go back up there having found four and we have to find five.”

Nobody argued with him. That was the part Ning Yan would remember. Eleven boys, in the cold, in the ash, hearing a number they did not want, and not one of them said the number was wrong.

She looked past them to where Shen Xiu stood by the fire with his list-board in his hands, staring at nothing. The Team Elder's face had gone the colour of ash. He had heard it too, and he had the look of a man whose private column had just been read aloud in public.

Not a man surprised by an error. A man who already knew the number was thirty-three and had been putting twenty-eight on his board anyway, because the board was what the Elder Hall came to check.

She filed that away, and did not let herself look at him again, because a girl who looked at the Team Elder for too long after an announcement is a girl with something to say about it.

The voice spoke, low and urgent.

“I count too. I hear thirty-three. I do not know what it is for.”

Ning Yan wrung out the cloth. Her hands shook, not from cold. Cold anger rose in her chest, the first of her life that was not hot and did not go away.

Not grief for her father in the furnace column or her brother in the kiln. Something cleaner. Somebody had taken a servant named Lin Shou for something no yard had been told about, and the only reason anyone knew was that the machinery spoke too loudly to keep the secret.

That was what it was. Not a fault in the counting. A fault in the telling. If the layer had been built to report what the sect had decided, thirty-three would never have crossed the bound at all, because a number that disagrees with the yard board is not news, it is a grievance, and nobody at the Elder Hall survives on grievances.

Whatever the layer was, it was not on her side, and it was not on the yard board's side either, and that was the first thing in four days she had been given that she could actually stand on.

She bound her chest again, tight, wincing, and made the decision aloud so Du Hong heard it and the voice heard it and she could not take it back.

“I am going to find out what the thirty-third was for.”

Du Hong stared. “You were barely stage four. You walked lame. You chased nothing bigger than your own token.”

“I chased my token and found a number,” she said. “I am keeping both.”

“What will you do first?”

“The run ends on the eighth day and the yard reads the ledger on the ninth morning.” She wiped her hands on her robe, which was the worst thing a girl could do to a wound and the fastest way she knew of getting it out of her own way. “I will stand in the yard and hear my own quarter-token settled. And then I will ask a question about the thirty-third, in a room with a clerk in it, because it is the only question anybody has been given in nine years. There will be a great many people in that yard who want to ask it. Exactly one of us will have a reason.”

Du Hong looked at her for a long moment.

“You are going to ask it with stage four and a scar and four hands of mine in your name.”

“Yes.”

“That is a bad plan.”

“It is the only plan,” she said. “Bad plans with paper in them are better than good plans without.”

Up the bank, Shen Xiu rolled his list-board and tied it with undue care, his hands precise and old. He did not meet her eyes.

She went and got him.

She had never once in seventeen years gone anywhere the Team Elder had not asked her to go, and she crossed the wet black mud of the bank with the sun not properly up and a scarf pulled over the binding cloth, and she stood in front of him while he worked at the cord on his board, and she waited until he looked at her.

“Does the board say twenty-eight,” she said.

“Yes.”

“It says five.”

“It says twenty-eight,” Shen Xiu said, “and you should go back to the tower before the light, and you should eat something.”

She did not go. She stood on the bank with her hands at her sides and let him look at her, and she waited, and she had learned that from a woman who folded robes for eleven years, that a tired man will keep talking longer than he means to if you refuse to help him stop.

“The boundary lamps,” she said. “That is where you send them. That is where Han Shu went and you wrote his dot in one stroke. Tell me what the first stroke is for.”

Shen Xiu’s hands stopped on the cord.

“You do not know what the first stroke is for,” he said.

“No.”

“Then you do not know that there is a first stroke. When a name goes into the loss column, the Elder Hall has to sign for it, and the Elder Hall does not sign for anything the layer has not already put in the air.” He shifted the board under his arm. “That is why they send us out before dawn. If they are in the tree line when the layer says a number, they have heard it too, and then the loss is not ours, it is the bound’s, and it cannot be checked against a board that says twenty-eight.”

She had not known that. She had known a plate, and a board, and a dot, and she had sent him because she could not carry him.

“Two?” she said.

“Two. The line at the boundary and the list at the gate. Everything else in between is firewood.”

“And if the layer says thirty-three and the gate says twenty-eight?”

Shen Xiu looked at her for a long moment. The tiredness in his face was not the kind that goes away with sleep, and she understood for the first time that she was not the only person in this camp carrying a number she had not asked for.

“It said what it said,” he said. “I heard it.”

“You already knew.”

“I heard it.”

That was all he would give her, and it was more than she had expected from a man with a brush in his hand and four names still to decide. She took it, because in her mother’s yard you did not take the first thing a tired man offered and then push for the second. You took what was on the table and you went away and you thought about it while you worked.

She needed a record. The only records were in a kiln nobody was supposed to enter, and in towers kept by clerks who coughed their lives out writing down what the air said.

She kept the pine splinter of the tower plate in her sleeve and felt its edges through the cloth. It was the first thing she had ever planned to keep.

The ash began to fall again, fine and silent, and the camp broke with unusual quiet, no one speaking about what everyone had heard.
