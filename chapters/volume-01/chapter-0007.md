# The Thirty-Third

Dawn of the fourth day came grey and windless, ash hanging motionless in the air like dust in a closed room. Ning Yan stood at the water's edge washing blood from the binding cloth, working carefully around the scar that pulled hot from sternum to hip, when the layer spoke again.

This time there was no breakthrough. No diligence recorded.

[Harvest thirty-three is gathered. The servant Lin Shou is taken. His diligence is recorded.]

Silence rushed back. The creek steamed. Somewhere a disciple dropped a cup.

Ning Yan went still with her hands in the black water.

Thirty-three. The quota said twenty-eight.

Every outer disciple at Qinghe knew the winter number. Twenty-eight this year, posted on the yard board in the Bureau's hand, read aloud by the Ledger Reader, believed by everyone in the yard because there was nothing else to believe. Twenty-eight hands, beasts or substitutes, to fill the sect's obligation before the thaw. Shen Xiu carried it on his list-board. The furnace crew planned by it. The infirmary kept linens for it.

Thirty-three was not twenty-eight.

She straightened slowly, water dripping from her fingers, cold biting through the numbness. A servant in a county she had never visited, taken in a harvest that was not on any quota she had ever seen. The layer reports, she understood. It does not editorialize. Whatever is in the arithmetic gets said out loud, even if no mortal wrote it on any board.

Du Hong came to the bank, face pale. “You heard the number?”

“I heard it,” she said.

“It miscounted,” he said quickly. “It must have miscounted. Quota was twenty-eight. Everyone knew twenty-eight.”

She looked past him to where Shen Xiu stood by the fire with his list-board in his hands, staring at nothing. The Team Elder's face had gone the colour of ash. He had heard it too, and he had the look of a man whose private column had just been read aloud in public.

The voice spoke, low and urgent.

“I count too. I hear thirty-three. I do not know what it is for.”

Ning Yan wrung out the cloth. Her hands shook, not from cold. Cold anger rose in her chest, the first in the book of her life that was not hot and did not go away. Not grief for her father in the furnace column or her brother in the kiln. Something cleaner. Someone was filling the number off the book, and the layer announced it anyway, flat and truthful, because the layer could not lie about what it was made to say.

Somebody had taken a servant named Lin Shou for something no yard had been told about, and the only reason anyone knew was that the machinery spoke too loudly to keep the secret.

She bound her chest again, tight, wincing, and made the decision aloud so Du Hong heard it and the voice heard it and she could not take it back.

“I am going to find out what the thirty-third was for.”

Du Hong stared. “You were barely stage four. You walked lame. You chased nothing bigger than your own token.”

“I chased my token and found a number,” she said. “I am keeping both.”

Up the bank, Shen Xiu rolled his list-board and tied it with undue care, his hands precise and old. He did not meet her eyes. He had decided four names, she knew, and now there were thirty-three harvests in the air and twenty-eight on his board, and the difference sat between them like a stone in the road.

She needed a record. The only records were in a kiln nobody was supposed to enter, and in towers kept by clerks who coughed their lives out writing down what the air said.

She kept the pine splinter of the tower plate in her sleeve and felt its edges through the cloth. It was the first thing she had ever planned to keep.

The ash began to fall again, fine and silent, and the camp broke with unusual quiet, no one speaking about what everyone had heard.
