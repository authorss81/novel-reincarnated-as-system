# Wen the Lamp-mender

The Lowwater road in daylight was a different road.

Ning Yan walked it in the second week with a travel writ for oil-buying, stage seven hidden under a plain coat, the pine splinter and Feng Lushi’s copy sewn inside her lining. She had leave until supper. She had a question she could not ask anyone at Qinghe, and one set of people who might answer it.

The Lantern order worked the road in pairs, carts with lamp-oil and spare wicks, mending the boundary lamps and reading last night’s news aloud in market squares. They were almost all illiterate, which made them good at the one skill the Bureau could not train, which was remembering exactly what the air said.

She found Wen’s cart at the second milestone, stopped with a wheel half off.

Wen the lamp-mender was a woman in her forties with oil under her nails and a back bent from years of lifting lamp-housings. She did not look up when Ning Yan approached. Her mate, a younger man with a bone-reader’s satchel, kept working the axle.

“The order does not stop for sect disciples,” Wen said, past tense, flat. “We mend. We move. Ask the tower if you want paper.”

Ning Yan bowed. “I asked the tower last week. Now I walk to ask the road.”

“That is a pretty sentence. It does not lift a wheel.”

Ning Yan set down her oil jar and lifted the other side of the cart without being asked. The axle settled. The younger man grunted thanks. Wen looked at her then, really looked, at the scar line showing at her throat, at the way she stood.

“You carry weight like somebody who buried people,” Wen said. “I remember you. You came through with the stretcher-party.”

“I came to the tower,” Ning Yan said. “I heard the layer speak twice on the run. I want to ask about it.”

“The order does not answer about the layer.” Wen wiped her hands. “We carry oil. We repeat what was said. We do not explain it. Ask a clerk.”

They walked together a while, because the cart moved at walking pace and Ning Yan had nowhere faster to be in daylight for once. The ash lay thin on the verges. The river showed grey between trees. It was the first time in weeks she travelled without fear of being counted, and the ease of it made her careless.

She asked anyway, carefully, the way she asked Yuan things and watched how long he took.

“Is it the sky?”

Wen stopped the cart.

Silence stretched. The younger mate moved ahead to check the next lamp, tactfully deaf. Wen studied Ning Yan with an expression that was not fear and not anger, but the professional disgust of a person asked to touch something unclean.

“No,” Wen said at last. “It is not a sky. Somebody speaks it.”

Ning Yan’s breath caught.

Wen went on, reluctantly, each word weighed. “There is a difference between weather and an office. Weather happens. An office writes. The layer is an office. The Bureau is the part of it anybody inside can see. The rest you do not ask after on the road.”

An office. Not a heaven. Not a god. Somebody read the layer and wrote it back. Ning Yan felt the furniture of her comfortable idea move all at once, the way a room moves in a quake.

She wanted to push. Who speaks it. Where is the office. Who writes back. Six years of wanting her brother’s name in ink rose up. She opened her mouth.

Then she closed it.

She let it go, on purpose, on the Lowwater road with daylight still good and the tower ahead. The letting go was the first time in the book she chose not to know something, and it cost her more than asking would have.

Wen watched her decide and nodded once, approving.

“You learn fast,” she said. “Most sect children ask three more. The third one gets them left at the milestone.”

They reached the tower turn before evening reading. Wen stopped the cart, checked the lamps, and would not go nearer the tower while the reading was prepared. Ning Yan thanked her for the wheel and for the one answer.

Yuan spoke as Ning Yan climbed toward the lamps, thin with awe.

“You choose silence. I respect it. I stay silent with you.”

She touched her sleeve where the copy lay. She had been operating inside a comfortable idea, that the sky spoke and clerks wrote. Now somebody spoke and somebody wrote, and the Bureau was only the visible edge.

She had Feng’s register ahead, and her brother’s name in his hand. That was enough for one day. The rest could wait until she could carry it.

She walked on toward the evening lamps with the office behind the sky pressing cold on her neck.
