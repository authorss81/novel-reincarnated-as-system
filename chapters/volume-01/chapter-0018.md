# The Kiln Road

The road from Lowwater to the Broken Kiln ran four miles through ashwoods that had not been cut in sixteen years.

Ning Yan left Lowwater after the evening reading with Feng’s copy sewn beside the first, oil writ expired, travel excuse thin. She had one goal and a hard limit. Reach the kiln before dark, unescorted and unrecorded, with an hour of light left to see the door.

She walked fast. Stage seven carried her, thin but willing. The ash lay deep off the path. No sect patrol showed on the first two miles, only a woodcutter’s cart going the other way, the driver not meeting her eyes.

Wen’s cart caught her at the third milestone.

The lamp-mender sat with reins loose, younger mate beside her, lamp-oil sloshing in the back. Wen looked at Ning Yan, at the pack, at the direction, and her face changed.

“You go to the kiln,” Wen said. It was not a question.

Ning Yan bowed. “I go to see where my brother was assessed.”

“The order does not go there.” Wen’s voice stayed flat, past tense, final. “Turn back. Ask the tower. Ask the Bureau. Do not walk that road after light.”

“I have leave until morning. No one recorded my direction.” Ning Yan kept walking, past the cart. “I need to see the door.”

Wen clicked to the mule. The cart pulled alongside, then ahead, as if to block. At the marker post where the road bent, the mule stopped without being told.

The marker was a plain post with a strip of cloth tied at the top, weathered grey, the sort of thing nobody thought to check twice. Wen’s cart stopped at it and would not go past. The mule planted its feet. The wheels locked on ash that was not steep.

Wen said, “Not here,” and did not say why.

Ning Yan stopped too. Cold went down her spine. “What is here?”

Wen looked at the post, at the cloth, at the four miles of road beyond going dark under trees. “The kiln is sealed,” she said. “Not by masonry. By marker. The order physically cannot cross it. Our carts stop. Our feet stop. Our lamps go out if we carry them past.” She met Ning Yan’s eyes. “I am not unwilling. I am unable. There is a difference and you will feel it if you go.”

Sect watch on the kiln road, a Warden’s marker she could not see, and a lamp-mender’s cart that would not go past a sealed place. Ning Yan understood all at once that the seal was not a door with a lock. It was an instruction, and the Lantern order, who lived by repeating what was spoken, could not disobey what was written into the air.

Small, useless, real terror rose in her. She let it rise. She had learned that terror, like anger, was useful when it was cold.

“Will it stop me?” she asked.

Wen studied her. “You are not of the order. You are sect. The marker was put for other things.” She hesitated. “If you go, go before dark is full. Come back before night watch. Do not take anything. Do you hear me? Do not take anything that was inside.”

Ning Yan nodded, throat tight. She wanted to ask what the marker was, who put it, what warden meant. She did not ask. Wen had given one answer on the daylight road and would not give another on the dark one.

She walked past the post alone.

The mule did not move. Wen did not call after her. The cart sat at the marker with its lamps burning, unable to follow, while a seventeen-year-old girl walked the last four miles into trees that grew thicker and quieter with every step.

She arrived after dark with an hour of light left, the way dying light lingers in ashwoods, grey and thin. The Broken Kiln showed through trunks as a ruin of fired brick and collapsed roof, chimney broken at half height, yard walled with fallen stone. No guard. No lock. No sign anybody came here. The seal did not need them.

Yuan spoke as she stopped at the yard edge, quick and thin.

“I am here. I go with you. I do not stop.”

She touched the post of the yard gate for steadiness. Her breath came fast. Stage seven felt very small against a place the layer sealed.

She was on the wrong side of a thing she had been told did not exist, alone, with light failing. The door of the assessment room showed dark beyond the yard.

She walked toward it.
