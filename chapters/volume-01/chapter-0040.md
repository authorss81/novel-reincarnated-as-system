# Feng Lushi Writes

The Lantern Register tower at Lowwater burned day and night, and on the stair the warm air came down at her face like something poured.

Ning Yan went up it with the wrapped book under her arm and the tablet against her skin and the log in her lining, seven days out of the eleven, stage seven leaking quietly through a crack in her chest every time she put her weight down. The stair was ninety-one steps and she counted them because counting was free and she had four days and would need every one of them.

The door was open. It had not been open before.

Feng Lushi was at the desk with his sleeves pushed up and his hair wet at the temples, and there was blood on the cloth he kept folded in his fist, and there was a second sheet on the desk under his right hand that he had been in the middle of covering when the door went.

She did not look at the sheet. He saw her not look at it, and something in his face let go.

“You are worse,” she said.

“I am exactly the same. I was worse on Tuesday.” He coughed, and it took a while, and he did not hide it. “You are worse.”

“Both true.” She set the wrapped book on the desk. “This is the furnace records book. The reassignment page is in it. I wrote nothing in it. I am returning it to the man who wrote it.”

He untied the cloth with one hand and looked at her face and did not ask what she had come for, which told her he already knew, because a clerk who has spent twenty-two years listening could tell the difference between a person who has come to leave something and a person who has come to take something.

He had come to the tower three times that season. He had never once been asked for anything.

“Eight days ago a sealed instruction came up from the county,” he said. “Bureau hand. It says the Lowwater tower will omit from the upward copy all matter concerning the Qinghe servant-born of the furnace crew, and will treat any such matter as an error of hearing.”

She felt the room get colder by four degrees and did not look at the sheet.

“They have written you out of the record,” she said.

“They have written you out of the record upward. They have not written you out of the room. I hear you. I always hear you; that is not something an instruction touches.” He wiped his mouth with the cloth and put it down.

“I have been copying upward as directed for eight days. I have also been copying upward in the third column of the middle register, which is my own, which goes nowhere and is read by nobody, and which I have used four times since you came down the mountain.”

“Four.”

“Four. The date you asked after your brother. The date on your tower plate. What the seal of the broken ground logged when you came out. And the night you were reported by a Qinghe list-holder three days early.” He said it without any weight at all, a man reading a docket. “The fourth one cost me a fortnight of coughing and I would do it again before breakfast.”

“You have been writing my story upward in a place nobody reads.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

He did not answer at once. He looked at the shelves, which held four hundred volumes of every sentence the bound had said in twenty-two years, and when he spoke the words came out in a clerk's flat delivery and the flatness was doing all the work.

“I have never once had a story where somebody was believed,” he said. “Twenty-two years of weather, of harvests, of bodies counted and quotas filled and men announced. Nine counties, nineteen towers, and not one line in any of it that anybody can act on, because it does not say what to do, it says what happened and then it stops.

You are the first person who has ever come up my stair carrying a thing that has happened. You wanted a name in a book. I wanted one sentence in four hundred volumes that was in there because a person made it happen.”

He looked up.

“So I put four of them in a column nobody reads, and I intend to keep going, and you can tell your Elder Hall that from me if it is any use to you, which I do not expect it to be.”

She put her hand flat on the desk and did not say anything for a while, because the account she had been keeping since the tower under the Ashjaw had just been increased by a man she had met three times, and she did not want to be the kind of person who thanked a dying man for something.

That was the rule. Gratitude was a resolution, and this book did not hand it out, and the worst of it was that she could not have repaid him anyway.

“I am not going to give you anything,” she said.

“Good.”

“I am not going to teach you anything, and I am not going to tell you what is in me, and if the Hall comes up this stair with a warrant for your volumes you will have to stand there without me in the room.”

“Good,” he said again, with the same flatness. “That is how it is done.”

“Then I am going to ask you for something and you may refuse it.”

He put both hands flat on the desk. “Ask.”

“Stage nine is in four days. After that is Condensing, and I have no seal and no map and no right to the page. I cannot find the last two stages myself and I cannot be told them by the voice, because that voice can find a technique in a living body and has never once in its life been able to say what it is in words.”

“I know that. I have listened to you say so on my stair twice.”

“Then I need the order. Not a secret and not a manual. The order in which it is written down.”

Feng Lushi did not move for a long moment, and when he moved he moved towards the shelves rather than away, and she understood that he was deciding to hear her out before he decided.

“What order,” he said.

“The sentence.” She stood up, which she had not meant to do, and went to the shelf and put her hand flat on the nearest spine and did not lift it. “Every advancement the region has announced in twenty-two years. You have written four hundred volumes of them. Where is it.”

“What it looks like is not what it is.”

“I do not care what it looks like. I have been listening to men explain what advancement is for eleven years. I want what is on the paper.”

He gave her the volume. She did not have the four days for four hundred volumes and she knew it, so he brought her eight, and then eleven, and then she asked him for the index, because he had one in his head and not on paper, and he said the years aloud while she wrote the shelf marks in her poor hand in a bad wash code under them.

Then she sat at his desk with eleven volumes and her own four hundred words of arithmetic, and it took her four hours, and the thing she was looking for was not a secret. It was a habit.

The sentence never changed its order.

Four hundred volumes, nineteen towers, every advancement report inside the bound since before Feng Lushi took the post, and the clause order was fixed: where the person was, who the person was, what the body had let go of, and what the body was holding afterwards. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Some in his hand, some in nineteen other hands, some in three counties' worth of a Lantern woman's memory of what the air had said.

And the third clause was always the top.

She put her palm flat on the open page and read eleven different people letting go of the same thing, which was never the whole of them and was always whatever was newest, which was not strength and not health and not a year of life. It was whatever was sitting on top of the body when the moment came, and what arrived in the fourth clause was always a shape, and the shape was always one the person had been holding just underneath.

Nothing in four hundred volumes said *empty*. Nothing in four hundred volumes said *in this order*. It did not need to. Every sentence had already said it four times in the order it had been said in, four hundred times over, in a fair hand, by a dying man who had never once been asked what any of it meant.

“You have given me the method,” she said. “You do not know that you have given me the method.”

“I have given you the order of the words,” he said.

“It is the order of the words.” She laughed, once, and it came out cracked in the middle. “That is the order of the words. It is also the order of a body, and it has been the order of a body for longer than this tower has been standing, and you have four hundred volumes of proof and nobody has ever read them for that, because they are not scripture, they are weather.”

She stood up too fast and had to put her hand on the desk. Stage seven was thinner than stage seven had been that morning. Eleven hours of reading on a body that leaked every time it moved had cost her something she could not itemise.

“Take the eleven down for me,” she said. “And then the eleven from before those, and then the third set. Put them on the crate by the stair. I will come down and carry them up the hill.”

“That is eleven years of the tower’s record.”

“Yes.”

“You are seven days out of a stage you have not reached and the Assembly is on the other side of that.”

“Yes.”

He looked at her for a long moment, and then he began, without hurrying, taking volumes off the shelf in order.

That was the last good thing that happened to her in winter.

She carried forty-one volumes down ninety-one steps and up four miles of mountain road in two trips, because she would not give him the third trip, and the weight of them took her from stage seven into stage eight somewhere on the Lowwater turn with the load against her chest and the crack open and the pattern taking it sideways, so that the cost of the carry landed in her shoulders and none of it went out through the hole.

[Body-Forging. Stage eight. Ning Yan of Qinghe. Nine counties hear a girl go up a stage on the Lowwater road carrying forty-one volumes, and the county of Lowwater has nobody in it who writes this down.]

She stood on the Lowwater road at dusk with a crate against her hip and a pattern running through her legs off the leak, and she was stronger than she had been at dawn and she had not trained once, and every stage in this book had been built by carrying something.

She had carried a stretcher. She had carried a plate. She had carried four hundred volumes of weather up a mountain, and it had made her a cultivator, and there was nothing fine about that and nothing she could have done differently.

She got to the base of the terrace at full dark and there were two men in Lowwater watch coats standing at the foot of the stair.

The younger one was holding a sheet. The older one had a lantern and had clearly been standing there a long while and had clearly been told to be there a long while.

The young man looked at her, and at the crate, and at the crate again.

“Furnace crew,” he said. “Ning Yan.”

“That's me.”

“There is a note come up out of the county about you.” He turned the sheet over and looked at the back of it, which was blank, and turned it back. “It says you went into sealed ground. The roundhouse has a room and the captain has a paper with your name on it and he would rather you came down quietly than that he had to write *refused*.”

Feng Lushi's cough came down ninety-one steps behind her and stopped on the stair.

“Mention my name,” he said, past tense, from the dark, “and they will take it down as though I said it. I did not say it.”

He did not say anything else. She had asked him not to help and he had been helping in the exact way she had specified, which was the way he had been helping since she had first climbed his stair.

She set the crate down on the terrace steps, in front of the two men, with the whole county of Qinghe above her and the Assembly eight days off and a pattern turning quietly in her legs.

“Fine,” she said. “I will come down quietly.”
