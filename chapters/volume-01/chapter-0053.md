# The Record of the Trial

The stewards got to her before she could get herself off the floor, and there were four of them and a hall that had no roof, and she could not stand, so they did the arithmetic of it between them and one of them knelt and put his hands under her.

She said, “I can walk.”

Nobody argued with her about it, which was worse. They simply waited, politely, in front of the whole of that hall, and she got her left leg under her and came up off the plank with her right arm hanging out of its own weight, and she got about four feet.

Du Hong was on the floor before she was upright again.

He did not ask. He came in under her left side and took her weight and lifted, which is a thing you cannot do with an arm that has been struck five times, and she felt him take it and she said, “You can put me down,” and he said, “No,” and she said, “Put me down, Du Hong,” and he said, “No,” and then after a second and a half he said, “There. I have put you down,” and set her on the front bench of the Speaker's table, which was the one piece of furniture in that building left standing at that height.

He did not look at her face while he did it. He looked at the bench.

“Thank you,” said Ning Yan.

“It is a bench,” said Du Hong.

She had known for nine days that he had been sent to report on her and had not. She had watched him not do it twice. She had worked out in the middle of the winter that he was a decent man who had been bought, and that he did not know she had seen the order, and that the only thing standing between him and a career was the fact that she had never once mentioned it in a room.

He did not know. He put her on a bench and looked at the wood and said *it is a bench*, and she sat there in a roofless hall with a hole in her and understood that she had kept that secret for nine days for a man who had not known he was being protected, and that she had kept it because it was the only leverage she had ever had, and that she was not going to spend it today.

They were all standing in an open-sided building looking at the front of the Speaker's table.

## Four Seconds

The Speaker ruled the hearing concluded at about eleven minutes past the ninth hour, in a voice that had nothing left in it, and then he said the thing that was next.

“Clerk. The record.”

Xie Shuo stood up.

There was a table for him and a page for him and a fair hand he had had for thirty-one years, and three walls of people waiting for a man with a brush to write down what he had seen happen to a girl.

He wrote.

She watched him do it and it was the longest minute of her life, longer than the four minutes of nothing between the second strike and the third. He wrote the hour. He wrote the count, which had not moved all morning, which meant every person in Qinghe Sect had come to the floor of its Hall and not one had left. He wrote the terms of the hearing and the member's rank and the number of the strikes and the words the member had spoken on the floor, which were *two more, that is all, that is all*.

He wrote for a minute and forty seconds and he wrote it all the way through without lifting the brush, and when he came to the end of it he squared the page against the table with two fingers and put the pen down.

And then nothing happened.

It was four seconds.

She knew it was four seconds because she was counting. She was always counting; she had counted the ash on the sill of her window in the first week of the fall and she was counting this, and four seconds is a length she had measured a thousand times in a hundred different rooms, and the whole hall stood in it like a man standing in water up to his chest.

In those four seconds nobody in that room moved. There was a hall full of people who had watched a girl get struck by lightning five times on a floor and every single one of them stopped at the same instant, the way a room stops when a clerk stops writing.

She looked for it. That was the thing she did with those four seconds, and she had not decided to do it, she simply did it: she went looking round that hall for whatever it was that everybody in it was waiting for, because she was the sort of person who, when a room goes quiet, looks for the cause.

Xie Shuo had both hands flat on the table and was not doing anything at all.

Elder Pei Shou was sitting down with his hands in his lap and was not doing anything at all.

Along the wall, in the second gallery, at every edge of a building with three walls — nothing. Every face in it was the face of a person waiting for a sentence to be read out.

Nobody was hesitating. Nobody was deciding anything. It was the room a clerk's pen makes in the second before it speaks, and there was not one thing in it that belonged to any person standing there.

So she went looking somewhere else.

He was not there.

Not gone — she knew what gone was, and gone was forty minutes in a dark room in a sealed building with a metallic taste afterwards. This was different. He was in there and he was not saying anything, which he had done twice since the ninth week and one of those had been in a grain cart four miles up a road while she was carrying forty-one volumes.

She had four seconds and she used two of them on the only question she had, and she said it inside her own attention, in the voice she used for tallies, because it was the only voice she had for a thing that mattered.

*What are you doing.*

## It Goes Out

It came out at the fourth second.

It did not arrive from anywhere. That was the thing she had never been able to explain to anybody and had stopped trying to explain to herself in about the eleventh day: it was not a sound that came from the sky or from a room or from a building. It was simply in her, the way her own pulse is in her, arriving in the middle of her chest at the same time as it arrived in the chest of everybody in three walls of a building.

[Assembly Hall of Qinghe. Ning Yan of the Qinghe furnace crew, servant-born. What was held is let go. What was taken is held.]

She heard the rest of the room hear it.

Nothing happened in that room for a second and a half after the announcement. Then about four hundred people turned round and looked at one another, which is what a room of cultivators does when the voice in their own chests says a sentence none of them have ever heard, and there was a noise in it like a field with wind in it.

“Clerk,” said Elder Pei Shou.

“The record must read as the report reads,” said Xie Shuo.

Nobody in that hall knew what the report had said. They were all still holding it in their chests, looking at each other, and not one of them could say out loud what he had heard, and the Speaker had just asked the one man in the building who would have to say it out loud.

Xie Shuo looked down at his page.

His page was in his own fair hand and it was complete and it was, in every particular that a clerk can check, correct: the hour, the count, the rank, the number of the strikes, the words spoken. It did not contain the report, because the report was not a thing that went on a page. It went out.

“Repeat it,” said the Speaker.

Xie Shuo looked up.

“Assembly Hall of Qinghe,” he said, and his voice did not go, and she understood that he had decided in about a second and a half to make sure of it, which meant he was going to make sure of it. “Ning Yan of the Qinghe furnace crew. *Servant-born.* What was held is let go. What was taken is held.”

He put the pen down.

The hall did not make a noise.

That was the thing. She had spent two months learning the acoustics of that building and it had never once in her life been silent at a sentence, and it stood in three walls with a clerk's voice in it and every person in the sect, and the sentence went out across the flags and out through the open side of the building and away over Qinghe, and she understood why it was quiet: everybody in that room had heard it themselves, at the same moment, in their own chests, and they were not looking at the clerk.

They were looking at her.

## The Word in the Sentence

Down at the east end of the second gallery a woman said it out loud, which she had not meant to do.

“Servant-born.”

She said it the way people say a word in a language they have just noticed is not one they speak. And then every other person who had the same sentence in their chest at the same instant said the same word out loud, and there was a noise in the hall like a door being opened in a storm.

They had heard it. All of them. Everybody inside the bound had it in their chests at the same moment, out in the furnaces and the yards and the terraces and nine counties of villages, and the only two people in the building who understood immediately why those two words mattered were sitting in the fourth and fifth seats of the second gallery.

Ning Yan turned her head and looked at Lu Kang.

He was looking at the floor with both hands flat on his knees, in exactly the posture Qiu Zhulang had been in at the fifth hour, and he had not moved for about nine seconds, and then he said something to the man beside him that she was too far away to hear.

She looked at the Speaker.

Elder Pei Shou had a hand over his mouth. He took it away and put it flat on the table, and she watched a man reach the age he was going to die at, sitting in his own Hall, in a building with no roof, on a morning in the fourth month of a winter that had not finished.

Not once, in four hundred volumes of Feng Lushi's fair hand, in eleven hundred years of advancement inside the bound. The voice carried a place. It carried a name. It carried a sect, a realm and a stage. It had carried every one of those things on every single occasion and it had never once, in any county, in any room in nine counties, said out loud what a person had been born to.

Every advancement in the history of the bound had been a statement about what somebody had managed.

This one had been a statement about who they were.

Somewhere out over Lowwater a lamp-mender was standing in a market square reading it out loud to a crowd that did not understand why people were shouting, and somewhere in four counties four copyists were writing down four sentences about it, and one of them was going to write it correctly, because there was one person inside the bound who wrote announcements down in a fair hand and had never once got one wrong.

Nine counties. Three walls and no roof.

The bound knew her name now, and it had said the rest of it too, and it had said it into every living chest in nine counties at the same moment, and there was no instrument in any office in the world that could put it back.

She sat on the front bench of the Speaker's table in a roofless hall with her right arm out of its own weight and looked at the ash coming down through the missing part of the sky, and she had got what she came for, and it was the only thing she had ever wanted, and she was the most famous person inside the bound and it was nine minutes past the ninth hour on the fourth morning of a winter that was not over.
