# The Elder of the Furnace

The furnace room below the terrace held heat like a held breath.

Ning Yan came down in the morning on a summons carried by a furnace-man's boy, stage nine hidden under a plain coat, tablet against skin, ledger flat to her ribs. Her goal was narrow. She needed to keep the tablet hidden and get through the conversation without being read.

Resistance was kindness. Elder Qiu Zhulang waited beside the furnace mouth with his hands folded, sixty-one, broad-faced, eyes tired and direct. He did not stand behind a desk. He stood beside the work, where heat reddened his sleeves. He looked at her the way a man looked at a ledger line he had already decided to settle, and smiled.

“You came up fast,” he said. “Nine in winter. Your father reached six and held it four years. He talked about your legs.”

Her throat closed. Nobody had spoken of her father as a man who talked in years.

“You knew him,” she said. Past tense, careful. “You worked him.”

“I burned with him.” Qiu gestured to the bench. Sat himself, heavily, knees cracking. “Sat with him nights the furnace ran long. He worried about your mother’s cough before it had a name. Sit. You stand like somebody braced for a blow, and I am too old to deliver one worth bracing for.”

She sat. Heat worked through her coat. Sweat started under the tablet. She wanted to shift it and did not move. The furnace mouth breathed behind an iron grate, orange and low, and a crewman fed it coal with a long shovel, face averted from the glare.

The room smelled of hot metal and old sweat. Tools hung in order on the wall, tongs and hooks and the long spoons for core-stone weighing. Everything had its place. Forty years of places.

He asked about the family without prying, the way old men did, meal by meal. How the laundry work paid. How long the wet lungs took. He did not threaten. He did not mention seals or tablets or logs. He spoke of Lowwater winters, of oil prices, of a furnace repair that took a month.

Then he said, quiet, “He was not the only one in that firing. There were four others that night. I remembered all five.”

Her breath stopped.

“You kept names,” she said. “The Hall never asked you to produce them.”

“I kept them here.” He tapped his temple, smiled tiredly. “Never once was asked. Forty years, never once. Paper burns. Men forget. I did not.”

Hope and dread moved together, hot. She had come to destroy a monster and found a clerk with a memory.

She asked for the five names.

“Gave them to me,” she said, low. “You owed my father that much. You owed all five.”

He gave them without hesitation, and not as a favour. Five names, plain, two syllables each, with sect ranks and years and villages. The first a furnace-man. The second a kiln-hand. The third an outer disciple stalled at stage six. The fourth a servant woman who carried coal. The fifth—

The fifth was not a servant. The name carried a rank that did not belong in a furnace firing, an inner name, a family with a wall and a gate.

She repeated the five back to fix them, and he corrected her gently on one tone, the way a teacher corrected, and she wrote nothing down because writing would have killed them both. She hid them instead in wash code in her head, dot and cross, the way her mother taught her to hold tallies.

Yuan stayed very quiet, counting, awed.

“He speaks true. I hear it. I believe.”

She felt anger shift under the heat. A villain she could hate cleanly. A man she could not.

“You told me freely,” she said. “You feared nothing in telling.”

“I feared everything,” Qiu said. “Gave them anyway. A girl walked in carrying her father and I was not going to make her file a request.”

She stood to go, legs unsteady from heat and from carrying five dead people out of a kind man's mouth. At the door she stopped.

The person she came to destroy would hand her the list if she asked. That was the revelation, and it frightened her worse than threats. Monsters could be fought. Men had to be answered.

She wanted the next name now. Her brother's. She held it until her hands stopped shaking.
