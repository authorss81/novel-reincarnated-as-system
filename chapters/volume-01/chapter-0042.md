# Three Counties

The copying floor under the Lantern Register tower at Lowwater was one long low room with a ceiling of soot and no windows, and at the fourth watch of the night it was full of women copying by ear what they had heard in the air.

There were seven of them. They sat in two rows at long boards with rice paper weighted flat, and they did not read, because none of them could. They had been taught to listen and to hold, and the order of the words was what they had instead of letters, and the ink went down in a big flat even hand with the pen held the way a child holds it.

They were copying the county sheets. That was what the floor was for. Nineteen towers wrote the day's layer down in their own hands, and every sheet came down to Lowwater on the monthly run, and the floor made the clean copy that went upward to an address that was written on the desk and never spoken aloud.

Ning Yan stood in the doorway with a lamp she had not been given and let the room see her.

The oldest of the women looked up. She had been in Lowwater the whole of Ning Yan's life and had been in the square that afternoon and had heard a girl on a platform say that she would show the plate to nobody.

“You were on the boards,” the woman said.

“Yes.”

“You should not be in here.”

“I know.”

“You are still in here.” She put her brush down. “Han, take the lamp off her. She can hold it by the handle. She cannot have it near the paper.”

The girl called Han took it, and the room went back to its work, and Ning Yan stood at the end of the second row and looked at the sheets, which was the only thing she had come down for.

[Three counties set down the same hour differently. The hour is one hour. The counties are nine counties wide.]

They were lying in a cloth roll on the sorting board, seventeen of them, one per county, all for the same day, all for the same evening reading. The women had them out because they collated upward on the fourth watch and compared, and comparing was the only thing that caught a copying error.

Ning Yan put her hand out for the Shouyi sheet and the woman called Han hesitated and then handed it over, because a caller who asked the way she had asked was somebody who had been taught to ask.

Lowwater's was on top. Feng Lushi's hand, twenty-two years of it, and it was the first one she read, and it went into her like cold water.

*Ning Yan of Qinghe advances to Body-Forging, the seventh stage.*

Four hundred volumes had not taught her to be surprised by the layer. They had taught her that it reported. She had stood in the Verge at four in the morning and heard Mei Jiu's promotion arrive in the air like weather and had decided then that it was an office rather than a sky, and she had gone at it since on the assumption that offices tell the truth in a dull voice.

That was still true. The sentence was dull and it was exactly one sentence and it was in the order that four hundred volumes said it should be in, and it said where she was and who she was and what she was holding afterwards, and it did not say one word about where she had been or what she had cost to get there.

She put it down and picked up Hanhui's.

*Ning Yan of Qinghe advances to Body-Forging, the seventh stage, for discipline.*

Same hand, same night, same ink, four clauses in the right order and then four more words at the end that no advancement report in four hundred volumes had ever carried, and the words made her a girl who had been disciplined, which was a thing you say about a servant, and it took a stage seven and a week on the ash-flats and eleven days of leaking through a hole in her chest and put it in the same shape as a girl who had been shouted at.

She put that down and picked up Shouyi's, and Shouyi's had a different kind of wrong in it.

*An outer disciple of Qinghe has advanced to Body-Forging, the seventh stage.*

Her name was gone. Not hidden, not smudged, not refused on some principle. Gone, with the grammar closed over the hole where it had been, and there was no mark on the sheet at all, and the woman who had written it had done it perfectly and without any opinion.

There was a person in Wanzhou. She had an appointment to be told about and a girl from Wanzhou to meet and she had four hours before the light came. So she picked up the Wanzhou sheet.

*A servant of Qinghe advances to Body-Forging, the seventh stage.*

Also her name. Also done perfectly.

She laid the four side by side on the sorting board and put the lamp on the stone so the light ran flat across them, and she read them again, one at a time, four times, and each time she read the whole thing and each time the whole thing was true and the whole thing was a different sentence.

She looked at the oldest woman.

“They were not in the room,” she said. “Any of them. Nobody is ever in the room. They only hear it and they only hold it and they put down what they held, and that is the whole of their job, and it is a good job.”

“That is the whole of our job.”

“Then they are not making mistakes.” Ning Yan kept her voice down, because two hundred people in a market were a different thing entirely from seven women in a soot room with a locked door. “Look at Wanzhou. Look at the wrist.

That hand has been unsteady since before I came down off the mountain, and the sentence on that sheet has no wobble in it at all. That sentence was handed to her. And look at Shouyi. That is not an ear, that is a copyist with good ink. And Hanhui — hold it to the lamp.”

The Hanhui sheet had a stamp at the foot of it. A small pressed mark in faded red, half a finger wide, the ordinary county stamp that went on anything the county wanted to be able to point at afterwards. It had not been on the other three.

“You have all three of those reasons and a stamp on one of them,” she said, “and I have been standing here assuming that the difference was me.”

“Do not do that,” the woman said. “There is nothing to be done with that.”

“There is one thing to be done with it. It goes in a book, with the date on it, and the three sheets with it, and then the book goes somewhere outside four counties, because a thing that can change what nine counties hear is not nine counties' business any more, it is up.”

“A book.”

“I have one.

It is in the lining of my coat and it is written in the worst hand in the Lowwater basin, and if you give it to me and I carry it up the hill, then in three months somebody can prove what the air said in four counties on one evening in the Low Water. Or somebody can prove that it did not say it. I want that on paper before I stand on a floor in front of nine hundred people and ask them for a thing.”

The woman looked at the four sheets and at the door and at the girl, and then she took the knife out of her sleeve and cut the string on the roll at the mark for Hanhui and cut it again at Shouyi and again at Wanzhou, and put the three in the flat of her own hand, which was the most illegal thing anyone had done for Ning Yan in this town and had cost nobody anything.

“Lowwater's you already have,” the woman said. “You had it first and you read it first and everybody saw you read it first.”

“That was on purpose.”

“I did notice.” She went back to her board. “Han, take the lamp back up to the keeper. He will have gone to bed. Put it where he can find it in the morning and tell him the shutter on the west side wants mending, because that is true.”

Ning Yan stood in the copying floor of the Lantern Register tower at Lowwater, at the fourth watch, with three county sheets in the fold of her coat against the three sheets that were not hers, and put them in beside the log in their order, Lowwater first, Hanhui, Shouyi, Wanzhou, with the date and the hour and the stamp described in wash code that only two people in the bound could read.

She wrote the four sentences out in full, which took the best part of an hour, and then she sat back on her heels and looked at what she had made.

Nothing in her life had ever been turned upside down in one night before. She had built her entire account of the world on the sentence *it reports, it does not advise*. Every month the air had said a harvest count and a quota and a name and she had taken the sentences at their plain value, because a thing that announces weather cannot also be setting a policy, because weather has no author.

It had an author. It had a room somewhere and a person in it and a set of words they were willing to put on, and the words were chosen, and the choosing was done differently for four counties, and the sentences that came out were all of them official and all of them audible and all of them, in the sense that mattered most, true.

That was the whole of it. The floor did not go out. What went out was the belief that true meant safe, and she had been carrying that belief since she was seven years old, and it was the largest thing she owned.

Six years. She put her hand flat on the closed log, on the shape of the folded sheet inside it. *Assessed, reassigned, unreconciled.* Feng had written that in a fourth column with no heading, and Feng had said that the air had said it, and Feng had spent twenty-two years writing down what the air said, and she believed him.

So the layer had said reassigned about a boy of thirteen. Which meant somebody, in a room, had written it. And it meant that four counties could hear it and four counties could not, and that it could be taken back out of three of them without a mark left behind.

If they could put it in, they could take it out.

Which meant that her brother's whole six-year sentence was a piece of furniture in somebody's office, and that the office had decided he was worth writing about, and that she had spent a winter walking into sealed rooms over a line in a ledger that somebody had chosen.

She put the log back in the lining and buttoned it and sat on the steps outside the tower with the cold coming up off the river and the copyists' lamp moving behind her.

“You knew this could happen,” she said, low, to her own collar, and there was nobody within nine feet of her and the watch had gone home. “Not this exactly. But you knew there were sentences going out of there and you never once said that somebody was choosing them.”

“I did not know.”

“Then answer me one question and answer it straight, because I am going to ask it properly in the morning and I want to know now whether the answer is the same.”

“Ask it.”

“Who writes them.”

The river went on under the bridge and the tower light burned behind her and the answer came back down the channel between them with the particular flatness of a thing that has checked.

“I do not know,” he said. “I have never known. That is not a thing I am hiding from you. There is a room and I am inside the room, and I have been inside it since I was nineteen and I have never once been shown who is in it with me.”
