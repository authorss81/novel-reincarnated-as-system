# The Count

The road out of Qinghe closes when the pass at the fourth bridge ices over, and it had been icing since the second hour of the morning, and she went down the nine steps at the fourth to be on the Lowwater side of it before dark, which was the last sensible hour she was going to get.

The snow had stopped by the time she reached the fourth step. What was left was grey and it had packed into the treads of the stone in a way that made the nine steps look like nine steps that had been left out in the weather, and the ash was still coming down on top of it, because it was the ninth day and there were two left, and she had counted every one of them since the fourth morning.

She had her own coat back. The sling came off on the seventh day, not because the arm had improved but because she had asked the steward to take it off and had looked at him until he did it. Her right hand came back about a third of the time now and she had stopped testing it more than twice a day.

Du Hong was on the fourth step with two pieces of paper in his hand and a coat that had not been made for the weather.

“Two of them came up the road this morning,” he said. “The lamp-mender out of Wanzhou and a woman off a cart out of Hanhui, and they were both asking after you at the gate, and neither of them would come up.”

“Why not.”

“They wanted to be asked,” said Du Hong, and handed the papers over.

She read them on the step in the grey light with the ash coming down on the paper.

The first said: *Assembly Hall of Qinghe. Ning Yan of the Qinghe furnace crew, servant-born. What was held is let go. What was taken is held.*

The second said: *Condensing, stage one, at Qinghe. A servant born of Qinghe furnace. What was released is released; what was taken is kept.*

“Same words in a different order,” she said.

“No, furnace crew.”

“It is the same words in a different order. The layer puts it in the order a body is moved in. That woman has it back to front and she has *kept* in there instead of *held*, and there is not one person in nine counties who knows there is an order, so there is not one person who could correct her.”

She folded both papers and put them in her coat.

Nine days. Three of them spent standing in front of a Bureau copyist saying eight words until he got them right, and the copyist had been the only man in nine counties who was ever going to get them right, and he had got them right on the sixth asking.

That was not carelessness, and on the sixth night she had not liked working it out. Everything she had learned about being counted had been about the sentence. This was about the copying. A thing copied four hundred times belongs to the ear that copies it, and the only part anybody will ever be able to check is a document that a person with authority has put a name to.

She had one of those now. She had signed it herself, four days ago, in a room with no roof.

“Furnace crew,” said Du Hong.

“Send these down.”

“I was going to ask.”

“The four county sheets are in my coat,” said Ning Yan. “The Hanhui one with the stamp on it, the Shouyi one with the hole where my name was, the Wanzhou one, and Lowwater's. I am going to have copies made in my own hand tonight and I want them at the Lantern Register tower at Lowwater inside a week, and I want them to arrive with nobody's name on them.”

“That is four sheets of county paper going down a mountain road in a coat.”

“Yes.”

“Anybody on that road can read a seal.”

“Anybody on that road can read a seal,” she agreed, “which is why they are going to be in my hand, and my hand cannot be read quickly, and I am going to write them so that a man who copies them out gets the wrong words unless he goes slowly.”

Du Hong looked at her for a moment.

“You have changed,” he said.

“I have got a document.”

“No,” said Du Hong, and did not say what else it was, and went down the steps with four sheets of county paper inside a coat that was not his.

## Four Hours

He was still not there.

She had known it since the ninth step an hour ago and she had not panicked about it and she was not going to. She sat down on the sixth step of the Nine Steps Terrace with her back against the cold stone, the two wrong sheets in her lap and the ash going down on her knees, and she did the only thing she had ever been able to do with a silence, which was to put a number on it.

She had three hours of it. She checked at the fourth hour and she checked at the fifth and it had not moved.

There was nothing wrong with her. She was not sealed. She was not hurt anywhere that had not been hurting for four months. The design was on a document in four counties and the account of her father had been struck in all four before dark on the fifth day, and her brother's record said one word in each of them, and the eight hundred jin claim was standing in the layer in every chest in the bound and had not changed, and she was as safe and as unsafe as she had been the night before.

She sat on the sixth step for the best part of three hours and was not frightened once, and she noticed that about herself with some interest. She had assumed that a silence from him would be the worst thing that could happen to her. The worst thing that had happened to her was four seconds on a floor four days ago, and she had been carrying that around since, and it weighed more than three hours of nothing.

He came back at about the fifth hour, with four hours and a half of the day gone out of the light.

He came back the way he had come back from the ninety seconds on the sixth day, which was with a difference in the room, and this time she felt the difference immediately, because it was the sound of somebody arriving who had already been busy for some time.

“Fifty-eight,” he said.

She waited.

“That is the answer to a question you have not asked yet,” he said. “Ask it.”

“What are you counting.”

“Fifty-eight,” he said, “and it has been one a day since the ninth night of the autumn, at the same hour, for fifty-eight days, and I have counted every one of them because I am a thing that counts and there was nothing else to do with my hands.”

She put her hands flat on the stone.

“Say the rest of it.”

“The rest of it is that I have never had a use for the number,” he said, “and I have had it since the third night, and I did not give it to you because there was no question for it, and because I told you in the autumn that it had been once a day since you were on the flats, and I believed that, and it is not true, and the number has been true the whole time and I was too pleased with the explanation to check it.”

“What is the number.”

“The number is fifty-eight days and the checking started on the first night.”

## The Arithmetic

She did it in about four seconds and it took her the rest of the afternoon.

Fifty-eight days. Once a day. Since the first night.

She had known him for fifty-eight days. He had been in there for six years, since the ninth night of the autumn, and she had made the arithmetic the way she made every arithmetic, in the flat voice, and then she sat on a cold step in grey snow and let the answer arrive.

The row had not started when she got there.

The row had been coming down past him at the same hour every day since before the first night she had ever spoken to him. There were nineteen hundred and something days of it in six years that she had never been in a position to notice, and one every day for fifty-eight days that she had, and he had filed all of them under *since you were on the flats* because it had been the best explanation available to him and he had been nineteen and dead and glad to be anything.

“It was not checking me,” said Ning Yan.

“No,” he said.

“It was checking the slot.”

“I do not know that,” he said, and then, immediately, in a different voice: “No. That is not right either, and I am going to say the other thing, and I am saying it because you have just asked me a number question and it is the only kind you have ever asked me and I owe you one straight.”

“Go on.”

“There is a place in this where the sentences are read after they go out,” he said. “Not read aloud. Looked at. The way you would look at an entry in a book to see whether the ink has run. And the thing that does it comes down the row once a day and it does not ask and it does not take, and it has never once missed, and for six years it has come down past a line with a shape in it and no name on it.”

He stopped.

“It has a name,” he said. “The office has a name. It is on the row and it is attached to the walking and it is the one thing in the whole of this that I have never once been able to read, and I have been looking for it for two days, and it is the only thing I cannot see.”

“Nobody can read the name of the office that reads the layer.”

“Nobody can read it except the office,” he said. “And I have spent four months believing I was the only one of us who could read anybody, and it turns out there is somebody who has been reading me once a day for six years, and I do not know what it is for, and neither do you.”

She sat on the sixth step of the Nine Steps Terrace with the ash going down on her knees and two pieces of paper in her lap that said her own life back to her in the wrong order, and she was not frightened of the eight hundred jin, and she was not frightened of four county offices, and she was not frightened of a Hall with no roof.

She was frightened of a number.

Fifty-eight. Once a day. Six years before her.

Whatever it was had been coming down that row since a nineteen-year-old furnace-man's son was burned out of a layer in the Qing furnace and woke up in it, and it had never asked him for anything and never taken anything from him, and it had looked at him every day of every one of those six years the way you look at a line in a book to see whether the ink has run, and he had spent six years believing he was a mistake in the weather.

He had not been a mistake in the weather. He had been a line in a book that somebody came past at the same hour every day.

And there was a name for the office. He could not read it. She could not read it. But it existed, and it was outside the bound, and in six hundred years it had looked at eleven Chosen Ones before her and there were seams at all eleven of them.

## Sixth Step

The pass at the fourth bridge had iced over before the sixth hour, which she knew it would. The road was shut for the night and would open for the market on the third day, and there was no version of her getting down that mountain tonight, and there had not been since about the second hour.

So she did not go down.

She sat on the sixth step of the Nine Steps Terrace through the whole of the last of the light, with her right arm out of its own weight against her knee, and the grey snow going into the creases of the stone, and the first of the two braziers lit at the north end of a building that had three walls and no roof, and the lamp on its pole.

She took the token out of her coat.

It was a plain iron thing the size of a thumb with three names cut into the back of it and nothing at all on the front, and it had been in her coat for four days and it was the only object in the world that had ever been handed to her for nothing, and every time she had put her hand on it she had wanted to put it down.

Three maintaining members. It meant three men who had got together and decided to spend paper on her, and every clause of it was a piece of paper she had signed in a room with no roof, and the word on her brother's record was on their paper and not on hers, and the eight hundred jin was in the layer and not in their book.

She turned it over twice and then did not put it back in her coat, and stood on the sixth step of the Nine Steps Terrace in the first snow of the season with an iron token in her left hand and nothing at all in the right, while the announcement she had made her went round nine counties in nine mouths and got the words wrong in seven of them.

Then she asked the question.

“Yuan Sheng.”

He had not told her not to use it again. She used it anyway, once, and she heard it go out into the cold the way she had heard it go out on a step five days before.

“What is it counting.”

He did not answer.

Not in the way of the four seconds on the floor, and not in the way of the three hours on a cold step, and not in the way of a man who has decided not to tell her. He had answered her question and given her a number and told her the worst thing anybody had told her in four months, and now he was doing the thing he did when there was something left to do and it was not talking.

He was counting.
