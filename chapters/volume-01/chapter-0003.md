# What It Took

The stages came in the night like knuckles cracking through a wall.

Ning Yan sat with her back to the tower stones after the kill, hands sticky, Du Hong's rope around the Ashjaw's jaw to keep the hinge true, and felt the first one go through her chest with a small unpleasant pop loud enough to hear through her own ribs. Heat raced down her arms. Her breath deepened without her asking it.

Lu Fen and Xie Qiao had gone off to make camp with the others up the line, but not before Lu Fen looked long at the jaw and said, “Plates get filed at the boundary,” in a voice that meant *plates get taken in the dark.*

Du Hong stayed. He built a windbreak from fallen slabs while she sat shaking, and pretended not to notice when her shoulders jerked with the second breakthrough an hour later, and the third near midnight.

“You trained through,” he whispered, envious and awed. “You held heat and it broke you open.”

She could not explain. The layer honoured the quest. That was the phrase the voice used, quiet and dazed with warmth.

“I hold. You grow. This is the trade.”

They kept watch in the way boys keep watch, which meant Du Hong slept sitting up with his hand on his knife and woke at every sound, and Ning Yan did not sleep at all. Han Shu came by once, at the turn of the night, to ask whether she had looked inside the Ashjaw.

“Why would I look inside it?”

“They say the stomach is lined with something.” Han Shu shifted from foot to foot. “Lu Fen says a sword-edge. He says you could take a whole set of Ashjaw teeth out of one and sell them in Lowwater.”

“Lu Fen says a lot of things at a volume.”

“He says it quieter now.” Han Shu scraped a boot. “He watched you do it. He went white.”

“He went white because he wanted it.”

Han Shu nodded, absorbing this, filing it. He was sixteen and still under the impression that things that happened to other people were opinions they could hold.

He went back to his slab of fallen stone with its dead root dragged across the mouth of the cavity, and lay down inside his own arrangement, and Ning Yan lay on her back with her hands on her sternum and listened to him breathe for a while because the sound of somebody else breathing was doing something to the inside of her chest she did not have a name for.

By second nightfall she stood at Body-Forging stage nine.

Six stages in a day and a night. Four months of practice would have bought her half a stage on the terrace edge. Now her blood moved like oil in winter sun, her bones felt poured full, her legs knew the Nine-Step like a song known by heart. She could feel the aperture in the centre of her chest, the place where qi pooled, whole and round and aching with fullness.

She was frightened and exultant and starving, all at once. She wanted to laugh and to hide.

The sixth stage came on like sunrise coming on. There was no crack and no heat. There was only an absence of resistance, and then she was on the other side of it, and the world had a colour it had not had before.

She got up to walk it off and the leg held.

It held for forty paces.

After that it began to fail on a schedule she could not predict, and she discovered that stage nine did not fix anything. It only made the failing faster, because the body now had more to lose. She walked the ring of the tower twice before she had to sit down, and she understood with a clarity that felt like a coin dropped down a well that the thing inside her head had not given her strength. It had given her a bigger body to have accidents in.

Han Shu found her sitting down and brought her water in a dented cup and did not ask.

“You are stage nine,” he said. “Nobody in this camp is stage nine.”

“Nobody in this camp knows it.”

“Nobody but me and Du Hong and Lu Fen.” Han Shu crouched, warming his hands. “That is three people, and two of them would sell it, and one of them told you about the sword-edge in the stomach.” He looked up. “I am telling you that you should not sleep behind a stone tonight.”

“Why?”

“Because I have been lying awake thinking about what happens to a boy who is short on four hands, and I have decided that I would rather be somewhere else.” He swallowed. “The inner tree line. Past the second water. Nobody goes past the second water on a quota run.”

“It is inside the boundary mark.”

“It is past the second water.”

The voice in her head had been silent for a long time. When it spoke it was careful.

“He is right. I see the sheath. I see nine of them, and I see the ground under it, and I do not like what the ground is doing.”

“Would you protect him?”

“I cannot touch anything.”

“Then do not tell me you can.”

“I am telling you I cannot.” A pause. “I am telling you this because I want you to know it, and I do not know why I want you to know it.”

She thought about that for a long time, sitting on the tower steps with stage nine pouring out of her like heat off a stove.

Then she said, out loud, so Han Shu would hear it too, “You take your slab. You take the root and the cup and you go back down to the boundary lamps and you sit inside the ring where the light reaches, and you do not move for anyone who is not a Team Elder. If Lu Fen comes, you give him the plate and you keep your hands.”

“The plate is yours.”

“The plate is under my robe and Lu Fen does not know that.” She held his eyes. “If he takes the plate off you, he has a lie, and a lie is a thing he has to carry. Go.”

Han Shu looked at her for a moment longer than she expected. Then he laughed, wetly, and said, “You are the worst thing that has happened to me this year,” and went to get his root.

They came for the plate after moonrise.

She heard them before she saw them, because she read shoulders even in the dark by the way feet placed weight. Lu Fen and Xie Qiao, knives out, moving around the windbreak with the particular care of boys who had told the others they went to piss.

“Hand over the jaw-plate,” Lu Fen said. “You filed nothing yet. We file together.”

Du Hong stood up fast. “It was her kill. I watched it.”

“You watched nothing,” Xie Qiao said. “You coiled rope.”

Ning Yan rose with her knife sheathed. She did not want to draw. At stage nine against stage five and stage four, she did not need to draw to win, and she knew it, and they knew it by the way she stood. The Nine-Step hummed in her calves, ready.

“Competing claims get settled at the boundary,” she said, keeping her voice low. “You knew the rule when you followed me back.”

Lu Fen's shoulders went high and forward. Decided. “Boundary settles what reaches it.”

He lunged.

She took it on the forearm. She had wanted his wrist, because a wrist is a thing you can break cheaply, but at stage nine everything she touched broke, and she had been braced for a blade that came in low and fast and went into the meat of her shoulder instead. The pain went up her arm and out through her teeth.

Lu Fen came on with his left hand flat, driving her back toward the tower, and she turned inside it with the turn the margin had given her, the one that put her small and turned his weight past her, and her elbow went into his nose. He went down on the stones and stayed down, holding his face, and Xie Qiao came in on her left with a blade held high in a two-handed grip like a boy who had been shown the grip once.

She did not see him come. The tower stone took the sound of his boots off her ears.

She saw the knife.

She had time to understand that the point of it was her throat, and that the point of her throat was a quarter inch below her jaw where a person is still a person, and that Xie Qiao was not trying to make a fight of it.

She put her forearm up.

Her arm folded. She felt the bone go. The blade came through and the point opened her shoulder from the outside to the bone in one clean stroke, and she went down on her knees on the tower stones with her arm in two pieces and a noise coming out of her that she did not know she was making.

And in her head, in the flat present voice that never hesitated, something said:

“I have four feet of stage-nine left. I am going to spend all of it on the eight words below. I cannot stop the knife with what I have. I can stop the knife with what I take out of you.”

She said, out loud, “Tell me the words.”

“You already know the words. You will not like them.”

“Speak them.”

“Yuan Sheng, aged nineteen, died six years in the Qing furnace. My Settlement is one part in ninety-one and rising. If I spend what is left, I stop being settled and the layer takes me back into the machinery. You will feel that happen. You will not get the rest of me back.”

He did not say: *and your body pays.*

She looked at Xie Qiao's forearm and at the ten inches of air between the blade and her throat and understood, the way a person understands that a hand on the back of the neck is not wind, that the price was hers and not his, and that the price was whatever he took out of her, and that nobody had thought to tell her the things he could take would be things nobody could notice being gone.

“Do it,” Ning Yan said, to her own knees, in the dark, with her arm in two pieces and a boy coming at her with a knife.

“Do you understand what you are saying.”

“No. Do it.”

The machine took her.

There was no light and no sound. What there was was a door being closed, quickly and without any opinion about it, on a place she had not known she was standing in. Cold went in through the floor of her chest and found the stored place, the one where the heat she had spent four months of her life making lived, and it did not fight for it. It simply took it out of her while the smell of hot iron came up through her sinuses.

She came back into her body about two breaths later and Xie Qiao was sitting on the stones eight feet away holding the wrist that had been holding the knife, with his own hand broken on the inside in a way that a hand does not usually end up. Lu Fen was on his knees at the edge of the firelight looking at his hands, and then he got up and ran, and did not stop running until he was gone.

The knife had stopped a finger's width from her skin, and where the light was going strange and soft she could see the edge of it, and her throat was still closed and still whole.

She lay down on the stones. She lay down on the stones because her legs had stopped taking instructions, and she put her good hand against the front of her robe to see whether anything was still in there, and what was in there was not what she had brought to the tower.

The room where the Nine-Step had been was dark.

She went for it the way you go for a door in the dark in a house you know. Step four. Step four. She had it, she had it, it was *turn inside, you are small, be smaller* —

It was not there.

It was not there and it was not somewhere else. It was *gone*, taken out at the root. What was left in her legs was the shape of it the way a riverbed is left after the water goes down. She lay on the stones with her face in the ash and made a sound that she had never made before.

Du Hong caught her before her face hit the stones. He shouted for help. Boots ran. Someone pressed cloth to her front. All through it, at the edge of everything she could still hold, she heard that the camp had gone quiet on the wrong side of the tower, where a boy she had sent to the boundary lamps was not making any noise at all. The cold ate everything.

She woke at false dawn with her chest bound tight and her mouth full of iron.

Pain sat on her like a stone. She counted breaths to keep from crying out. Twenty-two to the minute, shallow. The aperture in her chest felt wrong, smaller, rough at the rim, as if a cup had been emptied and chipped in the emptying.

She reached inside herself the way cultivators did, feeling for stage, for ground.

Body-Forging stage four.

Not nine. Four. Ten months of practice gone, four of them hers and six of them somebody else's gift. Five stages burned out of her in the time a blade took to fall. The strength she had carried at moonrise had been hollowed from underneath, and what remained stood on a foundation with pieces missing.

She lay still and understood, without being told, that the thing that had settled into the voice had been spent, and that it had not been spent from the sky.

Her hands shook. She tried once more to find the Nine-Step. Habit twitched in her thighs, useless without the head to lead it. The technique was gone and could not be relearned, because there was nothing left to relearn from. The manual was ash years ago. The boy who copied it was dead.

Du Hong slept sitting against the windbreak, her blood on his sleeve. Lu Fen and Xie Qiao were gone from camp, fled up the line with a broken wrist between them and a story they would never tell true.

The voice was quiet. Too quiet. Where last night it had hummed, now it sat low and dim, like a lamp turned down to the last quarter inch of wick.

*What did it take,* she thought. Her throat closed. She forced it out aloud, hoarse, to the grey air. “What did it take?”

Silence.

She waited. Snow settled on the rope coil. Somewhere up the line someone coughed. The tower stones ticked as they cooled.

He did not answer.

Ning Yan stared at the sky lightening over the ashwoods and felt partnership turn into accounting inside her chest, cold and clean as a ledger line. He had spent her without her consent. He had saved her life with her own months. She kept breathing, shallow and fast, and with each breath the silence shaped itself into the first thing she wanted to break.

She would find out herself.

She pushed up on one elbow, teeth sunk in her lip, and began to live with the scar.
