# What It Took

The stages came in the night like knuckles cracking through a wall.

Ning Yan sat with her back to the tower stones after the kill, hands sticky, Du Hong's rope around the Ashjaw's jaw to keep the hinge true, and felt the first one go through her chest with a small unpleasant pop loud enough to hear through her own ribs. Heat raced down her arms. Her breath deepened without her asking it.

Lu Fen and Xie Qiao had gone off to make camp with the others up the line, but not before Lu Fen looked long at the jaw and said, “Plates get filed at the boundary,” in a voice that meant *plates get taken in the dark.*

Du Hong stayed. He built a windbreak from fallen slabs while she sat shaking, and pretended not to notice when her shoulders jerked with the second breakthrough an hour later, and the third near midnight.

“You trained through,” he whispered, envious and awed. “You held heat and it broke you open.”

She could not explain. The layer honoured the quest. That was the phrase the voice used, quiet and dazed with warmth.

“I hold. You grow. This is the trade.”

By second nightfall she stood at Body-Forging stage nine. Six stages in a day and a night. Four months of practice would have bought her half a stage on the terrace edge. Now her blood moved like oil in winter sun, her bones felt poured full, her legs knew the Nine-Step like a song known by heart. She could feel the aperture in the centre of her chest, the place where qi pooled, whole and round and aching with fullness.

She was frightened and exultant and starving, all at once. She wanted to laugh and to hide.

They came for the plate after moonrise.

She heard them before she saw them, because she read shoulders even in the dark by the way feet placed weight. Lu Fen and Xie Qiao, knives out, moving around the windbreak with the particular care of boys who had told the others they went to piss.

“Hand over the jaw-plate,” Lu Fen said. “You filed nothing yet. We file together.”

Du Hong stood up fast. “It was her kill. I watched it.”

“You watched nothing,” Xie Qiao said. “You coiled rope.”

Ning Yan rose with her knife sheathed. She did not want to draw. At stage nine against stage five and stage four, she did not need to draw to win, and she knew it, and they knew it by the way she stood. The Nine-Step hummed in her calves, ready.

“Competing claims get settled at the boundary,” she said, keeping her voice low. “You knew the rule when you followed me back.”

Lu Fen's shoulders went high and forward. Decided. “Boundary settles what reaches it.”

He lunged.

What happened next stayed broken in her memory ever after, in pieces that did not fit. Xie Qiao going left to pin Du Hong. Du Hong shouting. Her own body moving through step three, step five, turning inside Lu Fen's sweep the way the margin said, her knife coming up to parry rather than kill because she did not want a death in the dark that neither of them would admit to.

Then Xie Qiao broke from Du Hong and came in behind her while she bound Lu Fen's blade, and the night went white at the edge.

She saw the killing stroke coming. A downward cut aimed at the join of neck and shoulder, meant to open her throat to the chest. At stage nine she was fast enough to see it and not fast enough to stop it, her weight committed wrong, her legs tangled in step seven, Du Hong too far, the tower stone too close.

She thought, with absurd clarity, *this is where I die, four hands whole and unfiled.*

The voice in her head screamed without sound.

Something tore.

Not in the air. In her. In the centre of her chest where the aperture sat round and full, and deeper, in the foundation under it, the months of practice, the heat held past comfort, the sequence of the steps as written in her head. Cold poured through her like water through a cracked jar. The world lurched half a step sideways, qi answering her all at once, far too fast, far too much, and Xie Qiao's blade stopped a finger's width from her skin as if it had struck a wall of glass.

The blade turned. Xie Qiao screamed and fell back clutching his wrist, bones broken by his own force rebounding. Lu Fen stared, dropped his knife, ran three steps and fell over his own feet in the snow.

Ning Yan stood in the sudden quiet with blood running down her front.

The cut had not landed. But something had opened her anyway, from sternum to hip, a line of fire that was not a knife wound and was. She pressed her hands to it and felt skin part under her palms, hot and slick. Her legs gave. She went down sideways into the ash-snow, and the Nine-Step went with her, pouring out of her head like water off a table.

She tried to catch step four. Nothing. Step one. Nothing. The shape of the turn, the margin note, the charcoal footwork on the laundry manual's back page. Gone. Her body remembered the weight-shift, her calves twitched with it, but the knowing was gone, the sequence, the order, the names of the steps. An empty room where a house had been.

Du Hong caught her before her face hit the stones. He shouted for help. Boots ran. Someone pressed cloth to her front. The cold ate everything.

She woke at false dawn with her chest bound tight and her mouth full of iron.

Pain sat on her like a stone. She counted breaths to keep from crying out. Twenty-two to the minute, shallow. The aperture in her chest felt wrong, smaller, rough at the rim, as if a cup had been emptied and chipped in the emptying.

She reached inside herself the way cultivators did, feeling for stage, for ground.

Body-Forging stage four.

Not nine. Four. Four months of practice gone. Five stages burned out of her in the time a blade took to fall. The strength she had carried at moonrise had been hollowed from underneath, and what remained stood on a foundation with pieces missing.

She lay still and understood, without being told, that the Merit that had settled into the voice had been spent, and that it had not been spent from the sky.

Her hands shook. She tried once more to find the Nine-Step. Habit twitched in her thighs, useless without the head to lead it. The technique was gone and could not be relearned, because there was nothing left to relearn from. The manual was ash years ago. The boy who copied it was dead.

Du Hong slept sitting against the windbreak, her blood on his sleeve. Lu Fen and Xie Qiao were gone from camp, fled up the line with a broken wrist between them and a story they would never tell true.

The voice was quiet. Too quiet. Where last night it had hummed, now it sat low and dim, like a lamp turned down.

*What did it take,* she thought. Her throat closed. She forced it out aloud, hoarse, to the grey air. “What did it take?”

Silence.

She waited. Snow settled on the rope coil. Somewhere up the line someone coughed. The tower stones ticked as they cooled.

He did not answer.

Ning Yan stared at the sky lightening over the ashwoods and felt partnership turn into accounting inside her chest, cold and clean as a ledger line. He had spent her without her consent. He had saved her life with her own months. She kept breathing, shallow and fast, and with each breath the silence shaped itself into the first thing she wanted to break.

She would find out herself.

She pushed up on one elbow, teeth sunk in her lip, and began to live with the scar.
