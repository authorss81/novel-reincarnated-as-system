# Three Days Visible

She refused the room.

There were four things on offer by the end of the first day — a cell in the inner terrace, two rooms off the Hall's antechamber, a bed in the Hall's second room, and a bed in the Elder quarter over the furnace — and she said no to all four in eleven sentences, and the reason she gave, which was true, was that she had been indoor in this sect for six weeks and every single one of those weeks had been a room somebody had put her in.

So she went back down to the crew quarters and the second bunk, and the washing crew made a place for her in the corner and nobody spoke about it.

The fever was gone. That was the strangest thing that had happened to her in four months. She had been running at the height of a bad cold since the second day past and on the first morning after the trial she woke up clear-headed, with a chest that went down eleven inches further than it had the day before.

Her right arm was gone. Not numb. It was there and it was the right size and it took instructions about half the time and did not report back at all.

She was stronger than she had ever been and she had lost the use of one arm, and no clerk in nine counties could put those two facts on the same page.

The ash was thinner. It had been the heaviest on the fourth day and by the sixth it had gone to a grey grain that hung rather than fell, and it was still on the sill, in the lye, on the backs of the washing crew, and it would be for five more days.

## Three Names in a Day

By the third hour of the first afternoon she had been spoken to by people from three directions, and by the third hour of the second day she understood three to be the actual number.

None of them were enemies and that was the difficulty.

Lu Kang's three sent a clerk at the second hour with the sheet and the ink and two men in Hall coats for an escort, and the clerk read the terms aloud in the anteroom and then asked for the hour, and she said the hour would be given in that building after the fifth day, and he wrote it down and left.

The outer disciples sent nobody. They came — eleven of them over two days, on the pretext of things that were not pretexts — and every one of them wanted something out of the door.

The furnace crew were the third, and they were not coming to her at all, which she understood was worse. She ate with them at the second shift and nobody said one word about the trial for four days, and they were not being cold, they were being careful. She worked out why on the second night: a Condensing stage one who was servant-born was not a triumph for her. It was a question about every one of the nine hundred people who had come up to stage nine in that sect under a sponsor's writ.

“Du Hong,” she said, finding him at the rail with a rope over his shoulder. “Who is talking.”

He looked at his board for a while.

“Half the outer yard thinks you are a prodigy and a quarter thinks you are a trick and the rest has not decided. The crew thinks you should not have been sealed. The Hall thinks you should not have been on the floor. The Elders in the second gallery think it was the most useful thing that has happened to that building in forty years, and I do not think any of them is lying.”

“The accounts.”

“The accounts,” said Du Hong, “are the part you need to worry about.”

## Four Offices

She found out on the second morning, from a clerk of the Hall's own table who had brought her a sealed copy of something she had asked for four months ago and had not asked for since.

Her father's account had been opened again.

It was not one account any more.

The furnace column of Qinghe held one line: Ning Bo, furnace-man, unsettled, balance carried forward six years, credit held. She had heard that line read aloud in the outer yard in the middle of the winter and had stood in the crowd and been nothing in it.

There were four copies of it in four counties, because the announcement had gone out to four counties and each had written it down in its own book, and each of the four books had opened the account again to add a line, and the line each of them had added was the same.

*Credit. Condensing, stage one. Held.*

And underneath, in four different hands, the furnace column had done the only thing it had ever known how to do, which was to bill the account it held against the nearest unspent thing.

Her father had been billed twice more.

“Twice in six years,” said the clerk, reading over her shoulder, which he was not supposed to do. “Once when she advanced to seven. Once now. It will be entered as two years carried.”

“Carried against what.”

“Against the household,” said the clerk. “It does not matter. It has never mattered. That is the entire function of the column.”

He put the four sheets down and looked at her a second longer than a clerk looks at anybody, and then took them away again, because he had decided how much of his life he was going to spend.

She sat in the crew quarters with a sheet of ash on the sill and understood the shape of the trade that had been made for her four seconds on a cold step.

If she had been invisible there would have been one account and one clerk and one line. Because she was visible, the same advancement had happened once and been entered four times, and four counties had billed her father for it twice each, and there were now four books in nine counties with a line about her and a debt attached to her family, and the debt followed the household for three generations.

Being famous had cost her father eight more years.

She wrote it in the log that night in her own poor hand with the wash code inside it, because she had learned in one afternoon that this would be the sort of thing that got denied in four years.

## The Claim

It came at the second hour of the third day.

[Inside the bound, a claim is entered against the Chosen One. The bound will honour a claim against the Chosen One. It has not had one before.]

Nobody outside the sect heard it differently from any other announcement. Inside the sect, two hundred people stopped what they were doing, and a woman dropped a pot, and Ning Yan sat on a bunk with the sentence going round inside her chest the way the advancement had.

There was a price on the Chosen One. It was not a large one — eight hundred jin, which was a furnace crew's year — and it was to be paid to whoever brought proof, and proof was defined in the announcement itself, in the layer's own flat grammar, as a body and a place and a name, all three.

“It did not use your name,” he said, from further away than usual. “It said the Chosen One. It is the first claim in the history of the bound and it does not know your name and it does not need your name.”

“Take it back.”

“No.”

“Take it back.”

“I cannot. The designation holds whether you speak to me or not and whether you want it or not, and you told me that in the middle of the winter and you were right. I cannot withdraw a claim from the inside of a thing that does not ask me.” He was quiet. “And it is not me. I want that understood first, before anything else. I did not do this.”

“You made me one.”

“Yes,” he said. “I did.”

She did not shout. There was nobody in the room to shout at and the woman with the pot had already picked it up.

She sat there for a minute and a half with her left hand on her knee and worked through it the way she worked through everything, and what came out at the end was not fury, and she was faintly disappointed about that.

Nobody else in nine counties had ever heard of a Chosen One. There was a price on one now, and it was eight hundred jin, which was the price of a furnace-crew year, which meant it had been set at a level a furnace man could pay — so either the machinery of the bound priced a Chosen One the way it priced anything else, or somebody had entered that claim knowing exactly who could afford it.

Both of those were frightening. She wrote the number down.

## What Believing Is Worth

Three people came up the nine steps in three days who said that they believed her, and she worked out what each of them wanted inside ninety seconds, which was the fastest she had ever read a room and the least satisfying.

A laundress of about sixty came with a covered jar and a boy of nine who should have been at his lessons. Her boy had been in a household whose account had been struck in a room by two men four years ago, and she had known it was not the sect's doing, because the sect's doing comes with a form and this had come with nothing at all.

An inner disciple of about twenty-five wanted to be introduced to her, and then wanted his application for the outer terrace to carry her name.

A man from Hanhui had walked eleven days to ask her to write down what she had seen on an Assembly floor so that he could carry it back and have it read in a market square.

She did not doubt any of them. They believed her the way the outer disciples believed her, with their whole chest, and every one of them had come up nine steps in the ash to get something out of the fact that she was worth something.

And then the laundress said the thing that turned it over.

“Nine of us are left out of that household and nine of us are in this wash house, and in three days nine of us have been asked by strangers whether it was true,” she said. “And three of the nine have stopped answering their doors.”

She put the jar down on the bunk.

“They believed you before you came,” she said. “That is the part I cannot get past. They believed you for four years with nothing to look at, and now there is a thing to look at, and half of them would rather you had not come up the steps at all.”

Being believed was a resource. She had spent four months learning that being looked at was free and being counted was the thing worth having. Believing was worth more than both, because it was the only one of the three that could be spent.

She used it. She wrote eight words on the back of a laundry docket in her own poor hand, wash code inside learner's strokes, and gave it to the laundress and asked her to take it down the Lowwater road on market day and leave it at the door of the Lantern Register tower with nobody's name on it.

“What is it?”

“Eight words out of a sentence that nine people will have nine versions of by the end of the week.”

The laundress looked at her for three seconds and then said, “Right,” and put it inside her coat.

It was the exact sentence the layer had said, in the order the layer had said it, with the two words in the middle of it where they belonged.

## Rage

The rage came on the third afternoon and it came over a copyist.

A Bureau man had come up to Qinghe with the season's returns and a list of things to certify, and one of them was the announcement of the fourth morning past, because a thing announced in four counties had to be entered in four county books correctly, and the Bureau was unwilling to take a clerk's word for it.

So they brought her a sheet, and a chair, and a man with a good hand, and asked her to say it again.

She said it five times.

The first time he wrote it down cleanly and asked her to confirm. The second time he got a word wrong and asked her to confirm again. The third time he wrote *servant born*, two words, with the whole weight of a century sitting on a hyphen, and asked her to say it once more.

The fourth time she said it and he asked her whether the third word was *furnace* or *funeral*.

She put her left hand flat on the table.

It was not a loud thing. It was a servant's hand going down flat on a Bureau table from four inches out, with the weight of the arm behind it, in a room with one door. The copyist — thirty-five, grey in his beard, a very good hand — went back about two feet in his chair and said, in a voice that had gone up half a tone, “I am doing the work as it is given to me.”

“I know what you are doing.”

“I have nineteen counties of returns and I have been given this one to certify, and I cannot certify what I have not heard, and now I have heard it five times, and I will hear it a sixth if that is required, and then I will write what you say and I will write it exactly. I would be obliged if you would not put your hand on my table.”

She took her hand off the table.

“Sixth time,” she said. “And then it is done, and then you go down the mountain, and then somebody in Hanhui writes it a seventh time with his ears and gets it wrong, and that is not your fault and it is not mine either, but it is going to be in a book that outlives both of us. So write it right.”

He wrote it right.

She sat there afterwards with the anger still going round in her chest and understood that it was the first straight thing she had felt since the fourth morning, and that it had come out of a copyist's hand rather than out of anything that had been done to her, and that this was apparently the arrangement.

## The Coat

He came and found her on the sixth step about an hour after the copyist had gone down the mountain. She had been waiting for him since the second morning without admitting it to herself.

Du Hong. The inner coat, worn since the winter session, the cloth gone the colour of a thing that is washed in a wash house. Eighteen, and holding a rope.

She had had the sentence ready since the trial and had not said it in four days, because every time she got to the place where she would have said it, he was carrying something.

“You were sent to report me.”

He stopped on the step.

“By the Hall. In writing. To an inner disciple who was supposed to have wanted it. I saw it before the trial and I have never once said a word about it in any room.”

“You have not.”

“No,” she said. “And that is what I have been doing to you for a fortnight. You were handed a woman to report and you came back with nothing, and every office in this sect that opens your file finds it, and nobody has asked you why, because I have never mentioned it out loud.”

“Why have you not mentioned it.”

“Because you told me at the well-house that you would not go back to being nobody, and that me asking would take it from you.” Her voice went for about a second and came back. “And then I could not say it. Every week I did not say it I bought the same silence again, and it was the only thing I owned that nobody in this sect knew I owned.”

Du Hong looked out at the weather.

“They will come for it now,” he said. “For you, not for the report. There is a price on you this morning and every man on that road knows it, and I am the one man on that road who has been inside your business for four months. That is not a thing you can hold open.”

“I know.”

“Then say whatever it is you are going to make me do.”

“I am going to put it on the record,” she said. “All of it. That you were advanced on my recommendation, and that you were sent to report me, and that you did not, and that I have known it since before the trial and have entered nothing until now. My hand. The hour.”

“That is the one thing in this sect nobody can use you on afterwards.”

“Yes.”

“Your name goes in a file with mine in it. A claim at eight hundred jin does not want you in a building with no roof. It wants a person who has been inside your business for four months.”

“Yes,” she said. “If you are not on my page, Du Hong, you are the cheapest walk in the bound. If you are on my page with my hand under your name, then everybody who opens you opens me, and I am expensive.”

Snow that was not white went sideways across the terrace between them.

“You are angry,” he said.

“Yes.”

“At me.”

“No,” she said. “At you, at the Hall, at the whole of them in that room who watched me get struck five times and then went home and slept. Mostly at you.”

“All right,” said Du Hong.

That was the entire interview. She went on down the steps with the anger still going round inside her and the taste of a thing she had been carrying since before the trial gone clean out of her mouth, and it did not feel like winning either.

## The Price of It

She went back up the steps at the eighth hour of the third day and sat on the sixth step where she had sat three days before and looked at the gap where the Assembly Hall used to have a roof.

The timber had been cleared off the street and stacked squared and counted along the south wall, because timber is timber and Qinghe would need it in the spring. Two braziers burned in the open at the north end where the wall used to be. Somebody had hung a lamp on a pole. The hall was still recognisably a hall. It just had weather in it now.

The announcement had been going round nine counties for three days, and Du Hong had read her four counties' versions at the second shift, out of a man who had heard one in a market square at Lowwater and three from the lamp-menders.

Two of them were right.

One had her at the Broken Kiln. One had her coming out of the Ashwood Verge in the autumn. Both of those were things that had not happened to her and were things that had happened to somebody.

That was not carelessness. She had worked it out on the first night and had taken until the second to admit it. A thing spoken once, heard by nine counties, and written down by four hundred people is not a record. It is a rumour with a heading on it, and the heading is the only part anybody checks.

And in every one of those four books there was one more line, and it was the reason she had come up the steps.

*The Chosen One is claimed. Eight hundred jin.*

The claim was the one thing about her that was not about Qinghe at all. It was not in the sect's book. It had no form, no office behind it, no clerk and no seal. It was the first claim in the history of the bound against the Chosen One, and it was standing in the same sentence, on the same morning, in the same four books, as the credit held by Ning Bo, furnace-man, who was going to be billed twice more for the advancement of a girl who could not feel her right hand.

The protection and the danger were not two things. They were the same event, written on the same line by the same machinery, and there was no version of her life from here in which she got one without the other.

She went down the nine steps sideways in the ash, on the left, with her right arm in the sling, because on the fifth day there was going to be a signing in a building with no roof, and the account of Ning Bo was open in four counties, and a word that had never existed in any of them was going to be written into all four at once by a clerk with a good hand.

She did not look at the Hall.
