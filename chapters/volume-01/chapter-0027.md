# Shen Xiu's Price

The lower terrace after night drill held fog and lamp smoke.

Ning Yan found Shen Xiu by the rail where the team lists went up, alone, reading names by lamplight with his shoulders bowed. He was a tired man with a quota, not a villain, and she had watched him carry half of Pei Lan's weight two miles without being asked. Her goal was plain. She needed to know what he wanted before the Elder Hall decided it for him.

Resistance was his decency. He looked up, saw her, and did not pretend surprise.

“You reached nine,” he said. “I watched the flats. You worked like somebody pursued you.”

“I was pursued by winter,” she said. “You posted the drill order. My name sat near the bottom.”

“It sat where the Bureau left it.” He rolled the list, tied it. His hands were chapped, nails black. “You came to ask what I decided about you.”

She leaned on the rail beside him, not facing him, the way servants talked when the words mattered. Cold bit through her coat. The tablet pressed against skin. The ledger pressed opposite.

“You looked at me twice at weighing,” she said. “You marked something and then you blotted it.”

He was quiet a long while. Fog moved through lamplight. Below, Lowwater lights showed faint. Somewhere up the hill a door shut, and a clerk coughed the way Feng coughed, wet and deep, and the sound carried in cold air.

She thought of his son, unseen, coughing blood last winter into a basin while his father marked team lists. She thought of her own mother coughing into wash water for a year before the wet lungs took her. Two houses with the same sound in them, divided by rank and joined by it.

“They offered me an inner place,” he said at last. Past tense, heavy, honest about being tired. “Not for me. For my son. A clerkship. Warm work. He coughed blood last winter. They said a servant-born with a seal on her would be worth noticing, and my noticing would be remembered.”

Her stomach dropped. A seal on her. The tablet. The log. Somebody had seen, or guessed, or been told.

“You were told to turn me in,” she said.

“I was told to report anything unreconciled.” He looked at his hands. “I had already decided to do it before you walked up. I would like to be allowed to not be told why. I am tired, and my boy breathed blood, and I did not want a reason to refuse them.”

Disappointment moved through her, clean and adult. She had hoped for a different arrangement. She had no time to be disappointed, and she was anyway.

She offered him the other arrangement regardless. She took out a folded sheet, a copy of the log's first page in her own poor hand, wash code hidden inside learner's strokes, and held it out.

“Took a copy for you,” she said. “You held it and you decided later. You kept it folded. Nobody needed to know you read it.”

He stared at the paper. He did not take it.

“You made me stupid,” he said, almost gentle. “You stood here and tried to make me a man who hid papers for girls. I carried your stretcher. I left your loss line open. I did those things because they were mine to do. This is not mine.”

She let her hand drop. She wanted to beg and did not beg. Begging would have made him cheaper, and he was not cheap. He was a decent man in a position where anything he did was a decision about her.

“You reported already?” she asked.

“Not yet.” He tied the list tighter. “The Hall meets on scheduling in three days. I report then. That was the agreement.”

She nodded. She understood agreements. She had made one in a sealed room with nobody listening, and one on the flats with a voice, and one with paper.

“I was never angry at you,” she said. “I was disappointed and I had no time to be.”

He looked at her then, really looked, stage nine girl with winter in her coat, carrying something he had agreed not to ask after.

“Stayed away from the furnace,” he said. “You heard nothing I said tonight.”

She folded the refused copy back into her lining. There was no version of this where a kind man was spared. His son breathed blood. Her brother's line read reassigned. The Hall paid in clerkships and collected in girls.

She left him at the rail with his list and walked up toward the terrace where Elder Qiu had been seen asking after her by name.

The drill stood done. The report stood promised. What came between would decide everything.
