# A Fair Hand

Lowwater at dusk smelled of lamp-oil and wet wool and the river.

Ning Yan came down the mountain road on the sixth evening with Du Hong and a stretcher-party, Pei Lan fever-light behind them, the record plate and the boundary mark wrapped in oilcloth inside her robe. The town hunched along the water in two rows of stone houses, shutters closed against ash, and above it rose the Lantern Register tower, a narrow finger of mortared rock with a lamp-room at the top that burned day and night.

She left the party at the weigh-station and climbed to the tower alone, because what she wanted copied was hers and not the sect's. Her scar pulled with each step. Stage five carried her, thin but steady.

The keeper opened to her knock after a long coughing fit on the other side of the door. Feng Lushi was a consumptive clerk in his forties, thin to the bone, ink under his fingernails, a brush-case worn smooth at his belt. Twenty-two years of announcements in his fair hand, copied nightly onto rice paper and sent upward to something he never named. The job was eating him; she saw it in the blood on his sleeve, the way he leaned on the doorframe to stay upright.

“You were from the run,” he said. “You carried a plate.”

She bowed. “I hoped to have it copied before I lost it in the dirt.”

He let her in. The lamp-room was warm, impossibly warm after the Verge, lined with shelves of bound paper, every spine dated in the same beautiful hand. The air tasted of oil and lung-blood and brush-ink. On the desk lay the night's sheet, half finished, the characters for *harvest thirty-three* still wet.

Her throat closed to see it written. Someone had written it. It was real on paper, not just in air.

Feng Lushi followed her gaze and coughed into his fist. “You heard that one,” he said. “Everyone heard that one. I wrote what I heard. Nineteen Listeners inside the bound heard the same. The job eats all of us.”

She laid out her pine splinter and the boundary mark and the tower tally. Her hands shook. “I wanted proof I was where I was,” she said. “Lu Fen filed a competing claim. I was stage four and lame. No one believed a lame girl cleared a sheath.”

He did not argue with the voice. That was the thing she remembered all her life. She told him, halting, about the voice that counted breaths, about the quest with a stated cost, about the five stages gone in a night, and he asked what the voice said, simply, the way a clerk asks for a date, and wrote it down in his fair hand on rice paper.

That was the first time anything about her had ever been written by anyone who believed her.

“There are nineteen Listeners,” he murmured, brush moving. “We hear. We write. We die. No one reads it back except the Bureau.” He looked up, eyes bright with fever. “I never heard of you before this week. Now I have.”

She gave him the tower's record plate to copy and kept the original, which was the first thing she ever planned to keep. He copied quickly, beautifully, his cough shaking the desk but never the line.

Then he turned the copy over and frowned. “Your brother's kiln record,” he said. “You asked after Ning Wu.”

Her heart stopped. “I asked nothing—”

“You carry it,” he said gently. “In your shoulders. Everyone who comes here carries someone.” He drew a second sheet toward him. “The kiln closed his tally last winter. But the copy I made—” He checked the date on her tower plate against his memory. “The date on your plate is after his kiln record was closed. Do you understand? Your tower stood and was marked after they said the kiln shut.”

She stared at the date in his fair hand. After. The tower's tally, chalked by a hand that must have been alive to chalk it, dated after her brother's file was shut and filed away.

What her brother took with him into the Kiln, where he went, whether the *assessed* stamp meant what the sect said — all of it cracked open in that one date.

She walked out into the Lowwater dusk with a name she never had to trade for, held in paper, and did not know what to do with her face because she had been believed.

Behind her, Feng Lushi coughed blood into his sleeve and wrote her name once more in the margin, to remember it.
