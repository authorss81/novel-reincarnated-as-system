# Wen Again

Lowwater two days later smelled of rain and lamp-oil.

Ning Yan came down the mountain road with leave for oil-buying and a plate of fired skin against her ribs that had not cooled in two days. She wore it under her inner robe now, directly against her skin, because cloth between felt like lying. It was never comfortable. It moved when she moved, warm, faintly damp, a weight that breathed with her.

Her goal was plain. Have the tablet read by somebody who handled one before.

She found Wen in the lamp-mender’s yard behind the Lowwater stables, working a lamp-housing with oil to the elbows. The younger mate was gone. Chickens moved through ash. A child carried wicks.

Wen looked up, saw Ning Yan’s face, saw the shape under her coat, and stepped back.

“No,” Wen said. “I do not touch it.”

Not fear. Disgust, professional and specific, the look of a mender shown a lamp fouled beyond cleaning.

Ning Yan stopped with her hands at her sides. “I need to know what it is.”

“I told you not to take anything.” Wen wiped her hands, then wiped them again. “You took. I smell the kiln on you. Put it down and I burn my yard.”

“I cannot put it down.” Ning Yan kept her voice low, past tense, steady. “My brother was assessed. His line says reassigned. This was on the table. I need somebody who knows to tell me what it holds.”

Wen stared at the shape under the coat. “You want me to read it.”

“I want you to tell me what a tablet is made of.”

Silence stretched. The child with wicks went inside. Wen set down her tools with care, the way people set down things they do not want to throw.

“A tablet is made of the people it describes,” Wen said at last. “Core-stone is not stone. It is fired qi, and the kiln’s output is partly made out of the assessed. The stalled are opened. The useful part is moved. What is left is fired with clay to hold the record. That is why the surface feels like skin. It is skin, after firing, after everything else was taken.”

Ning Yan stood very still. A thing she thought was evidence turned out to be a piece of a person. The warmth against her ribs was not imagination. It was residue, held heat, a record that remembered being alive.

She wanted to put it down. She wanted to drop it in the yard dust and walk away and wash her hands in the river until they bled. Instead she did the harder thing.

She did not put the tablet down. She adjusted it inside her coat, against her skin, and dealt with what that felt like later. Revulsion rose and she let it cool until it was useful, the way she let anger cool.

Wen watched her do it and something in her face changed, disgust giving way to reluctant respect.

“You wear it,” Wen said. “Against skin. You know what that means to the order.”

“I know it means I carry somebody.” Ning Yan met her eyes. “I carried Pei Lan. I carried Han Shu’s boots. I carry this now.”

Wen shook her head. “I will not touch it. Not for oil. Not for paper. The order does not handle kiln output. We mend lamps. We do not mend people.” She picked up her tools. “Keep it hidden. The night watch checks coats on the Lowwater turn. If they find kiln clay on a sect disciple, it is a capital matter. Do you hear me?”

“I hear you.”

“Then go. And do not bring it here again.”

Ning Yan bowed and left the yard with the tablet warmer than before, as if being named heated it. She walked the Lowwater street with her hands folded over it, stage eight steady, sick and clear.

Yuan spoke as she reached the milestone, gentle, present.

“I am with you. I carry with you. I do not touch.”

She leaned against the milestone until the nausea passed. Evidence she would now have to hold rather than show. A piece of a person, worn against her skin for the rest of the volume, never comfortable.

She had to decide who saw it, and there was one person she had not tested. One voice in her head who could touch through her hand, who had been saving it.

She turned her face toward Qinghe and started up the mountain.
