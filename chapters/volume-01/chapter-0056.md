# The Signature

The Assembly Hall of Qinghe had no roof and it was the fourth day of the fifth day and the signing was at the second hour because there were two braziers in it now and the light in it was better before noon than after.

Forty-one people were in it. Nine clerks at a table with papers. Elder Pei Shou on the Speaker's side, with Lu Kang beside him and nothing said about that. The whole of the furnace crew standing along the open side in the weather, in two ranks, because a hall with no roof is a hall with no roof and a furnace crew that would not stand in it would hear about it for a year.

And Xie Shuo, at the end of the table, with a fair hand, on his fourth day in a building where the roof had come off on top of him.

There was a witness clause and it had been drafted for the corridor on the second day and had survived three days of a furnace being taken off a man, and it said: *witnessed by not fewer than nine persons of the Hall and of the crew, entered with the hour and the names.*

Forty-one was not fewer than nine.

She came in with the tablet against her skin and the log in the lining and a piece of paper in her own hand in her left coat pocket, and she gave the paper to the clerk at the table before anybody said anything, and it was the schedule of the route, and she had written it on the back of a laundry docket in a hand that any clerk in the building could read.

Four miles of the Lowwater road past the weigh-station. The turn at the willows. A marker post of the Warden's office, the fourth from the bridge, with a white stone on it. Two miles of track after that and no other building. A yard, and a wall, and a door.

Eleven lines. She had written them at the fifth hour that morning in the crew quarters and she had checked them twice, and every line of them was true.

“Clerk,” said the Speaker. “Read the schedule.”

“Four miles of the Lowwater road past the weigh-station,” said the clerk. “The turn at the willows. A marker post of the Warden's office, the fourth from the bridge, with a white stone on it. Two miles of track after that and no other building. A yard, and a wall, and a door.”

“Enter it,” said the Speaker.

“Entered,” said the clerk.

## The Two Halves

“There are two halves of this document,” said Lu Kang, and he had aged four days in them, or he had decided to look as though he had. “There is the clause your brother, and there is the article. Both are required. We cannot take one without the other, and I will not pretend that is a formality.”

“I know.”

“Read the schedule of recovered articles,” he said to the clerk.

The clerk read it and nobody looked at Ning Yan while he did, which was the single kindest thing that happened to her in five days.

“One article. A single fired plate, the size of a hand, taken from the sealed ground of the Broken Kiln and returned to the seal within the week of signature.”

Then he stopped, because a document that has a schedule of recovered articles has to have somewhere to say what became of them, and the schedule did not say.

“Say the line under it,” Ning Yan said.

“There is no line under it.”

“There is one in my hand,” said Ning Yan, “and it is in that document, and it is the reason I brought the schedule.”

She took the paper out of the clerk's hand before he had finished reading it and put it flat on the table, and she read it out herself, in the voice she used for tallies, in a room with no roof and forty-one people in it.

“*One article, a single fired plate, the size of a hand. The article is not in the possession of the maintaining members, is not in the possession of this Hall, and has not been in the possession of the undersigned since the hour she left the sealed ground. The undersigned cannot produce it and does not undertake to. It is entered here because it will be looked for, and because a schedule that does not say what became of a thing is a schedule that will be read four ways.*”

Nobody in that hall said anything.

She had written it at the fifth hour that morning and she had not hesitated while she wrote it, and she had checked it twice, and the only thing she had checked twice was whether it was legible.

The article had been against her chest for ninety days. She had slept on it. She had washed with it. She had taken a lightning strike through her body on the floor of that building four days ago with a plate of fired qi tied between her breasts and it had not so much as marked her.

“There is a sworn entry on this sheet,” said Lu Kang, after a while, “and it is false.”

“Yes.”

“Say the whole of it once more.”

“It is false,” said Ning Yan, “and I am telling you it is false to your face in a room with witnesses, and I would like the clerk to enter that I said so, and I would like the hour entered, because in four years somebody is going to ask where that plate is and the answer is going to have to be somewhere.”

He entered it. She watched him do it and his hand was steady and the hand was better than any hand in that building.

That was the first thing she had ever done that was not strictly true, and she had done it standing up, in daylight, with people watching, having spent four months being proud of a certain kind of honesty and understanding inside about nine seconds that this was the other kind, and that the other kind was what the man in her head had been doing to her since the ninth week.

She did not feel clean and she had not expected to.

## The Clause

“Now the other half,” said Lu Kang.

The clerk read the entry that had been made on the second day at the third hour, out of the minute book, in the fair even hand of a man in servant-rank's cuffs who had never once put his pen down crooked.

“*Entered on the second day at the third hour, by Elders Lu Kang, Deng Chao and Pei An: that the record of Ning Wu, assessed at the Broken Kiln on the seventeenth day of the autumn two years past and reassigned above the authority of the furnace, be entered in the register as reconciled; that the entry was asked for by the member and not offered; and that the entry is made in consideration of signature of the sponsorship within the hour following the hearing entered on the fourth day.*”

“Enter it,” said Lu Kang.

“Clerk,” said Ning Yan.

The clerk had the pen up. She had never spoken to him in her life and she had never needed to.

“Read the record as it stands before you write the word. Not the schedule. The record.”

He looked down at the page and read it, because he was a clerk and she had asked him to.

“Ning Wu. *Assessed, reassigned, unreconciled.* Assessment entered at the Broken Kiln on the seventeenth day of the autumn two years past. Reassignment entered above the authority of the furnace, in a hand not held by this sect. No closing entry. No reconciliation entered at any office in this Hall or in the Bureau.”

“Thank you,” said Ning Yan.

He wrote one word.

She had asked, in a corridor, for that word to be entered with the hour on it and the clerk's name on it and the fact that it had been asked for and not offered, and there had been a man of servant-rank standing in the corridor who had been the one to carry the paper down to the terrace steps nine days before that with a sentence about not writing it, and he was in the room, standing with the crew, at the back, and he did not move at all.

She read the word out loud.

She read it into the weather, because the hall had no roof and the weather came down into it from three sides at once.

“The record says *reconciled*,” said Ning Yan. “It does not say what happened to him.”

She said it twice, because the first time her voice went out flat and small into a stone room and died on the back wall.

“It does not say what happened to him,” she said, “and it does not say that he is alive, and it does not say that he is dead, and it does not say where he went, and I am not going to be able to read anything into it that is not already in it. It is a word they gave me for a piece of paper. I have been given the word *reconciled* and I have not been given my brother.”

Nobody said anything. Forty-one people in an open-sided hall in the fourth month of a winter.

“It is still the best thing anybody has done for me in four months,” she said. “Enter it.”

## The Whole of It

The last clause was the one at the bottom of the first sheet in the different ink, and the servant-rank clerk read it, and it was the clause he had carried up six miles in the rain and refused to guess at.

“The member undertakes that she will not speak, report, describe or enter, in any record of this Hall or of the Bureau of Records or in any testimony before any office, any voice, sound or presence heard within her at any time since the ninth day past.”

She signed it.

She signed it in front of forty-one witnesses and the Hall's clerks and a crew standing in the weather, and the brush was bad and her right hand did not work, and she wrote with her left and it came out in the ugly slanting way that a left hand writes, and one of the clerks had to hold the paper and one of them had to blot it, and she did not apologise for any of it.

She was signing a document that said she would never mention him.

She looked at the place on the page where that clause sat and thought, with complete clarity, that it was a false sentence, because a Chosen One is not silenced by a piece of paper, and that it was also a true sentence about her, because the penalty was real and the penalty was her father's account coming back with six years on it.

So she had signed a document that bound her to a silence that could not be enforced, and enforced it anyway, against herself, by hand.

*Thank you*, she thought, in the direction of the inside of her own attention, and got nothing back at all for about four seconds, which she had expected.

Then, inside, not out loud: *That is the fourth one. I am going to keep doing that and one day you are going to notice and object.*

And nothing, still.

The account of Ning Bo, furnace-man, unsettled, balance carried forward six years, credit held, was struck whole in the minute book of Qinghe at the second hour and thirty-one minutes of the fifth day, in a hand that was not fair.

She stood up and felt the whole six years come off her at once and go nowhere.

That was the part she had not been ready for. She had spent two months wanting an account closed and she had wanted it closed the way a person wants a debt closed, with the person on the other side of it repaid. What she got was a line through six years and a document and a room where nobody was owed anything any more, and there was nobody standing in it.

Her father was not there. He had not been there since she was eleven.

Lu Kang gave her the token.

It was a plain iron thing the size of a thumb, with the number of the maintaining members cut into the back of it and nothing else on the front at all, and it was the only object in the world that had ever been handed to her for nothing at all, which was the second one this winter and both of them had come out of the same building.

“Furnace crew,” said Lu Kang.

“Maintaining member,” said Ning Yan, and did not put it away, and held it in her left hand for the rest of that hour while four clerks made four copies of a document she had just lied on, and the copies went up to nine counties, and she had bought one word.

## After

She went out of the Hall at the fourth hour and the ash was coming down and the sky over Qinghe had gone the colour of a coin.

The relief came on her at the second step and it was so large that she had to stop and put her left hand on the rail. Her father was off the page. The six years were struck in four counties before dark. A word with one syllable in it was on her brother's record and had been asked for by her and entered by name and by hour, and it was not the record of a man who had been assessed and reassigned above the authority of a furnace and had never been seen again.

It was *reconciled*.

She stood on the second step of the Nine Steps Terrace with her hand on a cold rail and an iron token in her other hand and felt the biggest single relief of her life, and she knew — standing on a step, alone, with the ash on her — that relief was not the same as knowing.

Nothing in that document had told her one single new thing about her brother.

It had told her that three men in a building could be made to write a word, and it had cost her a plate, and a false statement in front of forty-one witnesses, and the silence of the only voice in her head, written down in ink by a hand that could not be taken back.

She went down to the crew quarters and got out the log and turned to a clean page and wrote down, in her own poor hand, in the wash code inside it: the hour, the place, the four names of the witnesses she had counted, the plate, and the exact words of the entry, and then, underneath, three lines in her own hand without any code in them at all.

*My brother's record reads reconciled. That is not what happened to him.*

*My father is not repaid. He is not owed to anyone.*

*I am the only person in nine counties who knows both of these and there is a plate against my chest.*

*What was done to him was done in a kiln. The plate carries the assessment of a child of thirteen and it is the only object left anywhere that says what was written about him, and I gave a building the road to it and kept the road's reason.*

She put the brush down and sat with her right arm out of its own weight and the iron token on the bunk beside her, and outside the ash came down for the eighth day of eleven, and down in Lowwater a lamp-mender was reading out four different sentences about her in a market square, and in four county books on the same line as the credit held by Ning Bo there was going to be, by morning, a note about a claim against the Chosen One at eight hundred jin and the standing of a household that had just been written off.

One piece of paper, then. Four copies of it, going up.

The word she had bought and the price on her head were going to end up in the same hand, on the same line, on the same page, in every county in the bound, and there was not one clerk in nine counties who was going to think that was strange.
