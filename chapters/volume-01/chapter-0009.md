# Deadweight

The fifth day she carried someone.

They found the inner disciple at dawn below the sheath ridge, where the ash had drifted deep enough to bury a man to the waist. He wore the grey sash of the furnace crew, servant-rank inner, and his leg was broken in two places, the bone showing through the cloth. His name was Pei Lan, nineteen, and he had gone ahead of the quota party two days before with a sponsor's token and no sense.

Ning Yan knew him slightly. He had carried water past the laundry yard without ever looking at the girls folding. Now he looked at nobody because his eyes were crusted shut with fever.

“We left him,” Lu Fen said flatly, when she asked. “Shen Xiu counted him. He was one of the four.”

She looked at Shen Xiu. The Team Elder stood with his list-board under his arm, face grey with exhaustion, eyes on the ridge. He had decided who died, she understood, and had not been told anything new in the four days she had been gone. Four names. Pei Lan was one. She, probably, was another, the open line he kept re-marking.

She made the arithmetic anyway.

A body heavier than her. Four hours' walk at full strength to the boundary, two hours at stage five if she ran and did not stop, longer with ash falling and a fevered man who screamed when moved. Cold, exhaustion, and no strategic reason. Carrying him cost her the lead she had built, the rest she needed, the warmth she did not have.

She took off her own coat, the only warm thing she owned, and wrapped it around him. The wind cut through her sweat-soaked inner robe at once, ice along the scar.

Du Hong stared. “You froze.”

“I froze anyway,” she said. “He froze faster.”

They lifted him between them, Du Hong at the shoulders, Ning Yan at the broken leg, binding it with rope and pine splints while Pei Lan moaned. The first hour was bearable. The second was not. Du Hong's arms gave out at the water crossing, and they went down together in the black creek with Pei Lan half under, and Ning Yan thought *this was where we all three died, for no reason anyone wrote down.*

Then boots came through the ash behind them, unhurried, old.

Shen Xiu. The Team Elder, who had counted Pei Lan as lost, who carried four names and a quota, who was not a villain but a tired man with a brush. He did not speak. He took the shoulders without comment, lifting half the deadweight as if it were his own tally slip, and walked with them the next two miles through falling ash, breathing hard, saying nothing at all about it.

That silence changed what he was. Ning Yan walked with her hands full of Pei Lan's broken leg and watched the back of the man's neck ahead of her, the thin coat, the grey hair stuck to sweat, and understood he was deciding live, in front of her, against his own board.

They reached the boundary shelter by dusk. Pei Lan lived. The next morning his fever broke, under her coat, which she never got back in the same shape.

She sat outside the shelter with her arms wrapped around her knees, shaking with cold she could not stop, stage five thin in her chest, and felt the strangeness of what she had done. The first unselfish act she had ever committed, and it was not noble. It was just arithmetic about a boy who would otherwise die, done with a coat instead of numbers.

She did not know what to do with that. She had spent something she did not have, for no quota, no plate, no voice's quest, and the spending sat in her like a stone with no column to file it under.

The voice spoke, soft.

“You are cold. I have no coat. I am here.”

She leaned her head back against the shelter wall and let herself be grateful for company that could not warm her, which was a colder and truer thing than she expected.

The route back went past Lowwater, and there was a lamp-tower in Lowwater, and in the tower a clerk who wrote down what the air said. She kept that thought like a coal in her sleeve all the way into sleep.
