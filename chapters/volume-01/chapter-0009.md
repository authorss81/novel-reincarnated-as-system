# Deadweight

The fifth day she lost one and carried one, and she never worked out which of the two she had been doing.

Lu Mei was not at the fire.

Ning Yan woke on the second hour with her scar aching and went round the bank counting bodies the way she counted everything now, without deciding to. Nineteen where there had been twenty-one. The two that were missing were Han Shu, who was under the ash above the second water, and Lu Mei, whose bedroll was folded at the edge of the fire and cold.

Nobody had seen her go. That was the part that went round the camp all morning and did not stop going round. Her pack was still under her head. Her boots were still on, set out neatly side by side, because she had been in the laundry yard for two years and she had cleaned her own boots every night of her life whatever else she had not done.

The ash-cough went through a camp in a day. It took the wet and the thin and the ones who had breathed woodsmoke for nine days already, and it did not make any noise doing it, and the only sign anybody got was that the bedroll was cold.

Ning Yan stood over the folded blanket and did the arithmetic that was the only thing she had, which was that four feet of wick had gone from her sleeve and a boy had gone into the ash and a girl had folded her blanket and set her boots out, and none of that was going into any column except the one.

She folded the blanket the way a laundry-hand folds a blanket. She put the boots inside it. She had done it a thousand times and it took her hands four seconds.

Du Hong found her doing it and stood there and then knelt down and helped, which was the single most useful thing anybody had done for her in eight days.

“She had four feet of wick,” Du Hong said.

“She gave them to me.”

“She was not supposed to give quota goods to other disciples.”

“She said that. She said it to me, at the gate, and then she gave them to me anyway.”

Du Hong tied the blanket shut with a length of his own cord. His hands were not steady and he kept them in the firelight until they were.

They buried her under the ash above the second water, badly, in eleven minutes, because the ground was frozen and they had one shovel between six of them and a quota run that would not wait.

The voice did not say anything at all for the whole of the burial. Ning Yan noticed the absence the way she noticed a missing tooth.

They found Pei Lan an hour later, below the sheath ridge.

He was where the ash had drifted deep enough to bury a man to the waist. He wore the grey sash of the furnace crew, servant-rank inner, and his leg was broken in two places, the bone showing through the cloth. His name was Pei Lan, nineteen, and he had gone ahead of the quota party two days before with a sponsor's token and no sense.

Ning Yan knew him slightly. He had carried water past the laundry yard without ever looking at the girls folding. Now he looked at nobody because his eyes were crusted shut with fever.

“We left him,” Lu Fen said flatly, when she asked. “Shen Xiu counted him. He was one of the four.”

She looked at Shen Xiu. The Team Elder stood with his list-board under his arm, face grey with exhaustion, eyes on the ridge. He had decided who died, she understood, and had not been told anything new in the four days she had been gone. Four names. Pei Lan was one. She, probably, was another, the open line he kept re-marking.

She made the arithmetic anyway.

She said it out loud, because in her mother's yard the thing you did not say out loud was the thing that got done behind your back.

“Out loud,” she said. “I am taking him to the boundary. Du Hong at the shoulders, me at the leg. We leave when he can be lifted and we do not stop until he is on the other side of the lamps.”

Six of the party had come down the ridge with them. Nobody moved.

“He is on the board,” said the second-rank boy, the one who had done the arithmetic about thirty-three. “Elder Shen put him there at dawn. If he dies down there it is a line and a cart. If we take him, we are two disciples and four hours we do not have, and we go back short.”

“Then we go back short,” Ning Yan said.

“He has a sponsor's token. He is worth more to the furnace than he is to the quota. That is not an opinion, that is how the column works.”

“Then the furnace can send somebody.”

“He *has* a sponsor,” said the boy. “That is what a sponsor is. It is somebody else’s line in the book. He was sent ahead to be assessed and the line was written and the line says nobody carries him out. If we carry him out we are saying the line is wrong, in front of six witnesses, on the fifth day, in the ash.”

He was not a stupid boy. That was the thing Ning Yan kept coming back to on the walk up the ridge afterward. He was not a stupid boy and he had done the sums correctly and he was going to be right about the cart.

“Then I am saying the line is wrong,” she said, “in front of six witnesses, on the fifth day, in the ash.”

The second-rank boy looked at her for a long moment. Then he went and picked up his pack, and walked out of it without another word, and four of the others went with him.

That was the moment she stopped being a girl the camp made decisions about.

A body heavier than her. Four hours' walk at full strength to the boundary, two hours at stage five if she ran and did not stop, longer with ash falling and a fevered man who screamed when moved. Cold, exhaustion, and no strategic reason. Carrying him cost her the lead she had built, the rest she needed, the warmth she did not have.

She took off her own coat, the only warm thing she owned, and wrapped it around him. The wind cut through her sweat-soaked inner robe at once, ice along the scar.

Du Hong stared. “You froze.”

“I froze anyway,” she said. “He froze faster.”

They lifted him between them, Du Hong at the shoulders, Ning Yan at the broken leg, binding it with rope and pine splints while Pei Lan moaned. The first hour was bearable. The second was not. Du Hong's arms gave out at the water crossing, and they went down together in the black creek with Pei Lan half under, and Ning Yan thought *this was where we all three died, for no reason anyone wrote down.*

Then boots came through the ash behind them, unhurried, old.

Shen Xiu. The Team Elder, who had counted Pei Lan as lost, who carried four names and a quota, who was not a villain but a tired man with a brush. He did not speak. He took the shoulders without comment, lifting half the deadweight as if it were his own tally slip, and walked with them the next two miles through falling ash, breathing hard, saying nothing at all about it.

Ning Yan walked the last of it with her hands under Pei Lan's knees and understood the whole shape of the thing at once, and it was not a mystery.

The layer had said thirty-three at dawn and the board said twenty-eight and the difference was five. If Pei Lan had died below the ridge, nobody in this camp would ever have had to account for a fifth harvest, because a fifth harvest in the sheath country would be found by the bound and put in the air, and the Elder Hall could not sign for a number the layer had not given them.

Shen Xiu was not carrying a boy to the boundary. He was carrying a boy past the boundary lamp, into the half hour where the tally was still his, so that the name came up inside the time they allowed him.

It was exactly what he had said on the bank. It was firewood, and he had carried it.

That silence changed what he was. Ning Yan walked behind him and watched the thin coat and the grey hair stuck to sweat, and understood he was deciding live, in front of her, against his own board.

They reached the boundary shelter by dusk. Pei Lan lived. The next morning his fever broke, under her coat, which she never got back in the same shape.

She sat outside the shelter with her arms wrapped around her knees, shaking with cold she could not stop, stage five thin in her chest, and felt the strangeness of what she had done. The first unselfish act she had ever committed, and it was not noble. It was just arithmetic about a boy who would otherwise die, done with a coat instead of numbers.

She did not know what to do with that. She had spent something she did not have, for no quota, no plate, no voice's quest, and the spending sat in her like a stone with no column to file it under.

The voice spoke, soft.

“You are cold. I have no coat. I am here.”

She leaned her head back against the shelter wall and let herself be grateful for company that could not warm her, which was a colder and truer thing than she expected.

The route back went past Lowwater, and there was a lamp-tower in Lowwater, and in the tower a clerk who wrote down what the air said. She kept that thought like a coal in her sleeve all the way into sleep.
