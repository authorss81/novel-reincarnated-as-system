# The Bell at Ninth Watch

Ning Yan counted her hands again because the number refused to change. Four short of a quarter-token, with three hours left before the quota bell, and the staging ground below the Lower Gate filling with boys who already smelled like ash.

She had carried the tally slip in her sleeve since noon. The ink had sweated through where her wrist pressed it. Four hands. A hand was a beast's foreleg with the bounty mark still on it, or a bundle of core-stone that passed the furnace-man's weighing, and she had neither. What she had was a cracked basket, a laundry scar across her left thumb, and a mother six years in the ground who had taught her to fold other people's robes without looking at the names inside them.

The bell would ring at dawn. If her token came up whole, the run counted her as a person. If it came up short, the Elder Hall could substitute her for one of the losses, which was the word they used when four outer disciples went into the Verge and the Verge kept them.

She tightened the rope on her basket and tried to work out where four hands could be found in a yard full of people doing the same arithmetic.

The staging ground was a flat scar of trampled snow below Qinghe's Lower Gate. Eleven other outer disciples moved through it in the blue dusk, checking knives and rope and lamp-oil with the particular quiet of people who had been told not to waste their breath. Above them the Nine Steps Terrace rose in tiers, lamps already lit along the sixth step, smoke from the furnace tasting of iron even this far down. Team Elder Shen Xiu stood by the gate post with his list-board under his arm, calling names without looking up, his brush pausing a fraction longer over some than others.

Ning Yan knew what that pause meant. Everyone on the run knew. He had already decided four names.

“Ning Yan,” Shen Xiu called, when she stepped forward. “You were four hands short at last weighing.”

She bowed with the basket against her hip. “I was,” she said. “I hoped to make it up on the run.”

He marked something. He did not look at her face. He was a tired man in a padded coat gone thin at the elbows, forty-eight winters old and carrying every one of them in the shoulders, and the list-board in his hands had twelve names and four small ink dots beside four of them that she pretended she had not seen.

“You heard the rule,” he said. “What you carried back was what counted.”

She stepped back into the line. Her breath came fast and shallow. Sixteen to the minute, she would have guessed, if anyone had asked her to guess such a thing, from cold and hunger and the particular fear of being counted.

Then a voice spoke inside her head, clear as a finger pressed to the inside of her skull.

“You are Ning Yan. You are Body-Forging stage three. You breathe sixteen times a minute.”

She stopped walking.

The yard went on around her. A boy named Du Hong dropped his rope and swore and picked it up. Lamp-oil sloshed. Shen Xiu called the next name. Nobody else turned. Nobody else heard anything, because the voice had not come through the air at all, and Ning Yan understood that in the time it took her heart to beat twice, the way a person understands that a hand on the back of the neck is not wind.

Her first thought was spirit. Her second was god. Her third, arriving hard behind them, was that spirits and gods did not count breaths.

She kept her face still. Seventeen years servant-born at Qinghe had taught her that much, watching laundry-hands accused of theft and furnace-men accused of short weight, watching her father Ning Bo taken for a stalled ascension when she was eleven and her brother Ning Wu taken for the kiln at thirteen. Stillness was the only thing that had ever belonged to her.

*How did you know my name,* she thought, not moving her lips, testing whether thinking counted as speaking.

“I hear what you hear. I see what you carry. I have no hands.”

The voice was young. A man's voice, dry at the edges, embarrassed underneath the dryness, speaking too fast the way people spoke when they were afraid of being interrupted. Every sentence landed flat in the present, as if he could not afford any other tense.

“I am Yuan. I am stuck. I want out.”

Her basket handle creaked under her grip. Around her the line shuffled forward toward the Verge path. The ashwood trees stood black beyond the boundary stone, twelve days after the last Cracking, and the ash that fell after a Cracking fell eleven days and did not settle, which meant tonight it would still be in the air, in the eyes, in the lungs for years.

She needed four hands. She needed to not be one of the four dots. She needed, suddenly and violently, to know whether she had gone mad from hunger, because mad girls did not come back from the Verge and their names went into a column she did not want to think about.

*If you were inside my head,* she thought, *you knew what I was going to ask.*

“I know what you carry. I know what you lack. I do not know what you dream.”

It was an answer only a thing inside her head could have given, because she had not framed the question in words at all, only as a pressure behind the teeth. Cold went down her spine and pooled in her stomach.

She did the thing she did before every fight she had ever lost in the yard. She tested.

*Ning Wu,* she thought, the name of her brother like a stone dropped down a well. *If you heard that, you repeated it back.*

“I hear Ning Wu. I do not know where he is. I am sorry.”

She said it aloud before she meant to. “Ning Wu.”

Her own voice in the cold air. Two syllables, too loud.

Shen Xiu's brush stopped. He lifted his head. For a moment his eyes fixed not on her but on the air beside her head, the way a man looks when a lamp gutters though no wind passed, and his own name-list rustled under his arm though his hands had not moved it. Something had sounded in the air that was not his own, a tone under her voice like a second string touched by accident.

The yard quieted a fraction. Du Hong glanced at her. She felt heat climb her neck.

“What did you say?” Shen Xiu asked.

She bowed quickly, basket banging her knee. “I asked if my brother's kiln tally was counted in mine,” she said, which was a lie built fast from true pieces. “I misspoke.”

He studied her. He was not cruel. That was the worst thing about him, she would think later, that he looked at twelve children going into the trees with four dots on his board and still took the trouble to look tired about it.

“Your father's column was the furnace,” he said. “Your brother's was the kiln. Yours was the run. You kept them separate.”

She nodded and hated herself for the quick relief. Her father Ning Bo sat in the furnace column of the ledger. Everyone knew that much. Six years dead and still counting.

The voice spoke again, quieter, as if it had learned something from her fear.

“You are going to die out there. I can help. I ask for work.”

*What did you want,* she thought. Not who are you. What do you want. Servant-born questions went to the hands first.

“I want a Chosen. You fit the shape. I offer trades. You decide.”

Du Hong jostled her shoulder going past with his rope coil. “You looked like you saw a ghost,” he muttered, in the past tense of boys trying not to sound scared. “You prayed?”

She shook her head. She had never prayed. Laundry-hands' daughters did not pray to a sky that charged by the hand.

The line moved toward the boundary stone. The infirmary lamp burned yellow to the left, warm and safe and full of girls who would sit out the run with fever and live to be substituted next season instead. The Verge path opened to the right, black trees and falling ash and four hands she did not have.

“You decide now,” the voice said. “I am here. I stay here.”

Ning Yan lifted her basket. Her legs shook. Terror sat in her chest like swallowed ice, and under it something harder began to form, the way frost formed on a bucket rim, thin and clear and difficult to break once set.

She walked out toward the run rather than toward the infirmary lamp.

Behind her, Shen Xiu marked his board. His brush hesitated over her name, then moved on without making a dot, and the sound her voice had made in the air stayed in his ears all the way down to the boundary stone, where he stopped and looked back once at nothing.
