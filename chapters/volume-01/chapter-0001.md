# The Bell at Ninth Watch

Ning Yan counted her hands again because the number refused to change. Four short of a quarter-token, with three hours left before the quota bell, and the staging ground below the Lower Gate filling with boys who already smelled like ash.

She had carried the tally slip in her sleeve since noon and the ink had sweated through where her wrist pressed it. A hand was a beast's foreleg with the bounty mark still on it, or a bundle of core-stone that passed the furnace-man's weighing. She had neither. What she had was a cracked basket, a laundry scar across her left thumb, and a mother six years in the ground who had taught her to fold other people's robes without looking at the names inside them.

The bell would ring at dawn. If her token came up whole, the run counted her as a person. If it came up short, the Elder Hall could substitute her for one of the losses, which was the word they used when four outer disciples went into the Verge and the Verge kept them.

The staging ground was a flat scar of trampled snow below Qinghe's Lower Gate. Eleven other outer disciples moved through it in the blue dusk, checking knives and rope and lamp-oil. Above them the Nine Steps Terrace rose in tiers, lamps already lit along the sixth step, smoke from the furnace tasting of iron even this far down.

Team Elder Shen Xiu stood by the gate post with his list-board under his arm, calling names without looking up. His brush paused a fraction longer over some names than others. Ning Yan knew what that pause meant. Everyone on the run knew. He had already decided four.

“Ning Yan,” Shen Xiu called, when she stepped forward. “You were four hands short at last weighing.”

She bowed with the basket against her hip. “I was,” she said. “I hoped to make it up on the run.”

He marked something. He did not look at her face. The coat was padded and gone thin at the elbows, and the list-board had twelve names on it and four small ink dots beside four of them that she pretended she had not seen.

“You heard the rule,” he said. “What you carried back was what counted.”

She stepped back into the line. Her breath came fast and shallow. Sixteen to the minute, she would have guessed, from cold and hunger and the particular fear of being counted.

Then a voice spoke inside her head, clear as a finger pressed to the inside of her skull.

“You are Ning Yan. You are Body-Forging stage three. You breathe sixteen times a minute.”

She stopped walking.

The yard went on around her. Du Hong dropped his rope and swore and picked it up. Lamp-oil sloshed. Shen Xiu called the next name. Nobody else turned, because the voice had not come through the air. Her first thought was spirit. Her second was god. Her third, arriving hard behind them, was that spirits and gods did not count breaths.

She kept her face still. Seventeen years servant-born at Qinghe had taught her that much, watching laundry-hands accused of theft and furnace-men accused of short weight. Stillness was the only thing that had ever belonged to her.

*How did you know my name,* she thought, not moving her lips.

“I hear what you hear. I see what you carry. I have no hands.”

The voice was young. A man’s voice, dry at the edges and embarrassed underneath the dryness, speaking too fast the way people spoke when they were afraid of being interrupted. Every sentence landed flat in the present, as if he could not afford any other tense.

She did not wait to be told what he was. She had a thing to test, so she tested.

*Ning Wu,* she thought, her brother’s name dropped down a well like a stone. *If you heard that, say it back.*

“I hear Ning Wu. I do not know where he is. I am sorry.”

She said it aloud before she meant to. “Ning Wu.”

Her own voice in the cold air. Two syllables, too loud.

Shen Xiu’s brush stopped. He lifted his head. For a moment his eyes fixed not on her but on the air beside her head, the way a man looks when a lamp gutters though no wind passed, and his own name-list rustled under his arm though his hands had not moved it. Something had sounded in the air that was not his own, a tone under her voice like a second string touched by accident.

The yard quieted a fraction. Du Hong glanced at her. She felt heat climb her neck.

“What did you say?” Shen Xiu asked.

She bowed quickly, basket banging her knee. “I asked if my brother’s kiln tally was counted in mine,” she said, which was a lie built fast out of true pieces. “I misspoke.”

He studied her. He was not cruel. That was the worst thing about him, she would think about it later for years, that he could look at twelve children going into the trees with four dots on his board and still take the trouble to look tired.

“Your father’s column was the furnace,” he said. “Your brother’s was the kiln. Yours was the run. You kept them separate.”

She nodded and hated herself for the quick relief. Her father Ning Bo sat in the furnace column of the ledger. Everyone knew that much. Six years dead and still counting.

“I am Yuan,” the voice said. “I am stuck. I want out.”

Around her the line shuffled toward the Verge path. The ashwood trees stood black beyond the boundary stone, twelve days after the last Cracking, and the ash that fell after a Cracking fell eleven days and did not settle, which meant tonight it would still be in the air, in the eyes, in the lungs for years.

“If you were inside my head,” she thought, “you knew what I was going to ask.”

“I know what you carry. I know what you lack. I do not know what you dream.”

It was an answer only a thing inside her head could have given, because she had not framed the question in words at all, only as a pressure behind the teeth. Cold went down her spine and pooled in her stomach.

She needed four hands. She needed to not be one of the four dots. And underneath both of those, in a voice she could not shut off, something was standing in her skull and calling itself Yuan, and she needed very badly to know whether hunger and fear could count a girl’s breaths.

*What did you want,* she thought. Not who are you. What do you want. Servant-born questions went to the hands first.

“You are going to die out there. I can help. I ask for work.”

“I want a Chosen,” he said. “You fit the shape. I offer trades. You decide.”

That was the moment her legs decided to go. She caught herself on the basket hoop and it came down with a crack that rang off the ice, and for eleven whole breaths the only voice in the world was the wind coming off the ridge.

A girl said, “Share the wick.”

Ning Yan looked down. Lu Mei was fifteen and small and had folded laundry in the same yard as Ning Yan for two years before she was taken up to the sect, and she had come out here with a twist of lamp-wick in her fist, holding it out the way you hold out something you have decided to pay for.

“I have four feet of it,” Lu Mei said. “You carry the basket. I cannot carry anything, look at me.” She was already inside her coat. “Take it or the lamps fail on the second water and everyone walks blind.”

“You want a name.”

“I want a name that was not the last thing I did.” Lu Mei pushed the wick into her hand. “Four feet. That is my whole fortune. Do not lose it, I will know.”

“You are not supposed to give quota goods to other disciples,” Ning Yan said.

“No,” Lu Mei agreed. “That is why I am giving it to you and not to a clerk.”

Across the yard a boy called Han Shu was doing the same arithmetic out loud and losing, because Han Shu was sixteen and had the habit of saying whatever he was thinking at the volume the thought arrived. He had furnace-crew kitchen privileges and nothing else. He had spent his lamp-oil on a girl in the inner yard in autumn and had been hunting since.

“No,” Han Shu was saying to nobody, “no, that is not the arrangement, the arrangement is that the fire does not go out.”

“You will freeze,” a voice said.

“I have said this already.”

“Freezing is worse than being short.”

Han Shu turned the wick he was holding over in his fingers, looked at it, and put it back in his belt with the air of a man putting away a tool he had decided not to use. When he passed Ning Yan he said, “Four hands. Who has four hands?”

“Nobody,” Ning Yan said.

“Then we are all going to be a story somebody tells,” Han Shu said, and went off to argue with a tent peg.

Du Hong came through with his rope coil over one shoulder, eighteen and broad and bad at hiding a thing. He had stopped next to her twice already, which was twice more than most boys managed in a run.

“You are standing in the draught,” he said. “The wind comes round the post there.”

“I know where the wind comes from.”

“You are shaking.”

“I am cold.”

“You are shaking,” Du Hong said, “and you do not have four hands, and you were saying a name.”

The infirmary lamp burned yellow across the yard to the left, warm and safe and full of girls who would sit out the run with fever and live to be substituted next season instead. Ning Yan had a cousin inside it last year. The cousin had come out of it in spring with her eyes wrong and a name in the hurt column, and the hurt column was a slower road than the loss column but it was the same road.

“You decide now,” the voice said. “I am here. I stay here.”

Ning Yan lifted her basket. Her legs shook. Terror sat in her chest like swallowed ice, and under it something harder began to form, the way frost forms on a bucket rim, thin and clear and difficult to break once set.

She walked out toward the run rather than toward the infirmary lamp.

Behind her, Shen Xiu marked his board. His brush hesitated over her name, then moved on without making a dot. She went down the trampled slope with Lu Mei’s wick in her left sleeve and the ash already starting in her hair, and the sound her voice had made in the air stayed in the Elder’s ears all the way down to the boundary stone, where he stopped and looked back once at nothing at all.
