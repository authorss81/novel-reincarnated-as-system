# The Return

The road up to the Lower Gate on the seventh day was ice and ash and wind.

Ning Yan walked it with eight bodies. Twelve had gone into the Verge. Two would not make it — a boy named Han Shu taken by a sheath-cat on the second night, a girl named Lu Mei lost to the ash-cough on the fifth, both wrapped and carried down by the others because the quota counted the dead as carefully as the living. One would not be carried, Pei Lan, who insisted on walking the last mile on his splinted leg with his teeth sunk in his lip, weeping without sound.

Eight bodies to be counted as whole.

Shen Xiu walked ahead with his list-board. His brush had three names in the loss column already, written in the night while no one watched. The fourth line stood open, the ink wet and unwritten, and Ning Yan caught him looking at it, then at her, then away, with the expression of a man doing arithmetic he hated.

He was deciding live, in front of her, and she was one of the ones he had not decided. Hurt, not lost. Lame, but returned with a sheath cleared and a boundary mark. Useful, but expensive. The open line was never explained to her and he never admitted it again, but she saw it, and seeing it was enough.

The last climb broke Pei Lan. Half a mile below the gate his splints slipped on ice, the broken bone shifted, and he went down without a cry, white to the lips, unable to rise. Du Hong moved to lift him. Lu Fen stepped back.

“He walks in or he is carried in,” Shen Xiu said quietly. “Carried girls are not counted whole. You knew the rule.”

It was true. It had always been true. The run counted those who came back on their own feet. The carried were hurt. The hurt were substituted next season.

Ning Yan looked at the gate above, at the yard beyond filling with outer disciples come to watch the return, at the Ledger Reader's canopy already raised for tomorrow's reading. She looked at Pei Lan in the ice, at Du Hong's hands under his shoulders, at Shen Xiu's open line.

She bent, took Pei Lan's arm over her own shoulders, and stood with him. He was heavier than her by half. Her scar screamed. Stage five shuddered in her chest. Du Hong took the other side. Together they carried the fourth man up the last half mile, but she made sure her feet were under him, her legs doing the work where everyone could see, step-fragment, habit answering when it chose, her face set and streaming with sweat in the cold.

She carried him herself, into the yard, on her own feet, in front of the whole outer yard, so that the line could not be written.

The yard went quiet as they came through the Lower Gate. A hundred disciples watched a scarred seventeen-year-old carry a broken boy past the Nine Steps Terrace with ash in her hair and blood on her sleeves. Shen Xiu walked behind them with his board, and when they passed the gate stone he closed the board without writing, the fourth line left open and then smudged shut by his thumb.

Pei Lan lived to be counted. She had announced herself without meaning to and could not take it back.

That night she lay in the outer dormitory with four days of load-carrying humming in her bones and felt the deep shift of stage six come with a small crack through the wall of sleep, unannounced, private, hers. Body-Forging stage six. Built from carrying, not killing. No voice's quest, no price stated, just work.

The voice spoke in the dark, wondering.

“You carry others. You grow. I do not pay. I am learning.”

She turned her face to the wall and smiled despite everything. Tomorrow the ledger would be read in the yard. Tomorrow her quarter-token would be settled and she would be counted as a person who came back.

She slept with Feng Lushi's copy under her pillow and dreamed of fair handwriting.
