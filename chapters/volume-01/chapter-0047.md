# Waiting

The ash came into the crew quarters before she did, which meant it had been in the air all night, and by the time Ning Yan came in out of the dark at the second hour there was a grey skin on the inside of the window that had not been there the day before.

[The Cracking at the Verge's edge is entered. Eleven days of ash. It does not settle.]

She put her hand flat on the sill and lifted it and the ash came off in a smear, and it was not grey, it was the colour of the inside of a flue, and it went into the creases of her palm and would still be in the creases of her palm at noon.

The washing crew had been out in it since before the light. There were nine of them in the courtyard by the trough and she heard them before she saw them, because coughing is the sound nine people make when they have all been handed the same bad air, and it was not nine separate coughs. It was a weave.

She went down to the lye trough because she had to wash her face and because there was one bucket left that was not full of grey water.

The water had a skin of ash on it the way a pond has a skin of ice, and it broke when she put her hands in, and the ash did not go under. It went round. She lifted her face out of it and stood there dripping with grey water running down the front of her, and one of the washing crew — a woman of about fifty with a burn scar along the side of her jaw — turned and looked at her, and then deliberately looked away, in the specific way that people look away when they have decided not to see a thing.

“Mind the trough,” the woman said. “The rim's greasy.”

“I'll mind it.”

She went back up. Nine steps from the yard to the quarters' door and she had counted them since she was eleven years old, and today it cost her something she had not budgeted for. The cough started on the sixth and she had it by the time she reached the top and she took it standing on the landing with her hand on the wall, four short pulls and then a hold, and the hold was the difficult part.

She had been coughing for two years. Her mother had coughed into wash water for a year before the wet lungs took her, and the sound of it was a sound she knew the way she knew the tally song, and the difference between her mother's cough and this one was that her mother's had a wet place at the bottom of it and this one had six years of Verge ash in the bottom of it and no wet place at all yet.

“Do not do that on the landing,” said a voice behind her.

It was the shift leader's girl, nineteen, coughing since the first week of the winter, who had been on the furnace floor the night Ning Yan came back from Lowwater with a hole in her and had said nothing then and been extremely good at it.

“On the landing, the whole floor hears it,” the girl said. “On the flats, nobody hears it.”

“On the flats I cough into the wind.”

“Then cough downwind. It's the same cough.”

She went in and shut the door and stood with her back against it in the dark, because the lamp had gone out and she had not lit it, and she found that she could hear the courtyard better through the door.

Four days.

## The Arithmetic

“Do the numbers for me,” she said.

He was there. He had been there since the yard, sitting in the way that a thing with no body sits, which was to say that he was simply present in the direction of her attention and had been for most of two months, and she had stopped being able to tell the difference between him being there and her being the sort of person who talks.

“What numbers.”

“What I have. Not what I want.” She got the lamp lit with her left hand because the right one had been unreliable since the sixth day and she had stopped apologising for it to anybody, including him. “Days. Body. What happens if I do not go through.”

He did the thing he did, which was to take the whole of her apart in about four seconds and put it back in an order she had not thought of.

“You have four days,” he said. “In four days the trial is on the Assembly floor and the Hall has entered it, which means the seal is live, which means the year inside which you must attempt Condensing runs from the eighth day past. That is the only clock in this that can kill you.”

“Not the only one.”

“No. There is a second one and you have not asked about it, so I am going to say it once and you are going to be angry with me for saying it.” He was quiet for half a breath. “If you stand on that floor and you do not go through, the Hall records that a member sealed for Condensing refused to attempt. That is not a defeat. That is a *finding*. And a finding of refusal on a servant-born with a furnace column behind her is not a punishment, it is an intake.”

She sat down on the edge of the bunk because her legs had made the decision for her.

“A furnace looks for that.”

“A furnace looks for that the way a cat looks for a hole,” he said. “You have been worried for four days about lightning and you have not once been worried about the other thing, and the other thing does not need weather.”

“Then it is a nine-day problem and I am standing in the middle of it.”

“You can do both of that in the same room in the same hour,” he said. “That is the whole reason you asked for the floor. You knew it on the eighth day when you asked for it, and you have not once said so out loud, because saying it means admitting you wanted it.”

She did not answer him.

“You did not have to tell me about the refusal finding,” she said instead. “You knew it would frighten me and you told me anyway.”

“I am trying to be a better instrument than I was in the spring.”

“That is not what I asked.”

“No,” he said. “It is what I did.”

## What the Ash Does

She went up onto the terrace at the sixth hour to look at the sky and found that there was no sky, only a lid. The ash came across from the north-east in a long shallow slant, not thick enough to hide the furnace chimney and exactly thick enough to put a grey film on everything that stood more than a foot off the ground. The washing lines were grey ropes. The rail of the ninth step had a line along the top of it like frost.

She looked at the Hall a hundred and forty feet off and could not see the ridge of its roof properly, and the trial would be in that room in four days in front of everyone the sect could put in a building.

“The announcement is audible,” he said. “It is the one thing in this that does not go through air. In a room full of ash, with the whole of them coughing, the words arrive exactly as clear as they would on a dry day. You asked for nine hundred witnesses to a sentence. You will have them whatever the sky is doing.”

“That is not what I am worried about.”

“No,” he said. “You are worried that a hall full of grey air will look at a girl on a floor and see nothing worth the light.”

She went back down and washed the trough.

## Mei Jiu

She was not asleep when the knock came, and it was not a knock. It was somebody standing outside a door in the dark not making a sound, which she could tell because she had spent most of her childhood on the other side of a door that somebody was standing behind.

She opened it.

Mei Jiu had a cloth over her head against the ash and she had come up nine steps in the dark with her hair wet at the ends, and she was breathing carefully, which meant she knew exactly what her lungs were doing and had decided to be polite about it.

“Furnace crew,” Mei Jiu said.

Inner disciple. Fourth rank. Two years at stage eight with a seal of her own and a place of her own and everything there had ever been, in a building that had been telling her since she was fifteen that she was going to be something.

“Come in.”

“No.”

The two words came out flat and fast and she put a hand on the doorframe to hold it open and did not come over it, and Ning Yan understood the whole of it in about a second and a half: that a furnace-crew servant-rank girl standing in a doorway with a fourth-rank inner disciple visible behind her in a corridor with no lamp in it would be a thing that got repeated in a wash house by Friday.

Mei Jiu put her right hand through the gap and held something out.

It was a folded square of cloth about the size of a palm, grey with dust, and it went from her fingers into Ning Yan's and then her fingers came out of it and the doorframe came between them again.

“What is this.”

“The inner compression,” Mei Jiu said. “The one for a narrow gate. It is not in the scripture hall and it is not written down anywhere in this sect. I have been given it once, by a woman in Beihe who owed my master a debt, and there are four people alive who can do it and three of them are old.”

“Why.”

“Because in four days you are going to stand on a floor with a crack in your gate and try to open it in front of a hall, and the crack is going to be the interesting thing about you, and you do not have a way to make it boring.”

Ning Yan looked at the cloth in her hand.

“Why are you giving me this.”

“Because I am not going to compete with you.”

“I have been at stage eight for two years,” Mei Jiu said. “I had my seal at fifteen. I have a room, a stipend, a good name with four elders and a place in the third seat at every table I have ever sat at. And every single thing I have got, I got because somebody decided I was worth the paper.”

She shifted her weight and the cloth over her head moved.

“You were not decided,” she said. “You were counted. Your father was counted and then you were counted off him, and there was never a meeting.”

There was a long moment on that landing with the ash going past the window at the end of it.

“That is the reason,” Mei Jiu said. “If you go through on that floor, then this Hall has to write down *servant-born* on a piece of paper in a room, and it has never written that word on a piece of paper about an advancement in three hundred years, and it will never be able to take it back. And then somebody has to look at me and work out why it took me nine years with a sponsor and a room while it took you nothing at all with a cracked gate and a hole in you, and there is not one of them in that building who has ever wanted to have that conversation.”

She stopped.

“I would like them to have it,” she said. “I have been the one it was done to for two years and nobody has ever once asked me how it feels. If you go through, they will ask me how it feels, and it will be the worst morning of my life, and I want it.”

Then she put the cloth over her head again and went down the stair without saying anything else, and her footsteps were careful all the way, which was how Ning Yan knew she had been crying before the ninth step and had decided not to make it an event.

She stood in the doorway with a square of dusty cloth in her hand for a long time, and then she went back inside and shut the door and sat on the bunk with a cough coming up that she held down for about nine seconds before she let it out into the crook of her own arm.

“Do not start working out what she gets out of it,” he said.

“I was not going to.”

“You were already doing it. She has told you what she gets out of it, in words, out loud, and the most offensive thing you can do now is not believe her.”

She did not write it in the log.

That was a decision and it took her four minutes. The log was evidence. The log had a date at the top of a page and a thing written under it that a clerk could not read quickly, and it had carried four county sheets and a whole offer worth more than her life and it was the only weapon she had ever made. If she wrote a fourth-rank inner disciple's secret compression in it, then a person who found the log would have the compression too, and if that person was the Bureau, then a compression nobody had written down would exist in a Bureau hand by the end of the winter.

And if she did not write it down, and Mei Jiu was searched, and a square of cloth was found, then there would be a fourth-rank inner disciple with no floor to stand on.

She put it inside her shirt, against the tablet, and she left the log alone.

“You did the right thing,” he said.

“I did the small thing.”

“It was the small thing and it was correct and you did it in four minutes without asking me, and I want you to understand that I would have got it wrong, and slowly.”

At the fourth hour she came down the nine steps and across to the Hall's side door, because the trial was in four days and the Hall had entered a hearing and a hearing drew its own clerk, and a clerk sitting up the night with nine hundred accounts to go over in the morning was a clerk who would be in that room with the whole winter's business in his head.

The door was propped with a stone because the hinges had packed with ash and would not swing.

Inside, in a corridor with one lamp in it, there were five men around a table with wet cloths over their mouths, and the lamp was turned down to nothing because the eyes of all five of them were streaming, and one of them was Xie Shuo.

He looked up. He saw her. He did not stop writing, because the accounts of a winter are taken at a fixed hour and he was thirty-one years old and he had learned what stopping costs.

“Furnace crew.”

“Clerk.”

He put the pen down. That took him a second and a half to decide, and she watched a man do arithmetic, in front of her, about what it would cost him, and decide to do it anyway.

“There will be nine clerks on the floor,” he said. “For the reading and the count and the times. They come off the accounts, so they will be men who have not been up the night. I am going to ask to be one of the nine.”

“Why.”

“Because you asked for witnesses, and the witnesses are nine men with pens,” Xie Shuo said, “and nobody has ever thought to ask whether the pens are steady.”

He picked the pen up again and went back to the accounts, and she stood in the corridor a moment longer than she needed to, looking at the shape of it: a man arranging to be in a room he did not have to be in, for reasons he could not give anybody, in order to write down correctly a sentence about a girl he had met once.

Then she went back down the nine steps in the ash with her mouth shut and the cloth of Mei Jiu against her ribs, and the wind took her sideways on the stair the way it took everything sideways on the ninth step, and she got to the bottom on her feet.
