# The Assessment Room

Dark came into the assessment room the way water comes into a boat.

Ning Yan knelt on the brick with clay tablets stacked around her knees and forced her hands steady. Her immediate goal narrowed to a single line. Find Ning Wu’s record before her eyes failed.

Resistance was the room itself. Records on fired clay, arranged by year, no names on the outside of anything. Each plate had to be turned to the light, tilted, read for the small brushed name on its face, set aside in order so she could put it back. Dozens per year. Years of intake. Dust burned her throat. Her scar ached from kneeling.

She worked by feel when light failed, fingertip reading the cut year marks, lifting, tilting toward the doorway where grey still showed. Forty minutes alone and counting. No voice. No lean of qi. Only her own stage seven, thin, and her own memory of Feng’s dates.

She found him.

The plate was the size of her hand, heavier than it looked, fired hard. Small fair hand on its face, Bureau script, the name brushed clean: Ning Wu. Thirteen. Kiln intake. Below, three entries in three columns. Date. Disposition. Note.

She tilted it to the last grey.

*Assessed, reassigned, unreconciled.*

She stared until the characters blurred. Assessed, not processed. The kiln did not do what she thought it did. It evaluated. Somebody, somewhere, at some point, looked at a thirteen-year-old servant and wrote down what he was worth, and then the record stopped. No furnace entry. No harvest number. No death line.

The fourth column was reassigned. There was a category of person who left the kiln assessed and did not go into the furnace. Feng’s margin word, here in the kiln’s own hand. Her brother was worth something to somebody, and that was worse than relief. Relief would have ended. This opened.

She wanted to steal it. Her hands closed on the plate. Take it into light. Show Qinghe. Show Feng. Hold proof.

She did not steal. Not yet.

She laid her coat across her knees in the dark, set the plate aside, took out paper and brush and inkstone she carried for tower copies, and copied the date into her own hand on her own knee. It took an hour. Her hands cramped. Ink froze and she warmed it with her breath. She copied every stroke, the name, the three words in exact order, the year mark, the shelf position, until her copy could stand beside Feng’s.

Her hands nearly died from cold. She blew on them, worked them, went on. The assessment room watched without watching, shelves of assessed children in clay, years of worth written down and filed.

When she finished she put the plate back exactly where it lay, dust smoothed over the gap. She tied her copy inside her lining with Feng’s.

Not relief. Worse than relief. Her brother was assessed and then moved somewhere the sect had no heading for, and the record stopped because reassignment was not an ending the kiln was required to close.

Yuan flickered at the edge of her skull as she stood, a thin thread, there and gone, unable to hold. She felt his attempt like a hand reaching through water.

She left the shelves in order. She turned toward the procedure wall, where a board in Bureau hand promised to tell her what reassigned meant in practice. Her legs shook. Her eyes burned.

The answer was not an answer and it was still the first real news she had in six years.

She crossed to the wall and lifted her lampless hands to read by touch what the housekeeping sentence said.
