# Public Ground

The outer yard the morning after a reading held its shape the way a bruise held its colour.

Ning Yan crossed it with her token tied inside her sleeve and the copy sewn deeper, past the laundry lines where the winter wash hung stiff and past the practice rail where the inner girls already worked through their forms, and every step she took on the packed snow was watched by somebody who had stood in the ranks the day before and heard her name read as credit. She kept her face still. She kept her hands folded. She wanted to know whether the furnace column was a debt or a threat or a record before anyone came to collect, and she wanted it in words she could carry without them burning through her sleeve.

Du Hong found her by the well-house with his jaw still wrapped and his name still strange in his mouth.

He had not slept. She saw it in the red at his eyes and in the way he held his shoulders too square, the way boys held themselves when they had been praised and did not trust it. He fell in beside her without asking, the way he now did, as if walking together was a thing they had decided years ago.

“You heard what they called you,” he said. “You heard it and you walked back into the line like it was weather.”

She drew water. The bucket came up black with ash at the rim. “I walked back because there was nowhere else I was allowed to stand.”

“You were made a credit in front of a hundred people. Everyone in the dormitory talked half the night. Lu Fen told the second-rank boys your father’s furnace work bought your run.” He kept his voice low, furious on her behalf, which was worse than curiosity. “I told them to shut their mouths. I did not do it well.”

She set the bucket down. Her scar pulled when she bent. Stage six sat thin in her chest, steady enough to carry water, too thin to carry argument.

“I needed to know what the column did,” she said. “Not what they called it. What it did.”

He glanced toward the terrace where the Elder Hall canopy stood empty and toward the furnace chimney beyond it, which put its iron taste into every breath even here. “I asked the furnace-man’s boy last night. He said the furnace column was where they put the names that owed work. I did not like the way he said it.”

Ning Yan lifted the bucket to the trough. Water slopped, froze where it struck wood.

An account, she thought, was a thing that followed a family for three generations unless somebody with authority wrote it off. The Reader had said *carried to the nearest unspent thing*. She had spent the night turning that phrase over the way she turned the pine splinter in her sleeve, feeling its edges.

“The furnace column is an account,” she said. “I want to know who it asks to pay.”

Du Hong went quiet. That was new in him, that he could go quiet instead of filling the space.

They walked the terrace steps together, up past the sixth where the lamps burned even at noon in winter, and down along the public ground where the sect posted its notices on boards sheltered under eaves. Servant-hands scrubbed the stone. An inner disciple read a bounty list and moved on. Nobody stopped them. Two outer disciples with whole tokens were invisible in a way a girl named as credit was not, and Ning Yan felt the invisibility go thin around her wherever she passed.

The notice boards held the quota in the Bureau’s hand, twenty-eight, and beneath it a sheet in a smaller hand listing furnace dues, oil, cloth, lamp-wick, core-stone weighing fees. She read them the way her mother taught her to read laundry tallies, not for the words but for the order, for what came after what.

Du Hong read over her shoulder. “You think your father’s name is here.”

“I think his debt is here. His name is in the book under the canopy.” She touched the edge of the furnace-dues sheet without lifting it. “Six years. He was taken for a stalled ascension when I was eleven. If the account followed the family, somebody billed us for the taking.”

He frowned. “Nobody billed your mother. She did laundry until the wet lungs took her. Nobody came for money.”

“No,” Ning Yan said. “They came for something else.”

She understood it standing there with cold seeping through her shoes, with the furnace smoke coming down the hill and settling on her tongue. An unsettled harvest account was not money. Money could be counted in a yard. A harvest account was people, and people were settled in kind or in service, which meant the sect had been billing her family for six years in air, in work, in the slow subtraction of being the nearest unspent thing. Her mother folding robes for eleven years without ever being offered the map. Her brother taken at thirteen for the kiln. Herself sent into the Verge with four hands short and a dot waiting.

She had been paying. She had been paying since she was eleven and nobody told her the price.

Anger came up hot and clean. She let it come. Then she did the thing she had learned in the Verge with a knife at her throat, which was to let the hot thing cool until it was useful.

Du Hong watched her face and misread the cooling as surrender. “Leave it alone,” he said. “I mean it. You came back whole. You have a token. Pei Lan walks because you carried him. The yard forgot the furnace column by supper.”

“You did not forget it.”

“I am trying to.” He caught her sleeve, then let go, embarrassed. “Listen. I heard a number last night I did not understand and I am going to tell you because you are the only person I trust to hear it wrong with me. The furnace-man’s boy said the account was six years and some months, and when I asked how many months he looked frightened and said he did not keep numbers that fine. There is a number underneath your name. I do not know it. I do not want you to ask after it where they can hear you ask.”

She looked at him. Eighteen, broad, bad at lying, trying to protect her from a specific number he did not know, with reasons that were all good.

That was the resistance. Not Lu Fen’s smile. Not Mei Jiu watching from the terrace edge. A friend telling her to leave it alone at length, kindly, because he had carried her blood and did not want to carry her loss.

She asked him one question, plainly, the way she had learned to ask things in the tower when lying cost more than truth.

“Did you think the sect would ever settle it honestly, if I left it alone?”

He opened his mouth. Closed it. The furnace smoke moved between them.

“No,” he said at last. Past tense, heavy. “I thought if you left it alone you lived longer.”

She took his answer seriously, which was new. She had spent seventeen years trusting competence over people, following the capable thing that had not lied yet. Now she stood on public ground with a boy who had no competence to offer, only fear, and she let his fear weigh.

“I am going to live longer anyway,” she said. “I am going to find the books.”

“The books are under the furnace,” he said. “Nobody goes under the furnace except furnace crew and elders. You are outer. I am outer. Pei Lan is hurt and cannot help.”

“The books are under the furnace,” Ning Yan repeated. She felt the want settle, cold and precise. Not a document that said her brother was dead. A book that said what the furnace column was for, in ink, where somebody had to look at it. “Then I need to see them without going where I am not allowed.”

Yuan had been quiet through all of it, a low presence counting with her. When he spoke his voice came flat and quick, embarrassed to be heard wanting.

“I am here. I count with you. I do not leave.”

She did not answer him where Du Hong could see her lips move. She touched the copy in her sleeve through the cloth, once, to remind herself that somebody had believed her on paper.

Du Hong sighed, defeated. “You have that look. You had it at the sheath. Tell me what you want me to do.”

“Nothing yet,” she said. “You filed for me. You carried for me. This part I do alone first.”

He did not like it. His shoulders said so before his mouth did. But he nodded, because he was learning how to be refused by her without making it a fight, which was the first adult thing he had ever done well.

She left him at the well-house with the bucket half frozen and walked back across the public ground alone, past the boards, past the rail, with her name sitting in a book under a canopy she could not open. The cold burned her lungs. The scar burned under the binding.

She had been paying in air for six years. She wanted to see the bill.
