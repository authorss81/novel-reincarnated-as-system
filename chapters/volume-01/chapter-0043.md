# What the Voice Knows

The grain cart out of Lowwater was empty except for sacks, and the driver was a woman who gave her name to the driver and to nobody else, and who drove with her elbows locked and did not speak, and who Ning Yan suspected, once, of being one of Wen's, and decided not to ask.

It was the ninth day of eleven and the mountain was four miles up and the Assembly was nine days off the end of it.

She had asked the question at the foot of the tower at the fourth watch and she had been carrying it up four miles of road without an answer, and the answer had not improved with altitude, which was unusual, because most of the things in this world got worse the higher you went.

“Try again,” she said. “Not a refusal. A proper attempt, and tell me where you stop.”

The cart wheels went over the frost heave and the sacks moved.

“I will look at the sentence,” he said. “I will look at the four of them as they are in the four counties, which is how I read anything. And then I will tell you what I cannot do.”

He did it. She felt him go down the whole length of the channel the way a man goes down a ladder in the dark, out into the room where the things he could see were, and it took a long time, and then he came back.

“I can see the sentences,” he said. “All four. I can see that three of them are not what the air gave out. I can see the hole in the Shouyi one, where the name was, the way you can see the hole in a wall.

And I cannot see who put anything in any of them. I look for the hand that wrote it and there is no hand. I look for the office and the office is not a place. There is a place where the sentences are *ready* and there is nowhere that they are *decided*.”

“Ready where.”

“In me. They are ready in me and they come out of me, and I have believed for six years that they come out of nothing, because I have never once had to account for anybody else.”

She let that sit in the cart while Lowwater went past on their left and the river came and went through the willows.

“Keep looking,” she said. “I want to know what else you have never had to account for.”

He looked. It went on for most of an hour, in pieces, the way a man reports a walk through a building he has lived in for years and never looked at properly, and what came out of it assembled itself in her in the order he found it, and the order was not the order she had expected.

He could not read the layer's decisions. That was the first piece and she had known it for weeks. He could read a cultivator standing in front of her like a page. He could locate a technique in a living body and find the line of it.

He could feel a removal logged on a kiln road four miles away. And he could not see, anywhere in any of it, a single point at which anything had been *decided*, and he had lived inside that for six years without once finding the join.

The second piece came at the half hour and it was the reason she had to have the driver stop the cart.

She had been counting his count, and not on purpose. She had been counting her own breath every day for two months because that was what she did on a stage four body, and he counted with her, and it had become a rhythm she felt in her teeth the way a person feels the rhythm of a mill wheel they live beside.

And once each day, for about the length of four or five breaths, he was not there in it.

Not gone. Gone was the sealed room and gone was forty minutes in the dark with a metallic taste and the count wrong afterwards. This was different. This was him being *elsewhere*, in a way she could feel the way you can feel a person in a room looking at you.

It had been happening since the Verge. She had a hundred of them and she had not once put them together, because she had been counting how many he spent and not counting where he went.

“When does it happen.”

He was quiet for long enough that she thought he was going to lie.

“Once a day,” he said. “Near the same hour. It has been once a day since you were on the flats and I have not been able to stop it by being anywhere or by being doing anything.”

“How long.”

“Four breaths. Five.”

“What happens.”

“I am counted.”

The wheels went on. The sacks shifted.

“Inside the room,” he said. “Something comes along the row and looks at me. That is all. It does not ask me anything. It does not take anything. It looks, the way you would look at an entry in a ledger to see whether the ink has run, and then it goes past, and every day it comes down that row at the same hour and it has never once missed.”

He had said it in the flat present voice, and then, immediately, because he was nineteen and had been dead six years and had never once been looked at in his life:

“I have always thought I was a fault in the sky.”

The road came up out of the willows onto the terrace road and the whole weight of Qinghe came into view above her, the terrace steps and the furnace roof and the Hall.

“There is no sky,” she said. “You told me that yourself in the kiln road. There is a room and there is a row and there is a thing that walks down the row once a day and looks at every entry in it, and you have assumed for six years that the thing is the weather and that you are the mistake.”

“Yes.”

“It is not the weather. There are people in the room.”

“Then I do not know which of them I am. It came out of him before he could stop it and it came out exactly the way it would have come out of a mouth. “Ning Yan. I have believed for six years that I am a mistake in something enormous, and that was bearable, because a mistake is not responsible for anything and nobody is.

And you have just told me there is a row and a ledger and a set of hands. If there are hands then somebody wrote me down. If somebody wrote me down then I am an entry. I am in a column.

There is a page somewhere with a shape in it that is me. One day somebody will come down that row, reach my line, and not look and not count and only write, and that will be the day I stop.”

She sat on the sacks in a grain cart four miles below the Elder Hall and listened to a thing with no hands describe discovering it has hands, and she understood with a clarity that arrived like cold water that she was not frightened of this at all.

She had spent her whole life being looked at: in a yard as a credit, in a book as an entry, on a platform as a spectacle. Every single time it had cost somebody else nothing, and the only thing she had been afraid of for two months had not been the room or the row.

She was not going to comfort him. He would have taken it as a transaction and gone back to being a mechanism, and a mechanism that has been comforted is a mechanism that starts asking for things.

“Sit up,” she said.

“I cannot sit up. I have never had a back.”

“Be awake, then. Be sharp. She turned and looked at him as near as the channel allowed, which was the inside of her own attention. “I am not going to comfort you and I want you to understand why before I say the rest.

What I am about to say is not kindness. It is use, and it is the plainest use anybody has ever made of you, and you will get more out of it than you would out of kindness, and I would like you to hear that from me before you start hoping.”

He was silent. She felt him listening the way he had listened to her on the flats.

“In two days the Elder Hall is going to be asked a question it has never been asked, in a room, by a clerk, and after that there is a nine-day clock and after that there is an Assembly with nine hundred people in it and after that I am going to stand on a floor in front of all of them and try to go through a wall that nobody in nine counties has ever gone through.

And for none of that do I need a god. I need something awake inside my head that cannot be lied to about a cost.”

“That is what you have.”

“That is what I had. What I have is a thing that has just found out it is written down somewhere and has gone quiet for the first time in six years. She let that sit. “So be sharp.

Because when we find out who is in that room, one of them is going to reach your line, and I am going to be the only person inside the bound who knows the day it happens, and you are going to be the only thing in that room that knows it too, and neither of us is going to be able to prove a word of it.”

“Agreed,” he said. “I am awake. I am yours. Do not spend me and do not pity me and I will not ask either of those for another year.”

“Good.”

The cart came up onto the terrace road and she was quiet for a mile, because there was a thing she wanted to say once and only once.

“You were more frightened of that than I was,” she said. “It did not make you smaller to me. It is the first thing you have been afraid of in my hearing that was not me.”

“I have been frightened of you every hour since the tower.”

“I know. It is different when the thing in the row turns round.”

He made a sound that in a body would have been almost a laugh, and the terrace came up over the skyline.

She spent the rest of the road on the order of the four sentences, which was the work she actually had.

Where. Who. What left. What held afterwards. Eleven hundred reports in four hundred volumes and the order never varied, and she had spent forty minutes of the copying floor looking at four sentences that were about her and had not looked once at the machine that made the order.

The body did it in that order too. It let go of the top of itself first, and then it took the shape, and it could not do both at once, which was the rule that every cultivator in nine counties had been taught in the first winter of their training and which none of them had ever been told was a rule at all, because it had never occurred to anybody that a sentence could be an instruction.

She had been forcing it. For eleven days she had been pushing at stage eight on a cracked aperture with the pattern taking the leak sideways and she had been trying to take the shape before she had finished letting go, and it had not worked once, and the reason it had not worked once was written in a fair hand in a soot room a thousand times.

Empty. Then take. In that order. On purpose.

The cart reached the foot of the terrace at dusk and the driver put the reins down and looked at her and still did not speak, and Ning Yan got down with the log in her lining and the tablet against her skin and the sheets from three counties folded flat against her ribs.

“I will be at the foot of the stairs at first light,” she said. “If I am not, take the cart down and do not wait.”

The woman looked at her for a moment. Then she lifted one hand off the reins, which was as close as the order came to a goodnight, and drove on down the mountain.

Ning Yan sat on the bottom step of the terrace with her back against the stone and the cold coming up out of the rock and did not go up to the quarters.

She was not going to tell anybody. That was the whole of the asset. Eleven days of being announced bound-wide had taught the terrace to stop being surprised by her, and a terrace that has stopped being surprised does not look up at first light to see whether a furnace-crew girl is on the steps.

She put her hand flat against the cracked place and did the thing she had spent two days being afraid of.

She let go on purpose.

Not a leak. Not a spill from an overfull cup. She opened the aperture deliberately the way you open a hand, and let the top of herself go out of it — and the top of herself was the pattern, eleven days of it, the leak-as-channel, the thing she had found on the flats and had told nobody about — and the whole of it went out through the crack.

It went sideways, because there was nowhere else for it to go.

It ran the length of the pattern and there was nothing left to hold it, so it ran past the end of the pattern and out through her, and she felt the whole shape of herself go out into the cold air of the mountain and her chest go hollow from the sternum to the navel, empty, emptied, an opened hand.

She sat on the step in the dark with a hollow chest and an open hand and no shape at all, and that was the whole of the third clause, and nothing she had ever been taught had suggested it was allowed.

Then she took.

She put the shape back into the empty, and it went in the way a hand goes into a glove, because it had been hers and she knew its every part, and the aperture took it and closed round it, and the crack was still there and would be there when she was forty, and the heat came up out of her from her feet to her scalp and put the world a half-step brighter than it had been.

The small unpleasant sound went through her ribs loud enough to be heard on the road.

She felt all of it. Not the announcement, which she had been hearing all winter. The other thing. For one breath, on the inside of the moment the machine built its sentence, she saw him the way he saw everybody else.

A line in a column, with a space above the word on it, and a shape in the line that was nothing anybody had drawn, going pale at the edges and coming back.

Then it was gone and she was sitting on a step with her back against a wall, shaking so hard her teeth were moving, and she understood that she had just caught him being read and had seen for exactly as long as it took.

She did not tell him. Not that night, not the next morning, not on the road up. She had watched a man's whole life go past in four breaths on a staircase and she was not going to hand him that in exchange for anything, least of all comfort.

“Stage nine,” she said instead, out loud, to the dark, in past tense, with her hand flat on the stone. “I did that.”

“Stage nine,” he said. “And you did it yourself. I did not spend. Nothing came out of you tonight that I took.”

“It came out of me on purpose.”

“Yes.”

“Good,” she said.

She lay on the terrace steps until the sky went grey. She had nothing left to give the day and the day did not know the difference.

The Elder Hall sat in two days to close the winter season. The Assembly for the accounts was nine days behind that. Between them was a clerk, a hearing she had not yet asked for, and nine hundred people who would be in a room because of the weather and not because of her.
