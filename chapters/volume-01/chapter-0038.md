# The Scar

The furnace floor at the second watch was a country of its own, and Ning Yan had been living in it for two weeks.

Heat came up off the plates in a slow tide. Iron ticked as it cooled. Four crews worked in shifts with the furnace mouth open and the whole room went orange and then went orange again, and the noise was men breathing through cloth and shovels landing on coal that had been broken wrong.

The room smelled of hot metal, sweat, and the particular sweetness of worked stone, and after ten days it had stopped being a smell and become a taste at the back of her throat.

She was on the weighing stool with her feet in a bucket of cold brine, which was the crew's custom for shift changes, and her goal was small and had been small for six days. Hold the stool. Do not fall off it.

Resistance was the furnace, and the furnace was not clever. Stage four could not lift what stage nine had lifted, and she was required to lift it anyway, twice an hour, for nine hours, and the difference between those two facts was the entire subject of her life.

She got the tongs up. She got the core-stone up. Somewhere in the fourth hour her arms began to send reports that were not true, and the furnace room filled with the sound of a woman telling herself that her hands were working.

She kept a list in her head. This is the part that is gone. This is the part that is gone. It went on for eleven days like a washerwoman going down a wring.

At fifth watch the shift leader sent her off the stool and made a joke about it that the men laughed at generously, which was worse than if they had not laughed. She ate four ladles of broth standing up because sitting down would have been admitting something, and then she went out into the cold that came up the stair and stood with her back against the retaining wall and let the shaking come where it could not be seen.

That was the whole of it, one sentence, and she had heard it eleven days ago from a laundry floor and had not heard it since. Whatever the thing was that announced things, it announced a change and it did not comment. She had spent the whole winter building a picture of it out of what it never said, and this was the first direct evidence that it was capable of that too.

She put her hand flat on the wall to stop the wall moving.

She had a question she had been carrying since the third day in the crew room and she had not been able to ask it, because it was not a question you asked a person who had done a thing to you. It was a question you asked a ledger.

“What is in the scar.”

“Your scar is not what I took.”

“Then tell me what is under the scar. That is my question. Precisely, item by item, and do not be kind about it.”

He tried. She felt him go at it the way he had gone at it in the bunk room, and this time she had nine days of inventory behind her and she was listening for the shape of the answer instead of the content of it.

“A thickness,” he said. “It came as a thickness. Not a piece. There was a stack of things on you and the top of the stack went first and then the one under it, and I felt it go the way you feel a shelf come away when you pull a board out of a wall.”

“Which shelf.”

“The top. Then the second. Then the third. And under the third there was a fourth, and I did not take the fourth, and I have never understood why I stopped.”

“Why did you stop.”

“I do not know.” Then, quickly, as if he were afraid she would take it as an excuse: “I did not choose it. It ended. It went out of you at a rate and the rate stopped and that was all.”

She stood against the wall with the cold coming through her boots and felt the shape of what he had said assemble itself into something she already understood and had not known she understood.

“You did not take a thing,” she said. “You took the order the thing was in.”

Silence in her head, and a quality of silence she had not felt before, which was the feeling of a man being handed something about himself and not recognising it.

“I do not know what you mean,” he said.

“You do. You are describing a hand going into a shelf and pulling out the board, and you think that took the board. It did not take the board. It took the way the board was put in.

“The shelf is still here. It is stage four and it will do more in nine minutes than a soldier at nine does in an hour, and it will do that again tomorrow, because the shelf is not gone. What is gone is that the boards go in in that order now, and that order was the newest thing I had, and you took it because it was on top.”

“I do not understand.”

“You told me that.”

She pushed off the wall and started up the terrace path, slow, with one hand on the rail. The mathematics of it went along with her and she did not like how easy it was.

Four months of her own practice had bought half a stage at the terrace edge in four months. She had been back to stage nine in eleven days by working, and she had been back to stage four by having four months' worth of order taken out in the time a blade took to fall, and both of those were true. The difference between them was the thing she had just worked out standing on a stair in the cold.

Practice rebuilds the shelf. Nobody can rebuild the order.

She went back to her bunk with that and lay down on her back with her hands on her chest and felt the cracked place under her palms, and the crack was cold, and it did not close, and it had not closed since the records room.

“There is a difference between what was taken and what can be rebuilt,” she said aloud, into a room where nine men were asleep and one was coughing. “Say it back to me. Exactly as I said it.”

“I take the order,” he said. “Not the strength. Not the size. The order. It does not come back.”

“Does the order come back if I practise.”

“No.”

“Does the strength come back if I practise.”

“Yes.”

“Then you have described a thing that takes the newest thing first and leaves the oldest thing, and it has done it twice now, and both times you were in a hurry, and both times you looked at my body and not at my head.”

“I cannot see your head,” he said.

“That is the first useful thing you have said to me since the tower.”

She lay in the dark with that between them and felt the shape of what she had just done, and it was not clean, and she did not want it to be clean. He had handed her a description of his own worst act in the flat grey words a man uses for weather, and she had taken it apart and found the thing underneath, and the thing underneath was that he was the only clerk in nine counties who did not know what he was writing down.

He described things. He did not look at them. He had taken her order and put it in a sentence about shelves and boards and a shelf's being pulled, and he had believed that sentence completely, and the belief had been a hiding place.

“Stop describing things,” she said.

“I stop.”

“No. Listen to what I am asking. You have been telling me about what you took in weather since the day after, and every time you do it I learn something about you and nothing about the account, because you build the sentence out of what you can feel and you never look at what the sentence is doing. I am asking you to stop doing that. I am not going to work inside your metaphors. I have no room in this body for your metaphors.”

“That is not — I do not mean to —”

“I know you do not. That is not the point.” She turned her head on the pillow. “I am going to read you better than you read yourself. I did not choose to be able to do it. You make it easy by talking in weather. When I am tired, and I am going to be tired for eleven days, I am going to be more careful than I am now, because a thing that describes itself in images is a thing that gets to choose the picture.”

She felt him go very still.

“I am not a picture,” he said.

“No,” she said. “You are the reader. And I am going to start paying attention to what you read.”

The furnace floor below them ticked as it cooled, iron giving back the night's heat one plate at a time, and she lay and listened to it go and thought about the two orders of the body. The one that could be rebuilt and the one that could not, and the fact that he had known the second existed and had never once told her, in six weeks, when she had asked him twice and been given true and incomplete both times.

She would ask him about that tomorrow. She would ask it plainly, and she would let him answer, and whatever he said would go into the log in her own hand in a clerk's careful hand, because she had finally understood what the log was for. It was not evidence against the sect. It was evidence against a thing that described itself in weather and called the sentence a record.

She put her hand flat over the crack and slept, which she had not expected to do.

In the morning she was going to have eleven days and a furnace floor and a stage she had to build out of nothing that came back, and the difference between the two orders was going to be the whole of it, and she had found it on a stair in the cold by talking to a man who thought he had been describing weather.
