# The Log

The disused laundry room below the servant quarters held cold the way a well held water.

Ning Yan slipped in after the night count with a stolen ledger under her coat and a brush stub in her sleeve, and bolted the door with a kettle handle. Her goal sat plain in front of her. She needed a record the sect could not take by taking paper.

Resistance was everything she did not have. No lamp oil to spare. No desk. No hand trained for anything but tallies. She was a furnaceman's daughter who had learned letters from the backs of wash tickets, and the Bureau clerks up the hill wrote three hundred characters before breakfast.

She set the ledger on an overturned tub. The book was thick, bound in grey cloth, its front half filled with wash counts in a round older girl's hand. Soap. Lye. Mending. The back pages were blank because nobody wasted clean paper on laundry beyond the month. She had lifted it from the discard pile behind the wash house that afternoon, where ruined books waited for the stove.

She meant to fill those back pages with everything that could hang her.

The tablet lay against her skin under her robe, warm through sweat and cold both. She felt it when she breathed. She left it where it was. Work first. Fear later.

She ground a dry ink cake with spit on a chipped tile. The brush stub was short enough to hide in a fist. She tested it on her thumb. Black bled into the whorl. Cold numbed her fingers between strokes, and she warmed them under her arms the way washerwomen warmed hands between wrings.

Footsteps passed in the corridor outside, a night-watch pair with a lantern, talking about oil ration. She froze with the brush mid-air, breath held, ledger covered by her sleeve. The light moved past the door crack and went on. Her heart beat seventeen to the minute, counted with the voice that counted with her.

She wrote the date in the corner, small, in the cramped shorthand her mother had used for stains that would not come out. Then she stopped, because the next line needed deciding.

If she wrote fair, a clerk would read it at a glance and she would die. If she wrote too poor, even Feng Lushi could not copy it later. She needed a hand that looked like rubbish and kept like iron.

She remembered the man four miles away in Lowwater with a cough and four hundred volumes. His brush shook now. His index failed him. He copied her brother's line in one sitting and his hand worsened while he did it. Clerks did not read what they expected to be rubbish. She had learned that from watching the yard reader skip the laundry sheets without lifting his eyes.

So she wrote badly on purpose.

She copied the Bureau hand the way a washerwoman would copy it trying to learn a few words, strokes out of order, characters tilted, ink blotted where a learner pressed too hard. Inside the poor shapes she hid the true record, using tower marks for numbers, wash signs for names, a dot for the furnace, a cross for the kiln. A clerk skimming would see attempts. Feng would see accounts.

*Assessed, reassigned, unreconciled.* She wrote her brother's three words in wash code, and the date from her knee copy, and the shelf mark, and the procedure line about the stalled opened and the useful part moved. Her hand cramped by the third line. She worked it open and went on.

Yuan stayed close, counting breaths with her, quiet for a long while.

“I am here. I watch. I learn your hand.”

Pride and shame moved through her together, hot then cold. She had never shown anyone her writing. Her mother had praised the tallies and never asked for more. To have a dead man read over her shoulder while she formed each lying character felt like being caught undressed.

“You stayed quiet on the stairs,” she said under her breath, past tense, low. “You did not speak when Qiu looked at me.”

“I am afraid. I stay quiet. I count.”

She dipped the brush. Spit-ink was thin. She made it work.

“I needed you quiet,” she said. “I was glad you were.”

It was the first time she had thanked him without making it a debt. She felt him brighten, then check himself, the way he did when kindness surprised him.

She wrote the tablet's name, the one that was not her brother's, and its date, and where she had put the true copies in her lining. She wrote what Wen had said about fired qi, in wash words for bone and heat. She wrote the night watch turn, and the marker post, and the forty minutes alone with the metallic taste. She wrote that the layer logged removals, and that the note went somewhere she could not see.

The last line she wrote straight, without camouflage, because it was for herself and not for Feng.

*I took it. I carry it. I decide.*

She stared at that line until the ink dried. Hunted that morning. Author by night. The change sat in her chest like a second aperture, sore and new.

She tied the ledger inside her coat with string, flat against her ribs opposite the tablet, paper on one side and fired clay on the other, and understood she now carried two things the sect would kill to open.

She wanted her body fit for what came next. Stage eight held thin under work and cold. Stage nine stood three days of drill away, and the seal beyond that, and the Hall beyond that. She blew out the stolen lamp wick and sat a moment in dark with her hand over the ledger.

She had decided to be a record rather than an entry. Paper would remember what the Hall chose to forget.
