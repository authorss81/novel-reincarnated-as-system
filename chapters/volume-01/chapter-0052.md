# The Tribulation

“It has a number in it,” he said.

She was on her knees on the floor with her hands flat on the plank and everything in the room hanging in the air, and the man in her head said it like a man reading a line off a page.

“What.”

“The weather. Look at it. It is not weather. Weather does not have a number in it, and that has a number in it, and the number is five.”

The whole of that hall was looking at a girl on a floor and a room full of loose paper going slowly upward, and the temperature in the room had fallen off a cliff, and the ash was coming down through the air in a column that was perfectly straight and about four feet across, straight as a plumb line, straight as a thing that has been told where to go.

“A trial requires a tribulation,” he said. “That is not doctrine, that is furniture. Every advancement inside this bound has one and it has always had one, and every one of them has happened inside a building with a roof over it, and none of them has ever happened on a floor. You did not do this. The eighth-day clerk did this. He entered a trial, and a trial is a document, and the bound does not read the document — it executes it.”

“Five,” said Ning Yan.

“Five. And four of them have a place chosen for them and the fifth one has not, which is why it is the last one.”

## The First

Elder Pei Shou was on his feet.

“Clear the floor,” he said. “Clear the floor of everything and everybody and put the member outside the square. Stewards —”

The first strike came down through the roof of the Hall of Qinghe and did not stop at the roof.

There was no good way to write the sound of it. The sound of an advancement was small and unpleasant, like a knuckle, and loud enough to be heard through a wall; the sound of this was nothing like that and went on for a second and a half and was the sound of a large room being struck from above by something that had picked the room on purpose.

The light came down in a line. Not a bolt — a line, narrow, white, the width of a wrist, standing perfectly upright in the air above her with its bottom end about a foot above her head, and she had time to think one thought, which was *it is choosing a place on me*, and then it went in through the top of her left forearm.

It threw her eleven feet. She went across the pale square of worn plank and into the bench at the wall and took the bench apart and lay in what was left of it with her ears ringing and the smell of her own hair burning coming up off her scalp.

“Ninth hour and fourteen minutes,” said Xie Shuo, from somewhere she could not see. “The count of witnesses is nine hundred and sixty-one.”

“Ninth hour and nineteen minutes,” said Xie Shuo. “The count of witnesses is nine hundred and sixty-one.”

He had got back to his table. He had crawled to it. She understood later that a clerk had taken his coat and held it over his head.

She got up.

It took her four attempts and her left arm did about a third of what a left arm is supposed to do, and the burn on the inside of her forearm had a smell coming off it like a struck match in a small room, and she got up on her feet on the edge of the pale square with the room making the sound people make.

“No,” said the Speaker, from the table. “Stewards, the floor. Take her off the floor.”

Four stewards came across it. The first one got within four feet of her and stopped dead, and his partner stopped too, and neither of them had decided to stop; she watched their shoulders decide.

“Take her off the floor,” said the Speaker again.

“I cannot move her,” Ning Yan said, and her voice came out wrong, thin and scorched and nothing like the voice she used for tallies. “Elder, I am sorry. I cannot move. It is anchored to where I am standing and if you carry me off it I lose it and I have not got through yet.”

“Then it is not anchored to a floor. It is anchored to you.”

“Yes.”

Elder Pei Shou looked at the four stewards standing in the middle of his own Hall not doing what he had told them to do, and then at her, and Ning Yan understood the exact shape of the moment: he could not order her off it without killing her in front of a hall, and he could not leave her standing on it in the middle of his Hall while the roof of Qinghe came down, and a man who is Speaker of a Hall is a man who has run out of things he is allowed to say.

“Stand in the square,” he said. “Clear everything else off the floor.”

They cleared the floor. It took ninety seconds and it was the single most frightening thing she had ever watched, because it was orderly. They went to the walls and the galleries and under the stairs and behind the Speaker's table, in ninety seconds, without a sound but the coughing, and then the floor of the Assembly Hall of Qinghe was thirty feet of plank with a girl standing on the middle of it and every person in the building packed into the other two thirds of the volume.

## The Second and Third

The second one took her hand.

She felt it arrive as a column of heat up the arm that had already stopped reporting, and when it left, the arm went out of the conversation entirely, and she made a noise on the floor she had not planned to make and which every person in that room heard.

“Ninth hour and twenty-six minutes,” said Xie Shuo. “The count of witnesses is nine hundred and sixty-one.”

“Ninth hour and twenty-six minutes,” said Xie Shuo. “The count of witnesses is nine hundred and sixty-one.”

He was reading the count because the trial had been entered with a provision that any witness might ask for the hour and the count, and nobody in that building had asked for anything, and a man with a fair hand was doing his job in a building that was being taken apart around him.

Somewhere in the second strike the west gallery let go.

It did not fall on anybody, which was the one mercy in it. It came down across the west door and lay there, thirty feet of dressed oak and two hundred years of roof, and the south door was already pinned under the beam of the Speaker's own gallery, and the north stair that everybody in that room would have gone out through was under nine tons of plank and slate.

Nobody got out. One man tried it — a furnace-man in his forties who went out along the wall and tried to get a shoulder under the west beam — and the beam moved a quarter of an inch and stopped, and he came back and sat down on the floor and put his face in his hands.

The third strike came down through the roof, and took the roof with it.

There was a moment, afterwards, in which Ning Yan lay on a floor with her right arm gone and her left arm burned and her ears ringing, and did not get up, and nobody in the Assembly Hall of Qinghe thought she was going to.

It was two minutes and forty seconds. She knew that later because a man with a fair hand wrote it down, and because for two minutes and forty seconds there was nothing at all happening on the floor of that hall, and every person in that room watched a girl lie on a plank floor in a building that no longer had a roof, and made exactly the sound that a room makes when it has run out of explanations.

She was not unconscious. She was doing the third clause on her back with the ash coming down on her face, because she had let go, and the letting go was the only part that could still be done lying down, and if she was not holding the empty then she was going to have to start again from the top of the ninth.

## The Fourth

She came up on her hands and knees and then on her feet.

The sky over Qinghe had never been visible from inside that building in three hundred years. It was a flat pewter lid with a slant of ash coming out of it, and it was eleven feet above her head now, and it had the terrible ordinary look of a sky over a mountain on a winter afternoon.

The fourth one came out of it.

It went down out of the actual sky, past the broken eaves, and hit the plank six inches in front of her left foot, and the floor of the Assembly Hall was made of oak two hundred years old and it did not care at all.

The ash in the air came up off the floor in a wall and the wall went out through the open side of the building in one flat sheet and was gone.

She was on the ground. She got up.

## The Fifth

There was no place left in that building for the fifth one to choose, which meant it had to choose her, and it did, and she felt it come down the way you feel the exact second a rope goes slack after being tight for a year.

She did not put her shape in.

That was the whole of it and it took her about half a second and she had four days to think about it and nine days after that to understand it.

Every advancement she had ever made had been: let go, take. Hold the shape of what you want, and then put the top of yourself into it. That was the order and it was always the order and it had been the order on the terrace step with nobody watching.

She was not going to be able to take a shape on the fifth strike. Her right arm was gone. Her left forearm was open to the bone in one place. She had been at stage four nine weeks ago and she had a fever and a cracked gate and a hollow in her chest the size of two fists.

So she did not take.

She went the other way.

The qi that had been leaking out of her sideways into a pattern for three months, the thing she had found on the ash-flats and told nobody about, the thing that had cost her the rim of her aperture on every single use — she opened the crack all the way, on purpose, and she let it go *out*.

Not up. Out. Down and away, through her feet into a floor of oak two hundred years old, and off into nine feet of foundation stone under Qinghe, and out into the ash in the street, and away.

The fifth strike came down into a woman who was not holding anything at all, and went straight through her and out through the plank, and took a strip of the floor with it, and did not stop, and did not need to.

And the small unpleasant crack went through her ribs, loud enough to be heard on the terrace a hundred and forty feet away.

[Assembly Hall of Qinghe has no roof. The region does not amend the building.]

She was on her knees in the middle of the floor.

The whole of the Hall was in the open air of a building that had three walls and no ceiling and forty feet of broken oak in the street in front of it, and the ash was coming down out of a real sky onto all of them, and every single one of them had seen it.

Every single one of them had seen her get struck five times and get up five times except the fourth, and the last of those had no hands.

The Speaker was on his feet at the table. The stewards were on the floor. Somewhere behind her a man was reading the hour out loud and had stopped twice and started again, and what he eventually said, in a fair flat voice with a hand that was not steady for the first time in the volume, was this:

“Ninth hour and forty-one minutes. The count of witnesses is nine hundred and sixty-one. The member is on the floor.”

She had got to Condensing stage one.

She had got there by letting go of everything she had, on the floor of the Hall of Qinghe, at the ninth hour and forty minutes of the fourth morning, in front of everybody the sect could put inside a building, and she could feel it sitting in her chest like a stone a man has swallowed whole: a thing of hers, held, that she would keep until she died.

The crack in her aperture was still there.

It would be there when she was forty. It had settled into the shape of the thing now instead of the shape it had been, and it was no longer a hole and it was not a rim, it was a mouth, and it opened and it shut, and it was going to do that for the rest of her life.

The last thing she was aware of, before she stopped being able to stand up at all, was the whole of that enormous open room going quiet in a new way. Not the quiet after the third strike. A different quiet.

Every person in the building had stopped breathing at the same moment, in the same half-second, all the way down to the walls, and she understood from the floor with her face full of ash that whatever had been set moving by a clerk in a corridor on the eighth day of the ninth had come out of the ground at the top of the world, and had reached nine counties, and had made a sound, and that there was not one thing in the entire history of that building that could stop it now.

It was not hers to stop.

It had never been hers.
