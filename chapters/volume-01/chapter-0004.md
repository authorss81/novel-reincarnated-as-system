# Nine Steps

The third day she learned how she walked.

Ning Yan woke with the bound cloth stuck to the scar and Du Hong's coat over her shoulders and eleven stages of memory missing behind her eyes. The camp had moved on without her. Only Du Hong remained, pale with sleeplessness, heating snow in a dented cup over a twig fire that gave more smoke than warmth.

“You bled through twice,” he said, when she sat up. His voice scraped. “I thought you stopped breathing once. You went so still.”

She nodded because her throat would not make words yet. The scar pulled from sternum to hip when she breathed, a hot wire under the skin. Stage four sat in her chest like a room with the furniture taken out. She could feel qi move, thin and careful, avoiding the chipped rim of her aperture.

“You gave him the boundary lamps,” Du Hong said. “Han Shu. You gave him the plate and you sent him down.”

“He took the root and the cup.”

“I know what he took. I want to know if you know what happened.”

She knew. She had known at three in the morning with her arm in two pieces, in the way a person knows a number before they can say it.

She got up and walked down the slope to the boundary lamps, and it took her a quarter of an hour to cover thirty yards, and Du Hong came behind her with his hands open and did not offer once.

Han Shu was where he had been sent. He was against his stone in the ring of lamplight with his back straight and his knees up and his hands resting on his knees, the way a boy sits when he has decided he is going to be found like that. The dead root had gone up and over him and been dragged down on the far side.

He had not been eaten. That was the thing she had braced for and it did not happen. There were no teeth on him. There was a hole in the side of his neck the size of a thumb and a track in the ash behind the stone where something had come down off the ridge on its own line and gone straight on again the way a river goes on, without hurry, because it had somewhere to be.

She knelt down in the ash and put her hand on his cheek. He was cold already and his face was still mostly his face.

“You were going to tell me the sword-edge story again,” she said. “You said you had two more and one of them was about the goat.”

She stayed there until her hands stopped working, which was not long.

Du Hong came and stood over her and did not tell her to move. Eventually he said, “The quota counts him. We take him down ourselves, or we leave him and Shen Xiu writes a line and sends a cart, and he comes down in a cart on the fourth day.”

Ning Yan thought about a cart.

“He walked down with us for six hours of the eighth day,” she said.

“It does not matter whether he walks. It matters who writes it.”

“Get me up.”

Getting up took four separate attempts, and on the third one she put her good hand flat on the stone and understood for the first time that her body had been returned to her with pieces missing out of the middle of it, the way a house is returned after a fire with the middle floors gone and the roof still on.

She tried to stand. Her left leg would not pick up.

It was not weakness. It was absence. The Nine-Step had carried her weight for two years without her noticing, the small turns, the heat-hold, the way the foot landed on the outer edge before rolling in. Now the instruction was gone and the leg waited for orders that never came. She took a step and lurched sideways into the windbreak stones, catching herself with her palms, ash gritting her teeth.

Du Hong caught her elbow. “You lost blood. You rested none. Anyone walked badly after that.”

She wanted to believe him. She let him hold her up while she tried again, and again the leg folded at the same place in the sequence, where step two should have become step three. Her body twitched toward it. Her head offered nothing.

Practice is stored twice, she understood, with the clarity of pain. In the head and in the body. Only one of them is written in.

The voice spoke, low, ashamed.

“You live. I am here. I am sorry.”

She did not answer him. She had stopped answering him in the night, when she asked what it took and he gave her silence. She stopped blaming him somewhere around the fifth failed step and started watching him instead, which was a colder place to be. Blame was hot. Watching lasted.

*Half a stage,* he had said, for the first quest. Plain language. And then the second spending with no words at all, five stages in the time a blade took to fall. She filed both numbers away like tally slips. She would find the arithmetic herself.

“We moved slow today,” Du Hong said, shouldering both packs. “The line went ahead to the second water. Shen Xiu left word you were counted as hurt, not lost.”

Hurt, not lost. She held that phrase tight. Hurt girls were carried on the tally. Lost girls were dots.

She chose to keep moving instead of resting, because the ones resting were the ones Shen Xiu counted. She said it aloud to make it true. “We walked.”

Du Hong stared at her bound chest, at the blood seeping fresh. “You bled through.”

“We walked anyway.”

They walked.

The Verge floor on the third day was a grey waste of fallen trunks and ash-drifts that hid hollows. Every ten paces her leg forgot itself and she went to her knees, and every ten paces Du Hong waited without comment and offered his shoulder without looking at her, which was the only kindness she could have borne. The wind came down off the ridge with teeth in it. Her borrowed coat stank of smoke and boy-sweat and blood.

They came into the second water an hour after dark with Han Shu across Du Hong's shoulders and Ning Yan holding one of his ankles so that the ground would not take him twice.

There was no ceremony. That was the thing she had not been ready for. The fire was made up because the fire had to be made up, and the water was boiled because the water had to be boiled, and eight boys found reasons to be busy for perhaps as long as it took Ning Yan to cross to Shen Xiu and hold her hands out and say, “Han Shu. Second night. Below the first tower.”

The Team Elder wrote it. She stood in front of him and watched the brush put down the first character of Han Shu's name and the second, and the strokes were exactly the strokes, the same ones he had used for the twelve names on the board at the gate, and when he came to the mark beside it he did not hesitate at all.

He wrote the dot on the first try.

“Twenty-four in this camp,” he said. “There were twenty-four. Now there are twenty-two and one of them is not walking.”

Du Hong made a sound like a man hit in a place that does not show.

The boys came and looked at Han Shu lying on his back with his knees up. Some of them had eaten off his plate. Two of them had borrowed his knife and not returned it. One boy, smaller than the rest and nobody’s friend, stood at a distance for a long time and then came and put the knife down beside the body and went away again without saying anything.

Han Shu’s boots were still on. Du Hong had left them on because he had not known what else to do with them, and Ning Yan understood about ten minutes later that she was going to have to do something about them, and that whatever she did was going to be the thing she thought about for the rest of the day.

At noon she found step three.

It did not come as memory. It came as habit. Her foot slipped on an iced root, her weight fell wrong, and her body turned inside the fall without permission, hip dropping, knee bending, heat flashing through the calf. She landed upright, breathing hard, staring at her own feet.

Her head knew nothing. Her legs knew.

That was worse, she realized, standing in the ash with her heart hammering. It meant the layer could take things without taking the habit. It could empty the room and leave the footprints. She pressed her hand to the scar through the cloth and felt tears come sharp, not from pain but from the obscenity of it, that her own thighs remembered what her mind had been robbed of.

Du Hong whooped. “You did it. The turn. You did the turn.”

She tried again, deliberately this time, and fell. Habit was not technique. It answered only when it chose, like a dog that had been beaten and still loved the hand.

The line had made camp on the far bank of the second water by the time she reached it that evening, and she came in with the last three boys rather than at the front of them, which was a different sentence entirely and every boy at the fire understood it. Lu Fen looked up from the far side of the blaze and did not speak. Xie Qiao sat with his wrist bound and his arm in a sling and looked at the fire.

Shen Xiu stood among them with his list-board, counting heads as they came. His eyes rested on her bound chest a moment longer than on anyone else, and something flickered in his tired face that she could not read.

He wrote something after she sat down. She watched the brush move and did not hear what it said, and the not-hearing sat in her all evening like a swallowed stone.

She lay that night with her legs twitching through fragments of steps and understood the ten months were not coming back. Neither was the certainty that anything she built would stay built.

She wanted his number. The voice's number. What it held, what it spent, what it counted when it went quiet.

He would not give it to her.

She fell asleep counting her own breaths, sixteen, seventeen, shallow with pain, and dreamed of a laundry manual burning.
