# Nine Steps

The third day she learned how she walked.

Ning Yan woke with the bound cloth stuck to the scar and Du Hong's coat over her shoulders and eleven stages of memory missing behind her eyes. The camp had moved on without her. Only Du Hong remained, pale with sleeplessness, heating snow in a dented cup over a twig fire that gave more smoke than warmth.

“You bled through twice,” he said, when she sat up. His voice scraped. “I thought you stopped breathing once. You went so still.”

She nodded because her throat would not make words yet. The scar pulled from sternum to hip when she breathed, a hot wire under the skin. Stage four sat in her chest like a room with the furniture taken out. She could feel qi move, thin and careful, avoiding the chipped rim of her aperture.

She tried to stand. Her left leg would not pick up.

It was not weakness. It was absence. The Nine-Step had carried her weight for two years without her noticing, the small turns, the heat-hold, the way the foot landed on the outer edge before rolling in. Now the instruction was gone and the leg waited for orders that never came. She took a step and lurched sideways into the windbreak stones, catching herself with her palms,ash gritting her teeth.

Du Hong caught her elbow. “You lost blood. You rested none. Anyone walked badly after that.”

She wanted to believe him. She let him hold her up while she tried again, and again the leg folded at the same place in the sequence, where step two should have become step three. Her body twitched toward it. Her head offered nothing.

Practice is stored twice, she understood, with the clarity of pain. In the head and in the body. Only one of them is written in.

The voice spoke, low, ashamed.

“You live. I am here. I am sorry.”

She did not answer him. She had stopped answering him in the night, when she asked what it took and he gave her silence. She stopped blaming him somewhere around the fifth failed step and started watching him instead, which was a colder place to be. Blame was hot. Watching lasted.

*Half a stage,* he had said, for the first quest. Plain language. And then the second spending with no words at all, five stages in the time a blade took to fall. She filed both numbers away like tally slips. She would find the arithmetic herself.

“We moved slow today,” Du Hong said, shouldering both packs. “The line went ahead to the second water. Shen Xiu left word you were counted as hurt, not lost.”

Hurt, not lost. She held that phrase tight. Hurt girls were carried on the tally. Lost girls were dots.

She chose to keep moving instead of resting, because the ones resting were the ones Shen Xiu counted. She said it aloud to make it true. “We walked.”

Du Hong stared at her bound chest, at the blood seeping fresh. “You bled through.”

“We walked anyway.”

They walked.

The Verge floor on the third day was a grey waste of fallen trunks and ash-drifts that hid hollows. Every ten paces her leg forgot itself and she went to her knees, and every ten paces Du Hong waited without comment and offered his shoulder without looking at her, which was the only kindness she could have borne. The wind came down off the ridge with teeth in it. Her borrowed coat stank of smoke and boy-sweat and blood.

At noon she found step three.

It did not come as memory. It came as habit. Her foot slipped on a iced root, her weight fell wrong, and her body turned inside the fall without permission, hip dropping, knee bending, heat flashing through the calf. She landed upright, breathing hard, staring at her own feet.

Her head knew nothing. Her legs knew.

That was worse, she realized, standing in the ash with her heart hammering. It meant the layer could take things without taking the habit. It could empty the room and leave the footprints. She pressed her hand to the scar through the cloth and felt tears come sharp and sudden, not from pain but from the obscenity of it, that her own thighs remembered what her mind had been robbed of.

Du Hong whooped. “You did it. The turn. You did the turn.”

She tried again, deliberately this time, and fell. Habit was not technique. It answered only when it chose, like a dog that had been beaten and still loved the hand.

By dusk they reached the second water, a black creek steaming faintly where it cut through ash. The rest of the disciples were camped on the far bank, fires lit, and Shen Xiu stood among them with his list-board, counting heads as they came. His eyes rested on her bound chest a moment longer than on anyone else, and something flickered in his tired face that she could not read.

She lay that night with her legs twitching through fragments of steps and understood the four months were not coming back. Neither was the certainty that anything she built would stay built.

She wanted his number. The voice's number. What it held, what it spent, what it counted when it went quiet.

He would not give it to her.

She fell asleep counting her own breaths, sixteen, seventeen, shallow with pain, and dreamed of a laundry manual burning.
