# The Tree Line

The inner tree line was where the Verge stopped pretending to be a forest.

Ning Yan saw it at noon on the fourth day, past the second water, where the ashwoods grew so thick their crowns knotted together and the light came down in grey shafts full of falling ash. The boundary mark ended here. Beyond was sheath country, where Ashjaws denned in the roots, and the quota parties turned back unless desperate or ordered.

She was desperate. Du Hong's filed plate had made her quarter-token whole on paper, but paper at the boundary needed a record plate to back it, and the pine splinter in her sleeve was only half proof. She needed the sheath cleared and the tower tally confirmed, or Lu Fen's competing claim would eat her alive at the reading.

The morning's arithmetic had done the rest. Thirty-three in the air, twenty-eight on the board, five hands nobody had counted, and a girl who was four hands short to begin with.

Shen Xiu had let them go with a long look. “You came back by dusk,” he said, “or you did not come back.”

Nine of them in the sheath. She counted the breathing through the roots before she ever saw a body, the way the voice taught her to listen, low bellows-rasps overlapping in the dark under the trees. At stage four, with a leg that still forgot step three, it was suicide. With Du Hong beside her, who had never fought one, it was murder.

The voice spoke, grave and quick.

“I offer a quest. You clear the sheath. You bring back the boundary mark. The price is a full stage. There is a real chance the aperture opens. You decide. I cannot lie.”

A full stage. She stood at Body-Forging stage four, thin as paper. A stage was everything she had rebuilt since the night he spent her. And the aperture, chipped at the rim already, cracking open meant stopping wherever she stood, forever, a cultivator who could never hold qi again past what she reached.

But the layer could be bargained with, badly, and the price was always paid by her and never by him. That was the only leash either of them had, that he had to say it plain before she accepted.

*Say the rest of it,* she thought. *You have said a number. Say the thing that goes with it.*

He was quiet for a moment.

“If I say it now you will refuse,” he said, “and I cannot make you clear nine of them alone at stage four, and I will not be able to hold the door.”

“Say the number.”

“The number is one stage and the chance of the aperture. I have given you both. What I have not given you is that the second one is not one in ten. I do not know what it is. I am not permitted to know.” A pause. “That is the first time in six years I have said a thing out loud because I did not know it. I did not enjoy it.”

She put her hand flat on Du Hong's chest before he could speak. She had learned that much. You got the answer in the order that let you use it.

Du Hong nocked his knife wrong-handed. His shoulders screamed fear and determination in equal measure, the set of a boy who had decided to lose bravely on purpose.

She made the second decision of the run.

*I accept,* she thought, *but he stays outside the kill radius.*

“You accept. I cannot help. You do the work.”

“You cannot help at all?”

“In the tree I can hold the ground so that they come to you instead of going round you. If it is opened, I hold nothing and you are alone with nine of them and a body that has already been used as a purse once.” His voice went flat in the way she had learned to distrust. “I want you to know that I said it. You did not ask. I said it, because in six years I have learned that the ones who do not ask are the ones who cannot pay later.”

She turned to Du Hong.

“You wait at the mark stone,” she said. “You carry no blade past it. You live to file.”

“I fought beside you—”

“You owe me living.” She kept her hand on his chest. “You stayed. Do it for one more day.”

He looked at the trees. He looked at his knife. He looked at her, and whatever he found there was enough, because he put the knife in his belt and stepped back to the stone without saying anything at all, which Ning Yan understood she would be paying for a long time.

She refused to let him inside and it cost her the only advantage she had, a second blade and a pair of eyes. She went into the trees alone, lame and scarred and stage four, with habit instead of technique and a pine splinter for luck.

The sheath had nine Ashjaws in it and she fought them one at a time, because that was the only way nine was survivable, and the killing circle was smaller than she had thought.

She started at the outside, working inward, killing the ones that slept deepest and leaving the noise of it to pull the rest toward the mark stone. The first went the way the first had gone, a rock and a knife and her body turning inside the lunge while her head stayed quiet. That was the good one. That was the one where habit covered for the missing pages and she came out of it with her hands still able to hold things.

The second caught her shoulder and tore her sleeve to the skin. She screamed and kept moving, because stopping was dying.

The third and the fourth came at once and she learned something in about four seconds that she had not known at noon, which was that a kill radius is not a circle. It is a set of angles. Two of them from the low grey shapes came in flat and low, and a third came over her from the root-wall above, and the third one she never heard at all, and what she felt was a white flash in her left eye and then her body was lying in the leaf-mould with something across her throat.

A root held it. Nine steps back in her life, when a laundry-hand's son had shown her how to move a load without letting it fall.

She lay under it and let it hold and did not breathe, and above her the third Ashjaw worked at the root with its jaws, and she put her knife in the gap between its teeth with both hands and pushed until the point went through the soft place behind the hinge, and it came off the root and fell over her and was dead before it finished falling.

Her left eye would not open properly for the rest of the day.

The fifth and the sixth came in a place where the ground was wet and her footing went. She lost the sixth, half of it, letting its shoulder roll her into a root and its breath cook the skin off her forearm while her knife went in and out of the hinge in a rhythm she did not remember deciding. The quest's lean was in her legs now, half a step faster than the world, and she used it to run along the root-wall and drop behind the seventh and open its jaw from above.

Blood steamed. Ash stuck to blood. Her scar burned as if reopened.

Somewhere beyond the trees Du Hong shouted her name and stayed where she told him, which was the bravest thing he had done in his life and he was doing it by not moving.

The eighth she killed with her back to a trunk and her hands shaking so badly that she had to hold the knife in both of them to line it up, and the ninth she did not kill cleanly at all.

The ninth was the biggest and it had not been asleep. It came out of the roots already moving while she was still pulling the knife out of the eighth, and it took her across fourteen feet of open ground in a single lunge and it took the left side of her with it. She went down under it and got her knife hand up on its hinge and held, and its weight came down on her arm with her arm pinned to the ground beneath it, and she could feel the bone of her forearm being asked a question it did not have an answer for.

It was in her then. Under her ribs, at the centre of her chest, the cup she had been carrying since the tower. She felt the rim go.

Not break. Go. The chipped edge of it gave under the pressure the way a cracked jar gives when you set it down too hard, and something inside her chest made a sound that was not a sound, and all at once the qi in her came up out of her in a wave that had no heat in it and went through the animal above her and stopped it the way a wall stops a wave.

The breakthrough came in the same breath. Body-Forging stage five. Cold poured up her arm and heat poured down it and the two of them met somewhere in her ribs and tore, and she came apart into a half second in which she could see the whole clearing, every root and every carcass and the grey shafts of light coming down between the crowns, and in that half second she understood that this was what it cost and that she had been told, and that being told had not helped.

Then her arm broke and the animal came off her and she was on her back in the leaf-mould with nine carcasses in a circle and her own blood in her mouth.

She lay there and shook for a long time.

When it ended she stood up, which took four attempts, and she stood in a circle of carcasses with her knife broken to the hilt and her hands empty. Nine. She had cleared nine with habit and spite and a body that had already been used as a purse once that week.

The warmth settled into the voice, faint but real.

“I am clearer. You live. I am glad.”

Then the layer spoke, to everyone, about her.

[Sheath cleared at the inner line. Nine Ashjaws taken. The bounty is settled.]

She had filed nothing. She had carried no plate to any boundary. The layer announced a kill she did not file, because the quest itself was filing, because the machinery honoured what its Chosen did whether any clerk wrote it down.

The announcement put a bounty on the sheath and the boy in it. She understood that as she stood bleeding among the dead, hearing her own work said aloud to every county at once. She was visible now in a way she had never chosen.

Du Hong ran in, white-faced, and caught her before she fell. “You were dead,” he kept saying. “You were dead and you are not.”

She leaned on him, stage five unsteady under her, the rim of the aperture aching in a way that was new and specific and that she did not know how to think about yet, and felt fear of dying for somebody she met four days ago settle cold in her stomach. She could not make herself stop.

They walked out with the boundary mark pried from the root-wall, and the trees closed behind them without sound.
