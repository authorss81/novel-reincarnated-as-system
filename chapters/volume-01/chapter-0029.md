# Ning Wu

The furnace room held its heat. Neither of them left.

Ning Yan sat back down on the bench with the five names burning in her head and asked about her brother. Her goal was single. She needed to hear what the furnace did with a thirteen-year-old taken for the kiln six years gone.

Resistance was truth. Qiu answered the way he had answered before, without hedging, and that was always going to be the problem.

“He was assessed,” Qiu said. “I saw the intake slip. Thirteen, good lungs, steady hands. The kiln marked him assessed and then marked him reassigned. He did not come to my floor.”

Past tense, plain. No comfort in it.

“Reassigned where,” she said. “You signed it. You were Elder of the Furnace.”

“I did not sign it.” He shook his head, heavy. “The reassignment was signed above my authority, in a hand I did not know, and I was not told and I did not ask, because I had not asked about anything above my authority in forty years.”

She stared. Forty years of not asking, said the way other men said forty years of service. Behind him the furnace ticked as it cooled, iron contracting, and the sound was like knuckles cracking one by one.

Her hands ached to write the words down, to fix reassigned in wash code where no clerk could lift it. She held still instead, because a brush in this room would have ended the conversation. Memory would have to serve as paper until night.

“You let a boy go out of your books on a signature you did not recognise,” she said. “You burned five people you remembered and you let the sixth walk off paper.”

“I did.” He met her eyes. Kind, direct, unflinching. “Kept the furnace. Kept the quota. Kept the men paid and the boys fed. I told myself the higher office had its reasons, the way I told myself the bound had its shape. I was wrong to do it. I did it anyway.”

She came to get a monster and got a function. Grief moved through her, then anger, then something colder and more useful. A department had a manager. A manager had a hand. A hand could be recognised, even if nobody at Qinghe knew it.

Yuan spoke, low, present.

“I mark this. I keep it. I do not forget.”

She pressed her palm flat to her thigh to steady herself. The tablet warmed against skin. The ledger edged her ribs.

“You remembered five,” she said. “You forgot one because he was taken upward. I will not.”

Qiu nodded slowly, as if she had passed some examination he had not set.

“Looked for him in the records room,” he said. “You will not find him there. The reassignment page is the only copy in nine counties, and it sits under my floor, and the Hall barred that door last winter. But you are stage nine now, and the Hall meets, and offers are made. I am telling you this because you asked, not because I expect you to stop.”

She stood. Her legs held. Her breath came seventeen to the minute, counted with the voice that marked and kept.

She decided, entirely on purpose, the first decision in the book she made without being driven by bell or blade or winter. She was going to find out what sat above the furnace's authority, signature and hand and office, whatever it cost to read.

The hunt acquired a direction and it pointed upward.

She said the next question out loud before she left, past tense, steady, because there was no point stopping now.

“Asked what happened to the ones you did not reassign. You told the five. You told me true. I want the rest.”

Qiu looked at the furnace mouth, where heat breathed, and his kind face settled into lines she would remember for years.

“Stayed for that answer,” he said. “You will not like hearing it, and I will tell it anyway.”
