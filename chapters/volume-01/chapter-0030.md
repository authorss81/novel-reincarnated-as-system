# The Offer

The furnace room cooled a fraction as the firing settled.

Ning Yan stayed by the bench with her hands folded to hide their shaking. Her goal had narrowed to understanding. She needed to hear the offer before it was made formally, so she could refuse it without flinching.

Resistance was its goodness. Qiu made it anyway, plain, without ornament, and it was real.

“Furnace crew,” he said. “Servant-rank inner disciple. A bed indoors through winter. A stipend in oil and grain. Your family account written off on the day you signed. The condition was that you stopped asking about the reassignment.”

Past tense, steady. Each term laid like coal on a scale.

Her breath caught. A bed indoors. She had slept in dormitories where frost formed inside the shutters, where girls coughed through winter and called it weather. A stipend meant her hands would not crack to blood by second month. Written off meant no more furnace column, no more nearest unspent thing, no more six years billed in air. It meant sleep without counting footsteps. It meant winter without choosing between oil and grain.

She looked at his hands, folded, scarred at the knuckles from forty years of furnace heat. Decent hands. That was the worst part.

She wanted it. She wanted the bed more than she had wanted anything since warm food as a child.

“You applied my father's account yourself,” she said. “Six years. You were the one who kept it open.”

“I was.” He did not look away. “The sect's single most useful lever over servant-born, and I was the hand on it. Told myself it kept girls alive by keeping them obedient. Believed it most winters.”

Yuan spoke, tight, present.

“I am here. I doubt. I warn.”

She asked what happened to the ones he did not reassign, because she had promised herself she would, and because wanting the bed made asking harder and therefore necessary.

He told her the truth again.

“They were fired,” he said. “The stalled were opened. The useful part was moved into the failing. Both were filed under the same number. The tablets you have seen described were fired with what remained. That was the procedure. I oversaw it. I signed it. I believed the bound required it.”

The room tilted. Heat pressed. He kept answering honestly and it was the most frightening thing about him, more frightening than cruelty, because cruelty could be refused and honesty had to be carried.

She thought of Wen's disgust, professional and specific. She thought of the plate against her skin, warm, carried for weeks. She thought of five names, one not a servant, and a sixth taken upward in an unknown hand.

“I wanted the bed,” she said aloud, past tense, raw. “I wanted it enough to hate you for offering it.”

Qiu nodded as if she had spoken fairly.

“Refused it here and you refused it cold and hungry,” he said. “Accepted it and you lived to ask later from inside the building. I am not telling you which was wiser. I am telling you the offer stood, and would stand in the Hall, and would be honoured.”

She stood. Her stage nine strength felt thin under honesty. She needed air, and the stairs, and three days to lose an argument she had already lost in her heart.

She was going to refuse something she needed. That was the consequence, and it sat in her chest beside paper and clay.

At the stair she turned.

“Kept the names,” she said. “You gave them freely. I will not spend them cheaply.”

She climbed out of heat into cold with the offer burning behind her, real, generous, lethal, and the refusal already costing more than acceptance ever would have.
