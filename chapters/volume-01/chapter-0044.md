# The Terms of the Hearing

The Assembly Hall anteroom at Qinghe was a corridor with two benches and a clerk's table at the end of it, and on the morning the Hall closed the winter season there were eleven men in the corridor waiting to be let in and one of them was sitting against the wall with a cloth over her head.

Ning Yan had not slept. Stage nine had come out of her on a terrace step two nights ago by opening herself on purpose, and everything that had gone out of the hole had gone out of her, and what was left was a body that could lift and could not hold on. She had two days of practice in it and she had spent both of them on the flat form and both of them had gone the way water goes through a cracked cup: fast, and out.

She got up when the door opened. She had decided the night before that she would not be sitting down when the Elder Hall came through that door, because a room full of men who have already decided what you are will finish deciding it faster if you are underneath them.

They came through it, and they stopped, which she had not planned for and was glad of.

Nine elders in Hall coats, in the order they walked, which was the order they had walked in for forty years. Qiu Zhulang was fourth. Elder Pei Shou was first, and first meant Speaker, and the Speaker had a winter account roll three inches thick under his arm and a list-holder on his left and a clerk on his right.

The clerk was young for it, about thirty-four, with ink to the second joint of his middle finger, and he had the particular stillness of a man who has learned that his handwriting is his career. Ning Yan had never seen him before and she would remember the name on the docket he wrote for the next eight days.

“This is the furnace crew,” Shen Xiu said, from behind them, in the voice of a man reading a line off a board. He had a list-board again. He looked at it and not at her, and she was glad of that too.

“We have nine minutes before the accounts are taken up,” Elder Pei Shou said. “Furnace crew, you were not on the docket.”

“No,” Ning Yan said. “I put my name on it yesterday. On the docket board, in the place for requests, in the hand of a furnace-crew servant. It is still there, under three things about lamp oil. Nobody took it off.”

Somebody behind the Speaker made a sound that might have been amusement. She had wanted the sentence on a wall before nine o'clock.

“State it,” the Speaker said.

She had thought about how to say it for two days and every version she had made was worse than the plain one, so she gave him the plain one.

“I am at Body-Forging stage nine. I have no sponsor and therefore no seal and therefore no right to attempt Condensing. I am asking the Hall to rule on whether I have the right to ask for that right, in this room, in front of witnesses, and to enter my answer in the record.”

The corridor went quiet in a way she could feel.

“Remove her,” said an elder from the back.

“No,” said the Speaker. He had not looked at her once. He had looked at the docket board, where her name was in a servant's hand under lamp oil, and then he had looked at the roll under his arm, and she understood that he was doing arithmetic rather than judgement, and that arithmetic took longer. “Clerk. Is it docketed.”

“It is docketed,” said the clerk. “Received at seventh hour yesterday, written up, and entered.”

“Then the Hall must rule, or the clerk must enter a non-ruling, and the entry goes up with the accounts in nine days.”

Which was the whole of the door, and it was not a door anybody had built. It was a door made of the ordinary business of an office, and the Speaker's own clerk had put the handle on it before she had come up the stairs.

The clerk had the Hall's own book open before anyone asked him to, which told her he had expected this, or something very like it, and had prepared for it, and that his whole career had just become a matter of the next ninety seconds.

Elder Pei Shou said, “There is no precedent.”

“There is not,” the clerk agreed. He said it without any pleasure at all. “There is no precedent in this Hall or in the printed orders for a member born servant-rank to petition for a public advancement hearing. The orders provide for sponsorship. They provide for sealing. They provide that any dispute entered in writing against a disciple's standing shall be resolved at a session of this Hall with witnesses present. They provide that any member may lodge a petition in writing, entered and read where witnesses are present.”

He looked up.

“They provide that a non-ruling shall be entered and sent up.”

Nobody spoke for a moment.

“Then rule, then,” said Elder Pei Shou, in a voice with no opinion in it at all, and Ning Yan understood that she had won the first half of it and had not yet found out what the second half cost.

The clerk wrote for about four minutes. Nobody interrupted him, which told her that the Elders in that corridor had spent their careers learning that a clerk with a pen is the most dangerous person in a room.

Then he read it back.

It was nine sentences long and it was the single most absurd document she had ever heard. Where a dispute has been entered in writing against the standing of a disciple, and no sponsor's writ has been produced, and the member is at stage nine, the member may petition that the dispute be heard as a dispute concerning a public advancement.

The Hall has no precedent. The Hall is not able to supply a precedent. The Hall is required to hear the petition because the alternative is that the petition travels upward to an office which can and will rule, and the cost of that is not the Hall's to spend.

And then the last sentence, which she felt in her teeth.

“Where the petition is granted, the member shall be sealed for Condensing within nine days, a hearing having no subject without a body entitled to attempt one.”

“In nine days,” repeated the Speaker.

“Sealed,” said the clerk, “within nine days.”

“By whom.”

“By the Hall,” said the clerk, and he looked up for the first time, and his face was not unkind and was not bold either, and it was the face of a man reading a rule to the letter. “The objection that prevents her from being sealed is the objection of this Hall. If this Hall is the objector, this Hall is the writ. I do not have an order saying so. I have three hundred years of it not saying otherwise.”

Silence.

From the fourth seat, Qiu Zhulang said, in past tense, quiet, in the voice he used for furnace tools, “I will write a sponsor's writ.”

Every head in the corridor turned.

He stood up to do it. He was sixty-one and he got up slowly and he did not look at Ning Yan while he said it.

“If I write it, it is a favour,” he said. “A favour is revocable, and it is revocable by this Hall, and it is revocable by whoever holds the furnace next, and any man in this corridor can do the revoking. She would stand on a floor in nine days holding something I could take back with a sentence, and there is not one of you in coats who would not take it back, and I include the man who wrote the rule.”

He looked at Elder Pei Shou as he said that, flatly, and the Speaker did not move.

“A writ from a person can be taken back,” Qiu said. “A writ from the Hall's own objection cannot, because then it is not a favour, it is a finding. I am the wrong man for this and I have been the wrong man for this all winter and I would like the record to show that I offered.”

“Declined,” Ning Yan said.

She said it before she had finished understanding why, and then she understood it, and it was the right answer and it was the worst one.

“I am not going to be permitted by a man who can take it back,” she said. “I am going to be obliged by a document, in a room, in front of witnesses, or I am not going at all.”

Qiu Zhulang sat down again. He put his hands on his knees and looked at the floor for a while, and she saw the exact moment when a decent man understood that he had been told the truth in public and had lost something, and she filed it in the place where she kept the things she was not going to be able to pay back.

“Clerk,” said Elder Pei Shou. “Enter it.”

“It is entered,” said the clerk, and put the pen down, and picked it up again, and said, in the same voice, “The petition of Ning Yan, servant-rank of the furnace crew, at stage nine, for a public advancement hearing, on the Assembly floor. Entered this day. Witnesses, eleven.”

“Eleven,” somebody said.

“Eleven,” said the clerk. “I counted.”

And that was the moment. She had come up the stairs with two days of not sleeping and a hollow chest and a cracked aperture and the three county sheets folded against her ribs, and she stood in a corridor in front of eleven men who had decided what she was in a yard three weeks earlier and heard a clerk say the number of witnesses out loud, because a number of witnesses is the only kind of record she had ever trusted.

Not a witness who believed her. A number.

She had spent her entire life being looked at. In a yard as a credit. In a book as an entry. On a platform in Lowwater as a spectacle, and the looking had cost every single other person in the room nothing at all.

She was about to ask to be looked at on purpose.

“I will want the seal within the nine days,” she said. “And I will want the Assembly floor. Not a side room. Not a hearing before the Hall. The floor, with the accounts being taken, in front of whoever has come for the accounts.”

Elder Pei Shou looked at her for a long moment, and it was the first time he had.

“That will be nine hundred people,” he said.

“Yes,” said Ning Yan. “Nine hundred is the number I want.”

The Speaker's mouth did something that was not quite a smile and not quite anything else.

“It will not be the accounts that decide it,” he said. “The floor is for accounts.”

“The floor is where witnesses are,” she said. “You told me nine is what I needed. I have asked for nine hundred.”

Elder Pei Shou looked at Shen Xiu, who had a list-board and had not raised his head, and then at the clerk.

[Nine days are set against a body that has not been sealed. The region does not know what they are for. It has begun to keep the count of them.]

“Enter the Assembly floor,” he said. “Enter the ninth day. Enter that objection may be lodged in writing until the eighth, and that if no objection is lodged the hearing stands.”

“And if an objection is lodged,” Ning Yan said, “enter what it is.”

“It will be entered.”

“No,” she said. “I will read it, in the room, with the witnesses in it. That is the term.”

The Speaker stopped. She felt the corridor balance on her, and it took about two seconds, and it went her way, and she did not know until the clerk dipped the pen that it had.

“It will be read,” said the Speaker. “Session is at the ninth hour. This is concluded.”

## The Corridor Afterwards

It took a while to get out of the corridor, because nine elders do not move at the speed of one girl who wants to be sick in a stairwell.

Qiu caught her at the turn. He did not touch her arm. He stood a step back and gave her the whole of his attention, which in forty years he had presumably given a great many people and which in this corridor had cost him something she had watched him pay.

“Nine days,” he said. “And on the eighth, an objection. It will come from inside the Hall and it will be signed by three men who want my furnace and do not care in the slightest whether you live or die.”

“I know.”

“You do not, because it has not happened to you yet.” He was quiet for a moment. “They will come to you in a corridor. They will not threaten you. They will have a document already filled in, and it will be generous, and it will be better than the last one, and it will be in a servant's hand so that you think it came from your own side.”

“Thank you for the warning.”

“I am not warning you. I have been warning you since the furnace room and you have not once taken any of it, and you have taken every part of it.” He looked at her for a moment. “Read it before you throw it away. That is all I have left to give anybody in this building and I am going to give it to you anyway, and that is what I am.”

She went up the terrace with the stone under her feet and the hollow in her chest and nine days on the wall in front of her, and she did not feel triumphant and she was not sure she was allowed to.

She felt like a girl who had asked to be counted, in public, on purpose, in front of the only institution that had ever counted her.

Behind her, in the corridor, a clerk was writing a name down in a fair hand for the ninth time in nine days, with eleven witnesses entered against it, and in nine days somebody would find out his name.
