# Fair Hand

The Lantern Register tower an hour before evening reading smelled of hot oil and lung-blood.

Ning Yan climbed the narrow stair with her oil writ in hand and her heart beating too fast, stage seven unsteady under the climb. The lamp-room at the top burned day and night, lined with shelves of bound paper, every spine dated in the same beautiful hand. Feng Lushi sat at the desk with a cloth pressed to his mouth, thinner than she remembered from a week before, ink under his fingernails, brush-case worn smooth at his belt.

He looked up and recognized her. Coughed. Smiled through it.

“You brought paper again,” he said. “You are the girl with the voice.”

“I brought thanks,” she said. “And a question I could not ask last time.”

He gestured to the stool. The night’s sheet lay half finished, characters still wet. Outside the lamp-room window Lowwater hunched along the water, shutters closing against evening ash. In an hour the layer might speak and nineteen Listeners would write, and the job would eat them a little more.

Ning Yan had one goal and she held it tight. Ask Feng Lushi one question about Ning Wu and get an answer she could hold.

She asked it plainly. “My brother was taken for the kiln when he was thirteen. His tally closed last winter. I want to know where his name sits in your books.”

Feng did not look surprised. He had written down everything for twenty-two years and had never once been asked to look at it. It was four hundred volumes and he was dying and the index was in his head, and nobody at the Bureau, nobody at the sect, nobody in Lowwater had ever asked him to find a name.

“You carry him,” he said, as before. “Everyone who climbs here carries someone.” He rose slowly, bracing on the desk, and went to the shelves. “Ning Wu. Kiln intake. Thirteenth year. I remember the night it was announced. No announcement for intake. Only for harvest. That is why you ask me.”

He pulled volumes with shaking hands, three, four, five, checking dates against memory, muttering numbers. His cough shook the shelves but never the line of his thought. Ning Yan sat with her hands folded to keep from helping, because helping would break his order.

He found it.

He laid a volume open, turned pages thin as skin, stopped at a register in his own fair hand from years past. Three columns, neat, headed in Bureau script. Names, dates, dispositions. Her brother’s name sat there in ink that had not faded, Ning Wu, thirteen, kiln, with a mark beside it she did not know.

Then Feng turned the page sideways and showed her the margin, where in smaller hand, outside the sect’s copies, ran a fourth column with no heading in any copy she had ever seen.

Ning Wu’s line extended into it. A single word stood there, brushed quickly, already less fair than the rest.

She stared. Hope came up so sharp it hurt, the first since she was thirteen, arriving in the worst possible handwriting, because her brother was not where she spent six years assuming he was. Not in the furnace column. Not processed. Somewhere else, in a column nobody inside the bound had a word for.

“What does it say,” she whispered.

Feng squinted. His hand had gotten worse by the hour that winter, and the degradation was a plot fact she would follow for years, the fair hand going ragged at the edges. “Reassigned,” he read. “It says reassigned. The sect’s books do not have this column. I kept it because the air said it and the Bureau told me not to write what the air said when it disagreed with the form. So I wrote it here.”

Four hundred volumes, and the truth in the margin.

She asked him to copy it out, all of Ning Wu’s lines, with dates, and he did, in one sitting, brush shaking, blood spotting his sleeve, pausing to cough into the cloth and then going on. She watched every stroke, memorized the order, held the paper still for him when his hand shook too badly.

Yuan spoke once, hushed, present only.

“I see the ink. I remember with you. I do not leave.”

When Feng finished he tied the copy with thread and pressed it into her hands. “Do not show this at Qinghe,” he said. “They have no heading for it. They will take it as error and burn it.”

She bowed low, tears hot. “Why did you keep it?”

“Because somebody spoke it,” he said simply. “And I write what is spoken. That is my job, even when they tell me it is not.”

She walked down the tower stair into the dusk with her brother’s name in Feng’s failing hand against her chest. The evening reading bell rang above. The lamps lit along the road.

The kiln road waited, and the sealed door. She had hope now, in bad handwriting, and it was heavier than despair.

She did not look back until she reached the milestone, and by then the lamp-room glowed gold above Lowwater like a coal that refused to go out.
