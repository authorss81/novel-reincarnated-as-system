# Eleven Days

The ash-flats below the terrace were grey and flat and about a mile wide, and at dawn they had wind coming across them from the Verge that went straight through cloth.

Ning Yan came down to them with eleven days in her head and a place chalked on a slate by the drill-master for practice that had not happened yet. Stage four, a cracked aperture, no sponsor, no seal, and a season's account still open in the Elder Hall behind her.

She stood out in the middle of it with her arms loose and waited for the day to begin.

“I cannot make you strong,” he said.

“I know. Say it in the order you mean it.”

“I cannot make you strong. I cannot shorten it. I cannot grant a stage the way I grant a quest, because a stage is not a quest and there is nothing in me that pays for one. The only thing I have ever had that bought a stage was the only thing I am not going to take again.”

“You are not going to take it again because I will not let you, and you know I will not let you. Say the rest.”

“The rest is that I cannot do this for you and I am going to be in your head for all eleven days of it, and there is nothing at all I can do on any of them.”

“Then be quiet and count.”

“I will count.”

Resistance on the flats was weather, and weather was honest, and she had been beating weather for two months. What she had not gone back to since the record room was the sect, and the sect got her at midmorning on the practice ring where four inner disciples were running the terrace form and the drill-master was standing at the rail with his slate.

She asked for the ring at fourth hour. The drill-master looked at the slate, and then at her token, and then at the ring.

“The inner ring is for sealed and sponsored,” he said.

“The outer sand is for Body-Forging work. I am doing Body-Forging work.”

“The outer sand is for outer disciples. You are furnace crew.”

Du Hong, who had come with her and was already in an inner coat, started forward, and she put her hand flat on his chest without looking at him, which stopped him better than a word would have.

“Do not,” she said, past tense, low. “You have one coat.”

She went and worked the outer sand until noon and it was the worst ground she had ever trained on and she thanked it, because the worst ground was free and did not require anybody's permission.

At noon Mei Jiu came out and stood at the edge of the sand with her sleeves tied back and her own account written all over her face, and waited until Ning Yan had finished a form before she spoke.

“Four people have tried to go up from a low stage in under two weeks,” she said. “Three of them were inner. I watched the third one from this rail. She got to seven and then she stood in the infirmary for a month and came out at five.”

“Did you warn her.”

“No.” Mei Jiu's mouth moved. “I told her what it cost my mother and she did it anyway, and I have not said anything to anybody since. You are welcome. I am not being kind. I would rather the Hall be frightened of what happens if you are refused than be proved right about the seal by watching you break.”

She went back inside.

Ning Yan worked the outer sand until dark, and at dark the flat went cold from underneath, and she went back to the flats the next morning and did it again, and on the second morning something gave.

The shift came up out of the bottom of her with heat in it and a small unpleasant sound behind her ribs, exactly the sound a knuckle makes when it cracks, and there was a moment where the whole world had a colour it had not had before.

[Body-Forging. Stage five. Ning Yan of Qinghe. The sentence goes out across the bound the way it goes out for everybody, and nine counties hear a servant-rank girl go up a stage at dawn on the flats below the terrace.]

Body-Forging stage five, on the second day of eleven, built out of hauling core-stone and cold and nine hours of being careful.

She was halfway to being proud of it when the leak started.

Qi moved out of her through the crack. She felt it go the way you feel water leave a bowl with a chip in the lip, sideways, uselessly, and the pressure at her sternum dropped and nothing came of it. She had been leaking since the records room and had put it down to the crack and had not looked at the rate of it. She looked at it now.

It went out. Every time she pushed. Not slowly, not in a haze; a clean sideways loss, like a man emptying a bucket over the side of a cart while the cart is moving.

It had been costing her a fifth of everything she moved. She had been training at four and working at five and she had not known the difference because the difference had been sitting exactly where she could not feel it.

For three days she fought it. She worked the flats from dawn to dark and every time she pushed, qi went out through the crack and into the ash, and by the fourth day she had got stage five half an inch higher.

She understood then that the crack was not a place she could seal. Nothing inside the bound repaired an aperture. A hole in the thing that holds the qi is a hole in the thing that holds the qi, and she had one, permanently, and it was not a wound any more. It was an outlet.

On the fourth day she was sitting in the ash to get her breath back and she pushed once, hard, in the terrace form she had been drilling since she was fourteen, and the qi went out through the crack and it went sideways through the pattern instead of out.

She felt it happen.

Not much. A thread. But the pattern was there, the shape her legs still held and her head could not name, the one that had been lifted out of her in the tower and had never come back, and the leak found it and ran along it and the form finished itself.

Step four. The turn where she was small. The weight that went past him and not into him. It ran the whole length of the pattern and came back to her chest, and the pressure at her sternum came up instead of going down.

She did not breathe for a moment.

She stood up out of the ash and did it again. The leak went sideways. The pattern ran. The pressure came back up and there was more of it than had gone out, not more qi, not strength, more of the shape being used, and it filled the last of the space where the order used to be, which was the space he had emptied.

She did it eleven times in an hour and every time it cost her: heat out of her shoulders, a fine tremble in both hands by the eighth, and by the eleventh she was sitting in the ash again with her mouth tasting of iron and the crack leaking steadily into nothing.

Then she stood up and went and did the form again from the top, with the leak, on purpose, and it worked again.

“You cannot describe this,” he said, and he was right, and she had not asked.

“No. You cannot. That is the first thing I have needed in eleven days that you are no use for.”

“I can find it. I can tell you where it is in the body. I cannot tell you what it is.”

“Then find it and keep quiet.”

She sat in the ash on the flats and looked at her own hands and felt the shape of what she had.

Every cultivator in nine counties was taught that damage was a bill. Every one of them would have told her that a cracked aperture was the end of the road, and they were right, and it was not the end of the road. It was the road with a hole in the side of it.

Damage could be a technique if you were willing to spend it, and the bill was not paid once and cleared. It was paid every single time she used it, and it was paid in the same place.

She had found that out on the worst night of her life, on a stair in the cold, in the middle of an argument about the shape of her own chest.

She told nobody.

Not Du Hong, who would have carried it to the Hall in a week out of the good in him. Not Qiu, who would have made a rule of it and forbidden it and been right to. Not the log, which she decided that afternoon would hold it anyway, in wash code, under a date, with the word for what it was written in a language only two people in the bound could read: the leaky jar, and the servant's habit of carrying the drain in both hands.

She got stage six out of the pattern on the sixth day and stage seven on the seventh, and the seventh day was the day the region said it out loud, and by then she was so far past what anybody at Qinghe believed possible that nobody on the terrace bothered to be surprised and that turned out to be the cruellest outcome available.

She had expected the Hall. What she got was a kind of silence up the hill, the way a room goes quiet when a sound has gone on too long to be interesting.

On the seventh night she lay in the crew bunk with the tablet against her ribs and the log in the lining and understood two things at once, which she wrote down.

The first was that this was hers. It came out of the worst night of her life, and it was not going to be taught, and it was not going to be thanked for, and it was the first thing she had discovered in this book that belonged to her outright.

There were no conditions on it. Nobody had commissioned it. She had found it on the worst possible night and it was hers, and the price of it was hers, and she was not going to stand in a yard and let a man with a slate congratulate her about it.

The second was that she was going to need something for the last four days that he could not give her.

Stage five came out of hauling. Stage six came out of the pattern. Stage seven came out of the pattern with the leak. Stage eight would come out of the same place, and stage nine would come out of the place where the pattern met the aperture's rim and the whole thing either held or did not.

That was the moment that could not be forced, could not be drilled, could not be found by a voice that could locate a technique in a living body and had never once been able to put it into words.

She needed the order of the last two stages. Written down. In somebody else's hand, by somebody who had been listening for twenty-two years and had never once been asked to.

The cart up to Lowwater left at first light. It cost her the day there and the day back and two of the four she had, and she lay in the bunk and did the arithmetic, and the arithmetic came out where she knew it would, because it had been coming out like that all winter.

She was going to spend two days travelling to ask a dying man for a list, on a body that leaked every time it moved, and come up the hill with one day of margin and nothing to spend it on.

She set the alarm in her head for fifth hour and lay in the dark with the crack cooling against the tablet and the pattern sitting in her legs like a shape she had been given for free.
