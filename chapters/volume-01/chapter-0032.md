# Inside

The furnace crew's quarters smelled of sweat, oil, and hot iron that never left the clothes.

Ning Yan learned the building's routines in ten days because she was extremely good at it. She had been a laundry-hand's daughter for eight years and reading a room was the family trade. Who slept where. Who owed whom. When the floor was washed. When the elders came down and when they sent word instead. Which furnace-man drank and which one prayed and which one kept his tally stick honest.

Her goal was to be useful and not noticed. She hauled coal. She scrubbed the floor plates. She learned the weighing songs. She slept in a bed indoors for the first time in her life and woke ashamed at how deeply she slept, how her body unfolded into warmth like a hand opening.

Resistance was competence itself. She was so good at belonging that belonging began to work on her. The men called her Little Ning by the third day. The cook saved her the crust. The shift leader showed her how to read the furnace mouth colour without burning her brows.

On the tenth day the furnace floor gave her the procedure in full, live, in front of her. A stalled disciple was brought down, grey-faced, stage six gone wrong, carried between two crewmen. A failing senior waited above, breathing hard, sponsor hovering. The elder on duty read the order in Bureau hand, flat, housekeeping. Intake. Opening. Moving. Filing. The words were exactly what the wall in the kiln said, word for word, and worse in practice because here it was warm and the men doing it were on first-name terms with the elders, calling up for tea while they worked. The stalled boy wept without sound. Nobody looked at his face except her.

She stood with a coal scuttle in her hands and watched ordinary men do it. Not monsters. Employed, undertrained, paid badly, joking about supper while they strapped the stalled boy down. The horror was administrative. That was the book's whole argument, and it landed in her stomach like swallowed coal.

Hard, clean hatred came up, which was better for her than the complicated kind. She could hate a procedure. She could not hate Qiu's kind face while she hated the floor, and so she chose the floor.

She made two friends in the crew that week, both furnace-men's sons, both kind in the offhand way of people with little to give. One showed her the back stair that avoided the elder's eye. One shared his oil ration when her lamp ran dry. She learned their shifts and their mothers' names and did not write them down, because writing would have marked them. Both would die in Volume 2, and she would carry that, but here they lived, laughing, teaching her the weighing song's rude second verse.

Yuan spoke little inside, present, subdued.

“I watch. I count. I grieve.”

She answered at night, under blankets, past tense whisper.

“Stayed with me. You saw it too. We do not look away.”

On the last night of the ten days the shift leader told her the crew went down to the records room twice a month for tally check, and the next check was in three days, and new crew carried the lamps.

She lay in her indoor bed with the tablet against skin and the ledger under her pallet, stage nine steady, hatred clean, and understood the building had shown her everything except the paper underneath it.

The records room sat under the floor. She would carry a lamp down to it. That was next.
