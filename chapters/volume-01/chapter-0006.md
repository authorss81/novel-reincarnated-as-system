# What the Layer Says

Ning Yan did not trust herself to sleep.

The tower after midnight funneled wind and ash through every gap in the lashed poles, and her scar throbbed with the cold. Du Hong snored faintly against the wall with the jaw-plate wrapped in oilcloth under his head like a pillow. Outside, the Verge breathed, vast and patient, twenty-two disciples and a Team Elder scattered along the water in fires that looked from above like sparks on black paper.

She needed four hours. She did not dare take them. At stage four, with Lu Fen still circling, sleep was a thing other people did.

So she counted. That was what she did when fear would not leave room for rest. She counted breaths, counted heartbeats against breaths, counted seconds between the wind gusts to keep her mind from running back to the blade stopping a finger from her throat and the cold pouring out of her chest.

The voice kept her company without speaking, a low presence at the back of her skull, dimmer than before the spending. She felt him counting with her. That frightened her more than she wanted to admit, that he was lonely enough to count.

She had been trying all night to work out what he was made of. She had got nowhere useful and had arrived, in the small hours, at the only method she had. She asked him things and watched how long he took.

*What is the number of people walking on that ridge right now,* she thought. *Do not tell me who. Give me a number.*

“Nineteen.”

*How long did you take.*

“Four seconds.”

*And the number of people in Lowwater.*

“I cannot see them. They are inside. I see inside only when it matters.”

*What else is inside.*

“Everything the layer covers. All of it. Nothing outside.”

She filed that away in the place where she kept the tally slips, and it was the single most useful thing she learned on the third night, and she did not understand what it was for.

Then the night split open.

It was not sound. It was the absence of all other sound, as if the wind and the fires and Du Hong's snore had been lifted away at once. A sentence arrived, whole, in the air and in the bone, audible to every living person at the same moment, in a voice that was not a voice.

[Inner disciple Mei Jiu breaks to Body-Forging stage eight. Her diligence is recorded.]

Ning Yan sat bolt upright. Pain knifed through her scar. Du Hong woke choking. Outside, someone shouted. Someone else laughed in disbelief. Up and down the water, fires flared as people stood.

The sentence hung in the cold, then faded, leaving the wind to rush back in.

They came apart on the far bank. Ning Yan heard it through the wall of the tower, which was the worst part, because the tower was supposed to be the one place on the run where you could not hear the rest of it.

“Mei Jiu,” a boy said. “Mei Jiu at Qinghe. Third rank inner, the one with the sponsor’s token, she went to eight?”

“She went to eight.”

“In the practice hall? At this hour?”

“Does it matter where she was?”

“It matters that I was here and she was there.”

That was the shape of it. Twenty-two people had been pulled out of sleep at the same instant by a sentence about a girl none of them had spoken to that year, and the first thing they had done was ask where she was.

Ning Yan stared at the pole roof with her heart hammering. Mei Jiu. Fourth-rank inner disciple, the girl who had beaten her in the yard last autumn with clean sleeves and a sponsor's token. Breaking to stage eight at Qinghe, miles west over the ridge, in the practice hall with its braziers and its maps.

And every person in the bound had heard it. The lamp-menders in Lowwater. The clerks in their towers. The farmers in counties she had never visited. People three counties away with no reason to care about one inner girl's diligence had just been told, at four in the morning, in the middle of their sleep.

The layer was not a sky. It was a room with people in it.

She understood it all at once, cold and complete. Announcements reached everyone inside the bound and no further. They were audible, not written. They reported and never advised. The layer spoke to everyone equally, which meant it was not on her side. It would announce her death as readily as her breakthrough, to the same audience, in the same flat tone.

She lay there and did the arithmetic she could not stop doing, which was the only thing she had that nobody could take off her.

Stage four tonight. Stage four in the spring. Stage nine was six weeks away at the rate she had managed since the run started, and then stage nine was the end of Body-Forging forever, because nobody in this sect had ever given an outer disciple the map past it. Nine was a wall. Past nine there was a seal, and the seal was the Elder Hall's, and she was standing well outside the part of the room where the Elder Hall gave things away.

*How many people know what I am right now,* she thought.

“Eleven,” Yuan said. “Nine here. Two in the yard at Qinghe who can read your intake.” A pause. “The reading is not public.”

“Then keep it that way.”

“I do not write the reading. I cannot write anything.” A pause. “I am sorry. That was not reassurance. That was the truth and it did not help you.”

Du Hong whispered, “Did you hear—”

“Everyone heard,” she said. “That was the point.”

Xie Qiao came to the tower mouth a quarter of an hour later with his bound wrist held against his chest. He did not come in. He stood in the gap where the wind went past and looked at the dark behind her, and his face had the loose frightened look of a boy who had been the most dangerous person in a fight three nights ago and had discovered that the fight was not the largest thing in the world.

“They are telling everyone everything,” he said.

“They are telling everyone one thing.”

“Then it is not for us.” He said it the way a boy says something he has only just worked out and is already ashamed of. “Mei Jiu is fourth rank. She is worth a sentence. We are third rank. We are worth the number on the board.”

The tower was very quiet.

“Why are you telling me that,” Ning Yan said.

“Because you heard it first.” He shifted his weight. “You were sitting up. I saw the lamp.”

“You came to find out whether it meant anything for your hand.”

“Yes.”

“It does not.” She said it flatly, because it was true and because he had almost been killed by a girl who was now telling him the truth about his own place. “It means nothing for your hand. It meant something for mine, and it will mean nothing for mine either, and both of those are the same sentence.”

Xie Qiao stood in the wind and took that. Then he said, “You do not sound afraid.”

“I am sitting in a tower on four hours of sleep with a body that has been used as a purse,” she said, “and I am too tired to do the face.”

He went away. Du Hong, who had been awake for all of it, made a sound in his throat that was not quite a laugh.

“He came to ask a girl who cut him whether the sky was telling on him.”

“He came to ask whether he was worth a sentence.”

“Was he?”

“No.” She pulled the binding cloth tighter and felt the scar pull back. “That is the entire finding, Du Hong. Not one of us is. The thing that tells everybody everything does not know that any of us exist, and it will say our names the same way it says hers, in the same voice, on the same night, and the only difference between her and us is that a clerk will write hers down.”

Du Hong was quiet for a while.

“Then what is it for,” he said at last. “If it is not for us, what is it for?”

The voice spoke, thin with awe and fear.

“I hear it too. I am inside it. I am small inside it.”

She pressed her palm to her sternum, feeling the chipped rim of her aperture ache. He was inside the thing that had just spoken. A man with no hands lodged in the machinery that told the whole bound when a girl breathed wrong. The scale of it made her dizzy.

Then her mind, stubborn and servant-born, did the absurd thing it always did at four in the morning. She counted the seconds the sentence had taken to arrive and fade, measured it against the distance to Qinghe over the ridge, and worked out how fast the sound must have traveled to reach the far counties at once.

Too fast. Faster than any horn, any drum, any runner. It had not traveled at all. It had happened everywhere.

She lay back against the cold stones with that knowledge like a weight on her chest. The world had gotten larger and colder in the same breath.

On the far bank, Lu Fen laughed too loud about Mei Jiu’s luck, and kept laughing past the point where the joke stopped being funny, in the way of a man filling a silence he does not want examined. Shen Xiu sat long over his list-board with his brush unmoving in his hand. The whole camp talked for an hour about a girl who was not in it.

Toward the fourth hour she asked the question she had been holding behind her teeth since the sentence arrived.

*Who writes it down on your side,* she thought. *You hear it. Somebody hears it and puts it somewhere. Who.*

Silence, and then, flat and ashamed:

“I do not know. I hear it. I do not know who hears me.”

She lay with that. It was not the answer she had wanted and it was the first answer he had given her that she believed entirely, because he had said *do not know* the same way a man says it when the alternative is a lie he is not strong enough to tell.

Somebody was writing this down. Somebody with a hand, in a room she had never been in, on a night she would not be at.

She wanted to see the page. She wanted it the way she had wanted four hands on the first night, which was to say completely, and for reasons she could not have defended to anyone.

Ning Yan did not sleep. She listened for whether the night would speak again, and hated that she now wanted it to.
