# Lowwater

The Lowwater roundhouse was a low stone building with one door and no window that anybody had cleaned in a decade, and it smelled of wet wool and lamp oil and the particular sourness of men who sit indoors for eleven hours.

They put her in the back room on a bench with her back to the wall, which she noted, and did not spend the first hour being afraid. She spent it being accurate.

Three of them had walked her down. Two were young and one was not, and the older one was Old Gao, who had hauled water up to the terrace laundry every summer of her childhood and had once given a scrawny boy a full pail without being asked and been given nothing for it but the habit of it.

He had not looked at her once since the weigh-station. He had also not touched her once, and there was a place in the second hour where his hand came up about six inches from her collar and stopped and went back down, and she saw the other two men notice, and she understood that the collar was the whole of the evening.

Her coat was on a hook by the door with her bundle beside it. The log was in the lining of the coat.

The Clay Tablet was against her skin under her shirt with the string of it between her breasts, and it would come off in one pull, and a searched girl in a back room with a lamp is a girl whose clothes come off, and if that happened then the kiln, the assessed, and the six years of her brother's record came out of her body and into an office in a town of nine thousand people.

“You are not to be searched,” she said. “Not until the paper is completed.”

The young one said, “The paper gets completed first,” and reached past her for the hook.

[Removal from sealed ground is noted upward. The note takes four days to arrive and arrives first to the county, then to the Watch, then to a room with one door.]

Old Gao put his hand on the young man's arm.

He did it low and without ceremony, the way a man moves a lever he knows the weight of, and the young man took his hand off the hook and stepped back, and nothing was said about it then and nothing was ever said about it afterwards.

“You want the paper first,” the captain said from the doorway. He had not been in the room before. He was forty-one or forty-six and had the face of a man who has done arithmetic in front of angry people for twenty years. “That is correct. I will thank you for asking.”

He held the sheet up so she could see it, which no one had done yet, and she looked at it, because refusing to look would have been the one thing that made her dangerous.

It was a single sheet, Bureau hand, and it was a detention form, and it had a subject line and a clause line and a signature line, and the clause line read: *Detained for removal of kiln material from sealed ground, contrary to the sixth clause of the Standing Order of Register and Sealed Ground.*

“They have written me down for the wrong crime,” she said.

“There is one clause for taking things out of sealed ground.”

“There are two.” She did not move from the bench and she made her voice the voice she used for reading tallies out loud in the wash house, which was the only voice she had that nobody ever argued with. “I stood in front of the sixth clause in the assessment room at the Broken Kiln and read it by hand because there was no lamp.

The sixth clause is about kiln material. Fired clay, fired core-stone, the output of a kiln. It is theft. It is punishable by the county and it is not a sect matter, because a sect cannot lose fired clay, it can only be robbed of it.”

“And?”

“And there is a ninth clause, which nobody reads, because it is four lines long and it is about the assessed. It says that a registered assessed may be removed from sealed ground by order of the register keeper, for the purposes of enquiry, on the keeper's own hand. It is not theft. It is procedure. And it is the only reason that room I walked out of had a shelf in it.”

The captain did not answer. He read the form again, which was the first honest thing he had done.

“Your name is not on a register keeper's order,” he said.

“No. It is not. That is because no register keeper has ever issued one, and it is not because I stole anything.” She tipped her head very slightly. “Which is it going in the clause line, Captain. Take a thing out of a kiln, or carry a person out of a register. You can only write one.”

She could see him deciding, and it was not a decision about her. She had spent two months learning to read shoulders and this was the first time she had read one and found nothing behind it except a household. That was the whole of him. A man with a form and no authority over the form, and behind the form a house with people in it who ate because he kept the post.

“I cannot write *refused* on a Bureau note,” he said.

“No. You cannot. So you will hold me on your own authority, and you will write your own name in the line under it, and then you will wait for Qinghe to send somebody, and they will take four days, and they will arrive with a writ and a clerk and a debt.”

“And you will sit here four days.”

“Yes,” she said. “That is exactly what I am counting on. I have a stage to reach in four days and I would rather spend them in your room than on the road, and I am telling you that plainly because I would like you to know that you are not a villain in this and I would like it written down that you were not.”

He almost laughed. He put the sheet down on the bench beside her instead, which was a thing no one had done yet either.

“No,” he said. “Out. Not to the yard. To the square, in daylight, on the road, where people can see me walking you the whole way.”

“Why.”

“Because the roundhouse has one door and no window and I am not spending four days in a stone box with a girl I cannot search. Walk her through the market in front of me, in front of the whole town, and let anybody who wants to say something to me say it now.”

He had out-thought her. She had come in expecting the small frightened men and she had got a man who had survived twenty years by being visible.

“Read it there,” she said. “If you are going to have it read, read it in the square, with the paper in my hand.”

He looked at the paper, and then at the door she had put her coat on, and she saw him calculate the two costs against each other, and she saw which one was larger.

“All right,” he said. “In the square.”

## The Square

The market square at Lowwater at sixth hour was wet cobble and about two hundred people and a reading platform of three boards and a lectern, because Lowwater read the layer's news aloud every evening in the open and had done so since before the tower.

Captain Lu Tao put her up on the platform himself. He did not have to. She got up on it and turned round so the whole square could see her face, which was the single most useful thing she had learned all winter, and the noise came down off the roofs a little.

She did not speak first. She made him speak first, with a form in his hand, in front of two hundred people, and it took him a moment to work out that she had done it deliberately, and by then he was already beginning.

She read it for him.

She took the log out of her coat lining in front of the square and held it up, and she told them what it was — a page from a laundry ledger she had taken from the discard pile behind the wash house, and the writing on it was so bad that a Bureau clerk could not read it quickly, which was the only reason she was still standing here.

Then she read out the part of the page that mattered, in plain words, slowly, with the square going quiet the way a square does.

“Ning Bo, furnace-man, taken for a stalled ascension six years past. Account unsettled. Balance carried forward to the nearest unspent thing. Ning Yan, credit held.”

She had read that in a yard at Qinghe three weeks ago in front of a hundred disciples. She said it now in a town square in front of two hundred people, and the square made a noise, because the sentence was not a Qinghe sentence. It was a sentence about a man in a town of nine thousand, and it had been sitting in a yard for three weeks and had not come here.

“Three weeks,” she said, from the platform, with the log held up in both hands. “You heard it in a yard. I am not telling you anything you have not heard.

I am telling you the paper in the captain's hand says I am charged with *removal of kiln material*, and the sixth clause of the Standing Order is about stealing fired clay from a kiln, and I did not steal fired clay from a kiln, I walked into a room with four shelves in it and read the wall, and I walked out again, and there was a boy with a bad cough carrying water past the door who saw me walk out and did not stop me.”

That was a lie, and it was the only one she told, and she told it out loud to two hundred people who would repeat it for a year.

The captain was holding the form and reading the clause line again, and then he looked up and said the thing that saved him, which she had known he would say because it was the only sentence available to him.

“The clause is wrong,” he said, to the square. “I will send it up corrected tonight.”

The noise in the square was not for her. That was the part she had not arranged and the part she understood within about four seconds, because the whole of Lowwater knew what a register was, in the way that everybody inside nine counties knew what a furnace column was, without ever being told: it was the paper where the people who went into the machines were written down.

A woman at the front of the crowd said, loud, “Whose kiln.”

And a man behind her said, “Ask her which kiln. Ask her.”

And somebody at the back said, “The broken one. The one under the low road.”

For about four seconds she was standing on a platform in a town square in front of a crowd that had not come to help her. They had come to find out what she had taken out of the room where the assessed were kept, and there was a version of the next ten seconds in which a mob decided that the girl who walked into that room was the problem, and the version was decided by whether she could make them ask.

“I took a plate out of it,” she said. “I have it on my body and I will show it to nobody, because it is not mine and it is not yours and the clerk that says otherwise is a man with a household.”

“Whose plate.”

“I do not know,” she said. “There is a name burnt into it and I have not been able to read it and I will not read it in a square.”

That was the sentence. It stopped the crowd colder than anything else she could have said, because it told them she was carrying somebody and not selling them, and Lowwater had spent two hundred years learning the difference.

The captain had the form folded in his hand and the whole square watching him, and she could have finished him. She had it in the lining of the coat and she had been carrying it for two months and it would have taken about forty seconds: the Lowwater winter return, the in-kind figures, the captain's own hand at the bottom of a page that said a number of people had settled a quota.

She looked at his face and she thought about a register with a household behind it and she decided, in front of two hundred people, that the second page was not a thing she was going to do.

“Captain Lu Tao,” she said. “Your name, your authority, your hour and your place. Say it now and let them hear you, because in nine days it will be the Qinghe clerk saying it and then it will be yours.”

He said it. All four parts. His voice went thin on the household and she let it.

She came down off the platform and got her coat and the log went back inside the lining before her hands were steady, and she thanked him. She said thank you to Captain Lu Tao, flatly, in front of his men, and it was the first time in this book that she had thanked anybody for something that had cost them.

At the corner of the market he stopped her with two fingers on his sleeve, not hard.

“Your page.”

She gave him the date and the shelf mark and nothing else, and he wrote both in a small hand on the back of his own form, and she understood that he was not going to use it and was going to keep it, and that was the arrangement.

She walked up the mountain road in the dark with stage eight in her chest and three days of the eleven gone and the pattern turning quietly in her legs off the leak, and she was carrying two things she had learned at the bottom of a hill.

The first was that leverage felt like this. It was not satisfaction. It was the discovery that a person could be read at forty paces and moved, and that the reading worked, and that it had worked, and that somewhere in the middle of it she had enjoyed it.

She had never in her life enjoyed anything the way she enjoyed that, and the enjoyment had come on at exactly the moment when she had chosen not to hurt a man with a household, which told her something about herself she did not intend to look at directly.

The second was that the small people had not been the problem. Old Gao had put his hand on a boy's arm and said nothing. The captain had out-thought her. The reader on the platform had been a boy of nineteen who took direction and did it well.

Every one of them had done the small honest part of a large piece of paperwork, and every one of them would have carried it out exactly as written if the sect had written it out, and the reason she was walking up a mountain instead of sitting in a stone box was six lines of a clause she had read by hand in the dark.

So it was not the Watch, and it was not the captain, and it was not the two young men. It was the paper. The paper was the problem, and it went up, and the paper was written somewhere she had not been yet.

She turned that over twice on the road and then put it down, because she had one more day before the four days ran out and one thing she could not carry up the hill without.

She got to the terrace at full dark and went straight down to the copying floor under the tower, because the sheets from four counties were lying rolled in a bin where they always lay, and she wanted to see her own name in Lowwater's ink before it was tomorrow's news.
