# The Second Watch

The watchtower on the second water was smaller than the first, a stump of stone with a roof of lashed poles that funneled the wind straight down the throat of anyone inside it. Ning Yan drew second watch with Du Hong because no one else wanted the tower in the wind, and because Lu Fen and Xie Qiao had told the camp she was cursed and best left to her own fire.

She sat with her back to the lee wall, knife across her knees, bound chest aching with each gust. Stage four was a thin blanket in this cold. She could feel the other disciples' eyes on her back when they thought she slept, measuring how weak she had become, how easy the jaw-plate would be to take now that she walked like a lame dog.

Du Hong fed twigs to the fire. His hands shook. He had wanted an inner place since he was eleven, he had told her on the march, the way boys told things in the dark they would deny by daylight. His mother carried water for the practice hall. He had watched inner girls go past with clean sleeves for seven years.

“You filed nothing yet,” he said low. “Lu Fen told Shen Xiu you agreed to file together. He lied.”

“I knew he lied,” she said. “I watched his shoulders when he said it.”

“What did you plan?”

She looked at the fire. The claim was the thing she actually wanted. Four hands whole, the quarter-token made, her name counted as a person. Without it she was short again, and short girls were substituted. But holding the claim made her a target for every strong shoulder in camp, and at stage four with a leg that forgot itself, she could not hold it through the night.

She read shoulders. A servant's skill. Her mother had folded robes for eleven years and known which inner disciple would accuse theft before the accusation came, by the set of the back. Ning Yan had that inheritance, and it won her fights that strength lost.

Lu Fen wanted the kill. Du Hong wanted a future. Shen Xiu wanted four names without trouble. She wanted to live.

She made the trade before fear could stop her.

“Tomorrow you filed the Ashjaw plate under your name,” she said.

Du Hong stared. “Mine?”

“Yours. You coiled rope. You carried me. You kept the hinge true. No one doubted you were there.” Her voice stayed flat with pain. “I kept the tower's record plate. The wood one with the chalk marks. That was what I wanted anyway.”

It was true and not true. The record plate was a splinter of pine with the tower's tally chalked on it, worthless for quota, priceless for proof. She had pried it from the tower wall while bleeding, because some instinct deeper than arithmetic told her that what was written down lasted longer than what was carried.

Du Hong's face worked. “You gave away four hands. You were short.”

“I was short and now I am shorter,” she said. “You were ambitious and now you are filed. Someone in this camp owed me a debt after carrying my blood all day. I chose you to owe it.”

He laughed, startled, then wiped his eyes roughly. “I was bad at lying. Everyone knew it. Now I carried a lie to the boundary.”

“You carried a true thing with my name off it,” she said. “You learned to lie better.”

The wind screamed through the pole roof. Somewhere out in the ash something cried once and stopped. Du Hong added the last twigs and sat shoulder to shoulder with her against the cold, and Ning Yan realized with a jolt under the breastbone that she had made a friend for the first time in her life and had not noticed it happening. No one had ever sat beside her without wanting her stool.

The voice spoke, very quiet.

“You give away what you need. I do not understand. I am grateful.”

She did not answer him, but the cold place in her chest eased a fraction. He watched. She watched back. That was the arrangement now.

Near dawn Lu Fen came to the tower mouth with Xie Qiao's broken wrist behind him, ready to press the competing claim, and found Du Hong with the jaw-plate wrapped and witnessed by Shen Xiu's own mark from the evening count, and Ning Yan with nothing but a pine splinter and a scar. The lie died on his tongue. He could accuse a lame girl of theft. He could not accuse Du Hong, whole and witnessed, without calling Shen Xiu a fool.

He left. Du Hong exhaled shakily.

“You used me,” he whispered. “And you saved me.”

She nodded, eyes closing. The boy she used was now something she had to answer to. That was the reversal, and she felt its weight settle alongside the scar.

She slept an hour before dawn with her head on her knees, and dreamed of breath counted aloud.
