# The Second Watch

The watchtower on the second water was smaller than the first, a stump of stone with a roof of lashed poles that funneled the wind straight down the throat of anyone inside it. Ning Yan drew second watch with Du Hong because no one else wanted the tower in the wind, and because Lu Fen and Xie Qiao had told the camp she was cursed and best left to her own fire.

She sat with her back to the lee wall, knife across her knees, bound chest aching with each gust. Stage four was a thin blanket in this cold. She could feel the other disciples' eyes on her back when they thought she slept, measuring how weak she had become, how easy the jaw-plate would be to take now that she walked like a lame dog.

She was not asleep. She was listening to four boys talk about her in a voice pitched to carry only to each other, which was the version of careful they had learned.

“The Ashjaw was there,” said one. “She heard it before the line did. Ask anybody.”

“Ask Han Shu,” said another, and stopped, because Han Shu had been under the ash for a day and a half and the rule was that you did not use the dead as a subject of conversation at a fire. The silence lasted four seconds and then somebody said “she” again, in the same tone, and moved on.

Du Hong fed twigs to the fire. His hands shook. He had wanted an inner place since he was eleven, he had told her on the march, the way boys told things in the dark they would deny by daylight. His mother carried water for the practice hall. He had watched inner girls go past with clean sleeves for seven years.

Lu Mei came up at the second hour with the wick from her sleeve and a length of cord, because Du Hong’s fire had burned down to a blue eye and she was the only one on the third watch who had thought to bring spare wick. She crouched on the lee side with her hood up and looked at Ning Yan’s binding cloth, which had gone through twice in one night.

“Do not tell me,” she said. “I can see. You are leaking.”

“I am not leaking. I am seeping. There is a difference and it is in the word.”

“It is not in the word, it is in the quantity.” Lu Mei wound the cord around a stick and set it across the ash to catch the heat, and only then did she look at Ning Yan’s face. “You went to the boundary lamps.”

“Yes.”

“You went down there on a body you could not carry, in the dark, on a leg that does not work.” She said it without any admiration whatsoever. “Why.”

“Because Shen Xiu writes a line, and if there is a line there is a dot, and if there is a dot then Han Shu goes down in a cart on the fourth day with the wrong things written on him.”

Lu Mei was quiet for a moment. Behind the lee wall the wind found a gap and went through it with a note like a cracked pipe.

“That is not a reason,” she said. “That is a procedure.”

“It is the reason I had.” Ning Yan turned the knife over on her knees. “The other reason is that he gave me his wick and I did not give it back.”

Lu Mei made a small noise that might have been a laugh and might have been something else.

“You are going to be very bad at this,” she said. “The surviving.”

“Yes,” Ning Yan said.

“I am going to be very bad at it also, then, since we are doing it together.” Lu Mei pulled her hood tighter. “Do you know what Lowwater is going to say about all of us? It is going to say the run took four, and it is always four, and the four are always the ones nobody at the sect had a use for. That is how the arithmetic is supposed to work.”

“It is not going to say that. Han Shu did not have a use for.”

“Then it is going to be a very bad arithmetic.” Lu Mei settled against the wall. “Wake me for the third hour. Do not let Du Hong do it, he will fall asleep in the middle of the watch and blame his feet.”

Du Hong said, from the other side of the fire, in a small voice, “I would not fall asleep.”

“You fell asleep during the sixth-stage part of last night,” Lu Mei said.

“That was different. I was carrying.”

“You were carrying. You were also asleep. Both were true.”

She went down at once, in the way of girls who have decided that sleep is the correct response to anything, and was gone before she had finished settling.

“You file a plate under my name,” Ning Yan said, when the dark had settled back over the fire.

Du Hong stopped. The twigs stopped moving.

“Say that again.”

“Tomorrow, at the boundary, you file the Ashjaw plate under your name. Four hands, whole hinge, your hand on it, and I stand beside you and I do not say one word.”

Du Hong stared at her. “Mine?”

“Yours. You coiled rope. You carried me. You kept the hinge true with a loop around the jaw because you thought of it before I did, and nobody doubted you were there at all.” Her voice stayed flat with pain. “I kept the tower's record plate. The wood one with the chalk marks. That was what I wanted anyway.”

“It was not. I watched you go for it. I was holding the lamp.”

“You were holding the lamp. I went for it anyway.”

“It is a splinter of pine with numbers on it.”

“It is a splinter of pine with numbers on it, and the numbers are in a dead clerk’s hand, and in six months every clerk who was there will be somewhere else.” She turned the knife over again. “You do not understand what you are being given. Listen to it: nobody on this run will ask you where your four hands came from. They will assume you were lucky, and lucky boys are not asked questions. Lucky boys are put at the front of the next line.”

Du Hong’s face did something complicated.

“You gave away four hands,” he said. “You were short. You are going to be shorter.”

“I was short and now I am shorter. You were ambitious and now you are filed. Someone in this camp owed me a debt after carrying my blood all day. I chose you to owe it.”

He laughed, startled, then wiped his eyes roughly with his sleeve. “I was bad at lying. Everyone knew it. Everyone has known it since I told Mei Jiu I had never seen a Condensing breakthrough and then described one.”

“You carried a true thing with my name off it,” Ning Yan said. “You learned to lie better. That is the first thing I have ever taught anybody, and I taught it in a tower with the wind coming down the neck of it.”

The wind screamed through the pole roof. Somewhere out in the ash something cried once and stopped. Du Hong added the last twigs and sat shoulder to shoulder with her against the cold, and Ning Yan realized with a jolt under the breastbone that she had made a friend for the first time in her life and had not noticed it happening.

The voice spoke, very quiet.

“You give away what you need. I do not understand. I am grateful.”

She did not answer him, but the cold place in her chest eased a fraction. He watched. She watched back. That was the arrangement now.

She read shoulders. A servant's skill. Her mother had folded robes for eleven years and known which inner disciple would accuse theft before the accusation came, by the set of the back. Ning Yan had that inheritance, and it won her fights that strength lost.

Lu Fen wanted the kill. Du Hong wanted a future. Shen Xiu wanted four names without trouble. She wanted to live.

That was the whole of her situation and she had made the trade before fear could stop her.

She took the splinter out of her sleeve and held it flat on her knee where the firelight could get at it, and for the first time let Du Hong see it.

Pine, thumb-wide, an inch and a half long. Chalk on one face in a dead man’s careful hand: the tower’s name, a run of dates going back four winters, a column of marks for beasts listed and killed, and a smudge where a thumb had rested when the clerk wrote. In the corner, cut rather than chalked, a mend-date in the year and season.

“It is the whole tower,” Du Hong said. “It is just a board.”

“It is a board that a person made in the last four years, with a date on it, in the same hand all the way down.” She turned it over. “Everything else on this run is a story told by the person who came back. This is a thing that was written before any of us got here, by a man who did not get paid enough to lie about it.”

“So?”

“So when somebody tells me that the tower was empty that night, I can hold this up.” She put it back inside her sleeve, against the binding cloth, where it pressed against the scar and did not help it. “I cannot hold it up against anything if I traded it for four hands that will be in somebody else’s mouth by morning.”

Du Hong looked at her for a while.

“You are going to be in trouble,” he said.

“I am in trouble. I have been in trouble since I was eleven. I am simply going to be in trouble with paper.”

Near dawn Lu Fen came to the tower mouth with Xie Qiao's broken wrist behind him, ready to press the competing claim, and found Du Hong with the jaw-plate wrapped and witnessed by Shen Xiu's own mark from the evening count, and Ning Yan with nothing but a pine splinter and a scar. The lie died on his tongue. He could accuse a lame girl of theft. He could not accuse Du Hong, whole and witnessed, without calling Shen Xiu a fool.

He left. Du Hong exhaled shakily.

“You used me,” he whispered. “And you saved me.”

She nodded, eyes closing. The boy she had used was now something she had to answer to, and she had gone to sleep having made him, on purpose, in the cold.

She slept an hour before dawn with her head on her knees, and dreamed of breath counted aloud.
