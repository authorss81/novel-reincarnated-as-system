# The Petition

Nine hundred and eleven people were in the Assembly Hall by the third hour of the morning, and Ning Yan had been in the room since the first, because furnace crew are seated at the walls by rank and a furnace-crew servant-rank girl is seated at the wall.

It was a long low room with a plank floor and a roof forty feet up and four braziers in the middle that had gone out an hour ago because the accounts took four hours and nobody wanted the room warm. Nine hundred people made a noise like weather on a roof. There were windows on three walls and no dais, because the Hall did not sit above the room, it sat in it, on low seats, in the order it walked.

She had eaten nothing. She had been awake for six days and had not decided to be, she had simply not gone down. Her fever sat in her at about the height of a bad cold and came up when she moved.

She had a clean coat on and she had put the log in the lining and the three county sheets folded flat inside it and the tablet against her skin, and she had washed her face with snow at fourth hour, and not one face in that hall turned to her, and she could have been any furnace-crew girl at the wall.

That was the intention, and it lasted about a minute and a half, because the clerk came in at fourth hour with an armful of dockets and looked directly at her.

He was Xie Shuo. He had the fairest hand she had ever seen in an office, finer even than Feng Lushi's, because Feng wrote with a consumptive's economy and this man wrote the way a man does when he is young and believes a thing can be done well. He carried four dockets and put three of them on the Speaker's desk and kept the fourth in his own hand and did not look at her again for two hours.

## The Accounts

The accounts took until noon.

She had heard the winter reading in the outer yard in the middle of the season and it had been five minutes of names. It was not five minutes. It was four hours, and it was the entire winter, and every furnace and every kiln and every crew and every outer disciple in the third-rank sect of Qinghe went into it in order, and by the tenth hour a room of nine hundred people had been made to understand exactly what the year had cost.

Quota twenty-eight for Qinghe. Receipts: beasts, by weight, in tiers. Hands received: nine, of whom four were named in the yard and five were not, and the Reader read the five without slowing down, and there was no sound at all from nine hundred people when he did it.

The furnace column came at the eleventh hour. Account of Ning Bo, furnace-man, unsettled, balance carried forward six years. Credit held.

Her name again. It went out into a hall in the same flat voice it had gone into a yard in, and this time there were nine hundred people who had all just watched five unnamed hands be read out at a reading speed, and the number of people who looked round at the wall at exactly that moment was large enough that she felt it move across the room like weather.

She had got a bed and a stipend out of that sentence. She had got an indoor room. She had been inside the building that the sentence had put her in for six weeks.

The Speaker took the accounts to the hour and the Hall went back over four items of disagreement, and the objection on the fourth was from a man about lamp oil, and it took fifty minutes, and at the twelfth hour Elder Pei Shou said the accounts of the winter were taken.

Then he said that the session would proceed to the matters on the docket, and the clerk read the docket, and the fourth item on the docket was the petition of Ning Yan, and he read it, and it was the same name, in a hall, out loud, two hours after the ledger had said it in the ordinary way.

And Elder Pei Shou said, “That matter will be taken at the close of the session.”

“Close of the session,” said the clerk.

“Nobody will leave before I have the Hall’s leave.”

Ning Yan felt the whole room decide that it was finished.

She came off the wall.

She had thought about how to do this for nine days and every version had been a speech, and she had understood by about the fourth day that she had no speech, because she had never in her life been permitted to say a thing in that room and there was no amount of preparation that would make one permitted.

She did not say it loudly.

“Clerk,” she said, and eleven feet, and the braziers were out and the room had the acoustics of a stone barn and nine hundred people heard a girl say one word.

The clerk looked at her. Elder Pei Shou said, “Furnace crew, you are not on the docket for the floor.”

“The orders say that a member may lodge a petition in writing, entered and read where witnesses are present.”

“You have lodged it. It is entered. It will be read at the close of the session, which is *here*, and which is in this room, and which contains nine hundred witnesses.”

“The close of the session,” Ning Yan said, “is at the end of the day.”

Nobody moved.

She had learned this in a corridor with a clerk nine days ago, from the worst possible man she had ever met, and she said it now without quotation marks and without irony, because in a room of nine hundred people the only sentence that works is the one that cannot be improved.

“If it is read at the close of the session, then for the four hours before it is read the petition is a document in a room. If it is read now, it is a document in a record. I am asking you which of those two things you would like it to be for the rest of the day.”

Elder Pei Shou looked at her for a long time.

“Clerk,” he said. “Read the objection.”

## The Objection

There were two documents on the Speaker's desk and Xie Shuo had not mentioned the second one because the docket did not call for it.

“Entered on the seventh day,” he said, “by Elders Lu Kang, Deng Chao and Pei An, against the petition of Ning Yan, servant-rank of the furnace crew.”

Ning Yan felt her own name going out into the hall a second time inside thirty seconds and had to put her hand flat against the wall behind her.

The objection was good. She made herself read all of it standing up.

It was four paragraphs and it did not once insult her or accuse her of anything. It conceded stage nine. It conceded the seal. It set out, with total accuracy, that the seal had been issued on a finding that the Hall's own objection was the writ, and it set out, with total accuracy, that the hearing on the Assembly floor would require nine hundred witnesses to be diverted from the accounts of the winter.

And then it went to the root.

“Servant-rank is not a martial rank. The orders give advancement to those sealed. They do not give a seal to those found under a dispute. The dispute against this member arose from the furnace column and remains unreconciled. A member whose standing is unreconciled cannot be found entitled to a hearing on her own standing, and the finding of the ninth day, being itself an objection, cannot be used to cure the objection it is.”

“Read the last line again,” Ning Yan said.

“Clerk, read the last line again.”

“It is a difficult document,” said Xie Shuo, in the flattest voice she had ever heard from a living person, “and it is a difficult document in one particular place. The finding of the ninth day, being itself an objection, cannot be used to cure the objection it is. That is correct.”

“He says it is correct,” somebody said, from the middle of the floor.

“He does,” said the Speaker.

“Then the objection fails, and it fails in the room, and it fails by its own admission.” She had not raised her voice once in nine hundred people. “The seal stands. You can all go and look at it. It has the Hall's mark on it and it has three witnesses and the ninth day entered against it, and it is not a favour, because I refused the only man who offered me one, in front of eleven witnesses, nine days ago.”

“You refuse to be helped,” said Elder Lu Kang, without heat, from the third seat. “That is a private matter.”

“Yes,” said Ning Yan. “It is also the only part of my case that I cannot prove, so I have not used it.”

That got a sound out of the hall that she had not expected, and it was not a cheer, it was the noise nine hundred people make when they have been told something they were not expecting to be told.

“Clerk,” said Elder Pei Shou. “Dispose of it.”

Xie Shuo dipped the pen. He took nine seconds, which was nine seconds longer than it needed, because he was twenty-nine and he had just done the single most important thing that anybody in that building had done in his career and he wanted the whole of it in the ink.

“The objection is disposed of,” he said. “The seal stands. The petition is granted and the matter is set for the floor of this Hall. The seat of the trial is the Assembly floor. Enter it.”

Nine hundred people made a noise, and it went round the room twice, and underneath it there was a sound she did not expect, which was about forty people standing up.

## The Reading

“Ning Yan,” said the clerk, and she heard it.

It was not a shout and it was not a kindness and it was not the voice from the yard. It was a young man reading a docket in a room because a rule told him to, at the volume a rule requires, and it went out over nine hundred people and up into the roof beams and stood there.

That was the second time in the winter that anybody in Qinghe had said her name out loud in front of the sect. The first had been a Ledger Reader in the outer yard with the winter book in his arms and her father's six years hanging off her name like a chain, and she had been in the crowd and had been nothing in it.

She stood at the wall and let it go over her and did not move.

“Ning Yan, servant-rank of the Qinghe furnace crew, sealed for Condensing on the eighth day, petitions that her advancement be heard in public on the Assembly floor; and further petitions that the hearing be announced, in the layer's own voice, to every person inside the bound.”

Nine hundred people heard the second half of that and about two hundred of them did not know what it meant and the other seven hundred did, because everybody inside the bound knew what was said when somebody went through.

Elder Pei Shou stood.

“You may not petition the voice,” he said. “The voice is not of this Hall.”

“No,” said Ning Yan. “It is not.”

“Enter it as refused.”

“Enter it,” she said, “and enter why.”

The hall was completely quiet now.

“Because a thing that is announced is heard by nine counties, and each county writes it down,” Ning Yan said, “and what each county writes down is not the same.”

That landed in a room that had been hearing about harvests for four hours, and it landed as noise, and it landed because she said it in exactly the voice she used for reading tallies in a wash house, which was the only voice she had that nobody ever argued with.

“The eighth day past, the same hour, the same advancement, in four counties of nine. Hanhui wrote that it was done for discipline. Shouyi wrote that no name had been given at all. Wanzhou wrote that a servant had done it. Lowwater wrote what was said.”

She had the sheets inside her coat and she did not take them out, because a person who takes out paper in a room of nine hundred becomes a person arguing about paper.

“I have all four in a book in my coat in a hand that a Bureau clerk cannot read quickly. I am not showing it to you. I am telling you that it is in a book, with the date on it, and that a private advancement cannot be checked by anybody, ever, and that an announced one can be checked by four women in a market square who were not even in the room.”

“Nobody elected you—”

“I was not elected, sir. I was *assigned*.” She heard it leave her mouth and she did not stop it, because it had been in her since the Verge and it had never once been the wrong thing to say. “And you cannot refuse it, because refusing is a decision, and decisions are what get written down.”

Elder Pei Shou sat down. He looked at the floor for a moment, and then he said the thing that ended it, and he said it in the tone of a man who has found a doorway in his own wall.

“Enter it granted,” he said. “Enter that the hearing is set for the floor of this Hall on the fourth day, at the ninth hour. Enter that the hearing is a public advancement and that the layer is to speak as it speaks.”

“Enter the number,” said Ning Yan. “Enter nine hundred and eleven witnesses to the accounts, present now, and as many as come on the day.”

The clerk wrote it. He was not looking at his paper; he was looking at the room, and the room was looking at the wall, and then he wrote it down.

“Entered,” he said. “Fourth day. Ninth hour. The floor of this Hall. The voice to be let.”

She stepped back to the wall.

She stood at the wall for the rest of the session with four hundred pages of the winter's business read out over her head, and at no point in the four hours did she think about the sentence in the yard about her father's account and the nearest unspent thing. She had thought about it in the first minute.

She had gone looking for it on purpose, the way a person puts a hand into a coat to check for a knife they have been told is there, and it was still there, and it was still accurate, and there was nobody in that room who was going to rise up and say that the column was wrong.

Nine hundred people had heard her name. Nobody had admitted anything.

She got what she came for and it was not an admission and it was never going to be one, and she had known that on a stair in the cold nine days earlier, and she had asked for the hearing anyway, and the difference between asking for an admission and asking for a procedure was the whole of the next ten years.

Outside the hall the light was wrong. The sky over the terrace had gone the flat yellow-white it goes in the four minutes before a Cracking, and the wind had come around from the north in a single push that took the smoke out of the furnace chimney sideways and made every loose thing on the terrace jump, and somewhere out over the Verge something began to make a noise that was not weather and was not anybody working.

Nine hundred people came out of that hall into it and looked up.

The trial was in four days and the weather had opinions about it, and the weather on this occasion was nine hundred and eleven witnesses strong.
