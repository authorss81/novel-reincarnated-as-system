# Qiu's Fall

The doors of the Assembly Hall had to be propped on the fourth morning, both of them, with bars of timber because the hinges had packed with nine days of ash and would not lift a finger's width and had stopped pretending to.

She was inside at the first hour, standing by the wall with the furnace crew, and the hall smelled like a room in which several hundred people had been breathing the same air since before dawn.

The ash was at its heaviest. It had gone grey everywhere at once, the way it does when the air itself has given up on settling, and the light that came through the three high windows came through it as a flat pewter that put no shadow on the floor at all. The braziers in the middle of the hall had gone out at some point in the night and nobody had relit them, because relighting them would have taken four men off the doors, and the doors were what kept the room from being simply a grey box with nine hundred people in it.

Every surface in the building had a skin on it. The Speaker's table. The benches. The rail of the stair to the second gallery, where the inner disciples sat, and which had a line of grey along the top of it like frost on a rail in a mountain winter.

Qiu Zhulang was already in his seat.

She saw him from across the hall, at the fourth seat, in Hall coat, in the order they walked, exactly where he had been at every sitting she had been inside since the middle of the winter. He had a folded paper on his knee and his hands were on his knees and he was not reading it.

At the second hour she went out into the corridor and found him at the window with his hands behind him, looking at nothing, in the way of a man who is waiting for a room to fill up.

“Elder.”

He turned round and looked at her and for a second she had nothing, which had not happened in front of him before.

“You have four hours,” he said. “Go and stand somewhere with your back to a wall.”

“I came to ask you something and it is not about the trial.”

“Then you have about ninety seconds of it.”

She had prepared eleven questions over four days and had thrown all eleven away somewhere around the seventh step, because he had said the same thing to her in a corridor nine days ago about a document and he had been right, and because there was exactly one question left and it was a bad one.

“What will you do afterwards.”

Qiu Zhulang looked at the grey window.

“When they take it off me this morning,” he said, “I will go back to the furnace as a man and I will do the work, because the work still has to be done and there is nobody else in that building who knows how to do it. In about four years I will be too old. My sister has a daughter in Hanhui who does not speak to me and I have not seen her in nine years.”

He turned his head and looked at her.

“When I was twenty-two there was a woman in Lowwater who kept a lamp shop and a boy who was not mine and not hers, and I very nearly stayed, and she told me once that it was the shape of a happy life and I thought she was being romantic about a lamp shop.” He said it in the voice he used for furnace tools, and he did not smile. “I have been at the furnace for forty-one years trying to buy it out of other people's children. I did not get it and I am not going to get it and I would not have thanked you for it.”

“Elder—”

“Ninety seconds,” said Qiu Zhulang, and went back to the window.

## The Entry

The accounts went to the fifth hour and they took a hall of full lungs two hours longer than the clerk had reckoned, and twice somebody at the back had to go out into the corridor and stand there, and came back.

At the fifth hour Elder Pei Shou took the winter and said it was taken, and went down the list of matters entered for the session, and got to the fourth.

Clerk Xie Shuo had a fair hand and he read it out without hurrying, and every word of it went into a hall that could not see the far wall.

“Ning Yan, servant-rank, petitioned at the eleventh hour of the eleventh day past for admission to the furnace crew of this sect, under the writ of the Elder of the Furnace. The motion was entered at the eleventh hour. The entry of the member was made at the eleventh hour and eleven minutes. The ruling of the Speaker was given at the eleventh hour and eleven minutes.”

He stopped, and she saw his hand go flat on the table.

“A matter is entered under the standing orders,” said Elder Pei Shou, “in one of three ways. At the hour of the motion, before any hearing. At the close of the hearing, after the hearing is closed by the Speaker. Or, in no other hour at all.”

Nobody in the hall said anything.

“The standing orders do not permit an entry made between the making of a motion and the hearing of it. An entry so made is retrospective, and a retrospective entry is void, and the thing the entry carries is void with it.”

Elder Pei Shou looked down the line of the nine seats.

“I will ask it once,” he said. “Elder Qiu, do you plead the entry of the eleventh hour and eleven minutes.”

The hall did not move. Somewhere at the back a man coughed twice and stopped.

Qiu Zhulang stood up.

He was sixty-one and he stood up the way he did everything, which was as though he had a great deal of time and had decided not to use any of it. He did not look round the room. He looked at the Speaker's table.

“No, sir.”

“Elder Qiu, if you plead the entry and it is found good, the admission stands and the question ends. If it is found void, the admission of Ning Yan is void, and she returns to the outer yard, and the seal of the eighth day past is discharged, and she is a member who has been found to have attempted a public advancement on the strength of a rank she did not hold.”

“I am aware of it, sir.”

“I am required to put it to you twice.”

“No, sir,” said Qiu Zhulang, and sat down.

He did not say one word after that for the rest of the morning. He did not explain. He did not look at the three men who had lodged the objection on the seventh day, and he did not look at the floor, and he did not look at Ning Yan, and she understood exactly what he had done and exactly what it had cost and she was not able to say a single word about it in a hall of nine hundred people, which she suspected was the design.

[The warrant of the Elder of the Furnace is vacated at the fifth hour of the fourth day. The warrant of elder is retained at one below. The furnace does not pass to a person.]

## After

It took four minutes to vacate an office that had been held for forty-one years, because the standing orders were very good on almost everything.

The Speaker read the vacancy. Xie Shuo entered it. There was one question left in the room, from Elder Lu Kang, and it was a good question and it was asked in the ordinary voice.

“Does the standing of elder without portfolio carry a stipend.”

“It carries a pension,” said the Speaker, “at the rate of the third estate. It carries a seat. It carries the right to be heard on any matter of the furnace, and no vote in it, and no office in it.”

“Gentlemen,” said Lu Kang, and stopped, because there was nothing else in the sentence.

The room began to move, and the benches scraped, and the ash on the floor went into the air and came down again on everybody at once, and two hundred people stood up at the same time in a hall that could not hold them all standing.

Qiu Zhulang came down off the fourth seat and came along the wall.

He was not going to be able to get out through the doors. She understood that from twenty feet away: the hall was full, the doors were propped with timber, and there was no way through the middle of it. So he came along the wall the long way, past the furnace crew, past the outer disciples who got out of his way faster than they got out of anyone's way, past the stair, and he was going to pass within three feet of her.

She had four seconds.

She had a thing she could have said. She had had eleven things she could have said for four days and thrown all of them away, and what was left was not a speech. What was left was that he had given up his office for eleven minutes on a technicality so that her rank would survive, and that she knew it, and that he did not.

She let him go past.

He slowed for half a step beside her. Not enough to stop. Enough that a person standing close by would have seen it and would not have been able to say what it was.

“I have never once been asked what I am allowed to think,” he said.

Then he was past, and she did not understand it.

She stood there in the ash with the sentence going round inside her like a stone in a bucket and tried it three times against four months of the winter and got nothing at all out of it. He had lost everything he had held for forty-one years. He had let it go rather than raise a procedural point that would have put a seventeen-year-old girl back on the outer yard list with a discharged seal. And the sentence he had said to her, walking past, with three feet of air between them, was about being asked.

She turned it over for the rest of the morning and it stayed exactly as opaque as it had been, and she noticed that the man in her head had gone quiet somewhere in the middle of it, which was something he did perhaps twice a winter.

## What She Felt

She got what she had come for.

That was the thing that went wrong with the whole morning, or the only thing that went right with it, and she spent two hours after the accounts trying to work out which.

She had spent two months asking to be looked at on purpose and she had been looked at on purpose, and the looking had cost everybody else in the room nothing, and in exchange she had got: a roof with a nail in it, a hall full of grey air, a man of sixty-one walking along a wall with his office gone and a sentence in her ears she could not parse, and a clerk with a fair hand who had read a thing out loud in nine hundred people's ears and had not looked at her once while he did it.

Nothing anybody in that hall had done had been about her.

“You have been counting the room since the fifth hour,” he said, at about the tenth, from somewhere behind her eyes, “and you have not once looked at what it is doing to you.”

“I know what it is doing to me.”

“Then say it, because you do not, and I have been waiting two months for you to be the kind of person who does.”

She had been fourteen when her father was taken. She had not cried about it, then or afterwards, because crying had not been one of the available procedures. She had gone and found her brother and taken his things and done the whole of a removal for two people by herself, and she had understood at the time that she was being brave about it, and she had been wrong about that too — she had been working, which is a different thing, and which does not ask anything of you.

Now she was standing in a hall in front of nine hundred people and a man had just lost forty-one years for eleven minutes on her account, and she had let him walk past her without opening her mouth.

That was all.

She understood, in about the time it takes to count four breaths, that this was exactly what it felt like to be him.

Not the loss. The *cannot say anything about it*. Being on the inside of a thing that is happening to a person, and knowing the whole of it, and having no way to put a hand on it, and not being allowed to even change the subject. He had been doing this since the first night in the staging ground and she had thought, most days, that she was the one carrying it.

She found that she was not able to hold it and the fever and the cough at the same time, and she sat down on the bench at the wall, which was the only thing she had to sit on, and put her right hand flat on her knee.

## Her Name

The Speaker took the matters at the ninth hour and the hall was so full that forty people had given up on benches and were standing along the walls.

“Clerk.”

Xie Shuo stood up. He had not stopped writing since the fifth hour. He had the whole morning on one page and the page was already half gone at the edges with his hand resting on it, and she looked at that hand and thought about the fact that he had asked to be one of nine clerks on this floor, and then she stopped thinking about it because she could see his face.

“Clerk Xie,” said the Speaker.

“Furnace crew,” said Xie Shuo.

He looked out over the room. He had a page in front of him and nine hundred people in front of him and the ash coming down through three high windows into a light that put no shadow at all.

“Ning Yan, servant-rank of the Qinghe furnace crew, sealed for Condensing on the eighth day past, is called to the floor for the hearing entered at the fourth hour of the fourth day, and the hearing is a public advancement, and the voice is to be let.”

He put the pen down.

It was the last time she ever heard him say her name and she had not known that at the time.
