# Terms

The summons came on a slip of paper the width of a finger, in a hand that had been taught to write small, and it said *Assembly Hall, west corridor, third hour* and nothing else, and no seal, and that was how she knew it had not come from the Hall.

She was in the corridor at two.

It was a wide stone passage with a bench down one side and the great doors at the end, and the ash had got into it through two days of doors being propped and lay across the flags in a grey film that had been walked into the middle and was going grey again at the edges. A brazier at the corner was burning something that was not lamp oil and made a smell like a wet dog being dried.

The clerks of the Hall's own table worked along the wall with cloths over their mouths and their eyes streaming, and they were doing it anyway, because nine hundred accounts go up with the season and there is no other table.

Three men were sitting on the bench. Two of them she knew by name from a document lodged on the seventh day past: Lu Kang in the middle, Deng Chao on his left with his hat in his lap, Pei An on the right. Between them and the bench was a low table with a sheet of paper on it, a small block of red paste, and a man in servant-rank with his cuffs turned back and ink to the second joint of his middle finger.

She stopped eleven feet away, because eleven feet was the distance she had used on the Assembly floor and it had worked.

“Furnace crew,” said Lu Kang, who was sixty-eight and had not stood up. “Sit down. There is no one at this end of the corridor.”

She sat down.

The servant-rank man put his thumb in the paste and turned it, and that was the sound of the room.

“This is not the Hall speaking,” Lu Kang said. “The Hall has nine chairs in it and six of them are yours. This is three men with a schedule, and a schedule has to be kept, and that is the whole of the difference between us and that building.”

“Understood.”

“You are not to agree with me yet.”

“I was not going to.”

“You are not to agree with me until you have read all of it,” Lu Kang said, “because a man who reads three clauses and says yes has been in this building a long time and has never once been in a wash house.”

## What They Offered

He had the clerk read it.

It was four sheets, fair and even, with no dust on them anywhere, which meant they had lain flat in a drawer and had not spent an hour in a corridor. She had what she needed by the third line and let the rest of it run over her, listening to the shape instead.

The two previous offers had both been built the same way and she had refused one and understood the other. This one was built differently, and she could not immediately say how, and that frightened her more than anything else in the corridor.

“Inner rank at first sitting, and not at review, and not at the discretion of any later sitting,” the clerk read. “The bed and the stipend from the day of the hearing, which is in four days and has not been heard. Foundation within two years at the cost of the maintaining members, on their own writ, and not subject to the ordinary schedule of the furnace, which stands at eleven years.”

“Foundation outside the ordinary schedule,” Ning Yan said.

“Outside it.”

“You cannot do that.”

Lu Kang put one hand flat on his knee. “The ordinary schedule is for men the sect expects to lose. It is generous to them and it takes nine years, and in nine years a third-rank sect changes its furniture four times. The writ is ours. The schedule is the sect's. On the schedule you will be finished inside four years and nobody will feel it.”

She looked at the third sheet, which was the account.

The account of Ning Bo, furnace-man, unsettled, balance carried forward six years, struck whole on the day of signature. And beside it, on the same line, in the same hand, eight further accounts, struck on the same afternoon, going back eleven years.

Nine households.

Nine names, and she knew two of them, and both were the kind of name that is on nobody's list because nobody has a list. The third sheet was a furnace column with nine lines struck through in one stroke by a hand that was not shaking, because it had done this before.

“Why these nine,” she said.

“Because they are quiet,” said Deng Chao, who had not spoken until then, in a low voice with a cough in it and his hat still in his lap.

“They are quiet because they have been paid,” Lu Kang said. “Nine households over eleven years. Every one of them signed a paper in a room with two of us in it, and not one has ever made a sound inside this sect. Not one, in eleven years. That is not cruelty, that is *efficiency*, and I will not have it called anything else in a corridor.”

“You have been running your own furnace,” Ning Yan said.

“For eleven years,” said Pei An, tipping his head very slightly toward the doors at the end of the passage without saying a name, because in that building you did not say a name to make that kind of point.

“He has never once asked to see this schedule,” Lu Kang said. “He runs the furnace for the sect and we run it for ourselves out of the same rooms, and the reason we can is that he is a decent man who does not look at the second book. If he looks at it he stops being usable and then we have no arrangement at all. When he is gone, somebody else looks at it.”

[Eight accounts are struck from the furnace column of Qinghe in the entry made today. The region does not strike them together.]

She kept her hands still.

Nine families over eleven years, and not one of them had ever made a sound, because every one of them had been handed a piece of paper in a room by two men and told the paper was the end of it. The whole of Qinghe Sect was built on the assumption that when that happens to a servant family, the family is grateful.

Nobody had ever been grateful. They had simply had nobody to be angry at who could write.

## The Last Two Clauses

“The tablet,” the clerk read. “The article of kiln clay taken from sealed ground at the Broken Kiln, to be surrendered to the maintaining members within the week, entered as a kiln article recovered and returned to the seal.”

“Not taken,” Ning Yan said. “Recovered.”

“Recovered,” the clerk agreed, without any expression whatever, and went on, because he was reading a document somebody else had written and had not been asked to care about the tense.

“And last. The member undertakes that she will not speak, report, describe or enter, in any record of this Hall or of the Bureau of Records or in any testimony before any office, any voice, sound or presence heard within her at any time since the ninth day past.”

The corridor was extremely quiet.

The clerk read it out twice, because the clause had a second limb, and the second limb was not a fine.

“Say the penalty again.”

“If the member is found to have so spoken, the undertaking is void and the sponsorship is void, and the account of Ning Bo is reinstated in full with the six years carried again, and the member is recorded as having entered sealed ground and removed kiln clay without the seal of any office.”

He put the pen down.

“So it does not fine you,” Lu Kang said. “It makes everything we just did not happen, and it puts you back in the furnace column, and it makes the paper you read in a corridor on the seventh day true.”

They had thought about that. Somebody in that building had sat down and worked out that the most useful clause was not a gag but a *warrant*, and had written a document whose real purpose was to be able to unfire a firing if she ever opened her mouth.

## The Word

“I want one clause added,” Ning Yan said.

Lu Kang's eyebrows went up half a centimetre and stayed there.

“Name it.”

“Ning Wu. Assessed at the Broken Kiln on the seventeenth day of the autumn two years past, reassigned above furnace authority, not reconciled.”

She heard it go out into the corridor in her own voice and it was the plainest sentence she had said in a month.

She had it ready. She had had it ready for nine days, on a stair and in a cart and on a terrace step in the dark, and it had been assembled so many times in her head that it came out without any weight at all, like a tally read out of a wash book, and she watched the three of them receive it and she watched what happened to the room.

Nothing happened.

“Deng Chao,” Lu Kang said.

“Enter it,” said Deng Chao, immediately.

He did not write it down. He said it to the servant-rank clerk and the servant-rank clerk wrote it down, and it took about four seconds, and in those four seconds Ning Yan understood the entire shape of what had just happened to her.

They had not hesitated. Not one of them had needed to find out whether they could do it. Which meant it had never cost them anything, which meant that in eleven years nobody had ever come into that corridor and asked for a name. They had a machine that struck accounts and they did not have a part that produced words, because nobody had ever needed one.

They would have said yes to anything. She understood, sitting on that bench with her hands on her knees, that they would have said yes to *reconciled*, and to *found*, and to *struck twice*, and there was not one thing she could have asked for that they would have had to think about.

“No,” she said. “Wait.”

They waited.

“Write it exactly as I said it. Not as *reconciled*. As the three words it is now.” She heard her own voice do something she had not authorised. “The record is *assessed, reassigned, unreconciled*. I am asking you to enter it in the register as *reconciled*, and I want the entry to say what the entry does and when it was made, and that it was asked for and not offered.”

The clerk looked up at Lu Kang.

Lu Kang looked at the third sheet with the nine accounts struck through it in one stroke, and then at the girl on the bench in a grey corridor.

“Enter it,” he said.

“Will that take long?”

“As long as it takes,” Lu Kang said. “And it is the cheapest thing anybody in this sect has ever given away, and I would like you to understand that I know that, and that I am giving it to you anyway, and that I am not asking for the tablet in exchange, because the tablet is not worth a stroke of a brush to me.”

## Thirty Seconds

The servant-rank man turned his block of red paste toward himself, out of habit, and Ning Yan watched him do it.

It was the most beautiful thing she had seen in a month.

She thought: where do I sign.

She thought it in the voice she used for reading tallies, and she heard the shape of it go out into the corridor the way a stone goes out of a hand, and the corridor took it, and nobody moved.

Six years of her father came off a page, and the accounts of eight other households came off the same page, and her brother came back from a kiln into a register with one word on him, and her mother's wash house and the flat grey lye trough and eleven years of a stone on a step and two scars and a cracked gate and a hole in her ribs that will not close.

All of it. For a piece of paper in a corridor with a man turning a block of paste in his fingers.

Her right hand came up off her knee about two inches.

Then she thought: I would be buying it.

It went through her from the front to the back like cold water.

If she paid for that word then the word was hers. She would carry it for the rest of her life as a thing she had bought at a table from three men who had never once in eleven years been asked to produce a word. And every night for as long as she lived she would have to decide whether she had bought a truth or a piece of paper, and she knew before she got to the end of that sentence that she did not know which one it was, and that a person who does not know which one it is cannot go on using it.

She put her hand back on her knee.

“No,” she said.

The three of them did not move.

“I am not refusing it,” Ning Yan said, and she heard how careful the voice was, and how it took her a moment to understand that she had decided something in the last four seconds and had not known she was deciding it. “I am not signing today, and I am not refusing, and I want the sheet to stay on that table with the hour blank and the clause you have just written still in it. And I am saying that out loud so that the three of you cannot pretend later that I was unclear.”

Lu Kang looked at the doors at the end of the corridor for a while.

“That is an unusual thing to say to three men who have just offered you your life.”

“Yes.”

“Why.”

“Because if I take it now, then in four days when I stand on that floor, everything anybody in that Hall has seen me do for two months was bought,” Ning Yan said, “and I will not be able to tell the difference myself, and neither will you, and the only people in nine counties who would have believed one word of it are the nine clerks with pens.”

The brazier at the corner made its wet-dog smell and went on burning the wrong thing.

“Three days,” said Lu Kang.

“Three days.”

“The clause stays open for three days and the sheet stays on this table and the hour stays blank. On the fourth day, fourth hour, if it is not signed, it is dead, and the account of Ning Bo stands, and your brother stands unreconciled, and you go to that floor with nothing. Is that the shape of it.”

“That is the shape of it.”

The servant-rank man did not move his hand away from the paste.

“Get up.”

She got up.

“Furnace crew.” Lu Kang had not stood either and he did not look up at her. “I have been in this building forty-one years and in all that time I have never had a person come to me and refuse a paper after they had read all of it. It has never been worth doing. You have cost me nine minutes and you have bought a clause with nothing, which is a rate I do not have.”

“That is the first offer anybody has made me this year that I did not have to check,” Ning Yan said.

“Go and cough somewhere else.”

She went out into the corridor and up the nine steps in the ash, and she got about eight steps up before she had to stop and take a cough, and she stood on the fourth step with her hand on the cold rail and understood that she had not spent an hour in that corridor.

She had spent thirty seconds of it on the bench with her hand two inches off her knee, and everything else had been nine minutes of a man in servant-rank's cuffs turning a block of paste toward himself out of forty years of habit.

*In three days the offer dies or she does not.*

If she signs it in those three days, then in four days nine hundred people will watch a girl go through a wall, and every one of them will do the arithmetic that anybody does the arithmetic, and the arithmetic is: somebody paid for that.

And if she does not sign it in those three days, then the word *reconciled* does not exist, and her brother's record stays three words long for the rest of her life.

She had bought the only thing she had wanted with the only thing she had left, which was an afternoon.
