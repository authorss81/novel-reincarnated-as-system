# The Book of Accounts

The training yard at first light was a place for losing.

Ning Yan knew it before she stepped onto the packed snow with a borrowed practice blade in her hand and Du Hong waiting opposite with his own, patient as a wall. She had not trained properly since the Verge. Stage six carried her through chores and stairs, but the body remembered stage nine the way a house remembered a fire, in the height of the ceilings, and the gap between what she had been for one night and what she was now was exactly the gap Yuan bought her life with.

She wanted it back. The sect held its winter drill in nine days, and outer disciples who showed stage seven or better were given extra oil and a second weighing, and outer disciples who showed stage six with a limp were given advice.

Du Hong saluted with the blade. She saluted back. They began.

He beat her in eleven exchanges.

It was not close. He was not cruel about it. He came in low, turned her borrowed blade aside with the flat, tapped her ribs, stepped out before she could answer. Her left leg forgot itself at the same place every time, where step two should have become step three, and habit answered only when it chose. She went to her knees in the snow on the fourth pass and got up with her face hot.

“Again,” she said.

They went again. He beat her again.

By the sixth pass her scar burned and her breath came shallow, twenty to the minute, and the aperture in her chest ached at its chipped rim. She hated him for his steadiness. She hated herself more for hating him.

On the seventh pass something happened that stopped them both.

He came in high, she slipped outside without thinking, hip dropping, knee bending, heat flashing through the calf, and her body turned inside his reach and put her elbow where his nose would have been if she had finished it. She stopped a finger from his face, breathing hard, staring at her own feet.

The turn. Step three, whole and clean, performed by a girl who could not remember learning it.

Du Hong stepped back slowly. “You did it,” he said. “You did the turn you could not do yesterday.”

“I did not do it.” Her voice shook. “My legs did it. My head was somewhere else.”

She tried again, deliberately, and fell. The snow burned her palms. Habit was not technique. It answered only when it chose, like a dog that had been beaten and still loved the hand.

She sat in the snow and understood what the body kept and what the head lost, and that the head could be made to keep things too if somebody worked at it. Practice stored twice. One store burned out in a night under a tower. The other stayed in the thighs, waiting.

Yuan spoke, low, careful with her in front of another person.

“You move well. I watch. I am proud.”

She kept her face still. Pride from a thing with no hands, in the present tense, while she sat in snow with a borrowed blade. She wanted to laugh and to hit something.

She got up. She dusted snow from her knees. She looked at Du Hong, who was trying not to look worried and failing, and she decided to ask the question she had carried since the tower stones.

She asked it plainly, because plain was the only way she knew to make him answer.

“Did you take something else while you were in there?”

Silence in her head. Real silence, not absence. She felt him count, felt him weigh, felt the Settlement move the way a rope moves when somebody leans on it.

“I take what the layer allows. I take only what you agree. I hold what I take.”

It was true and incomplete. She knew it was incomplete the way she knew a tally slip was short, by weight, not by reading. He had spent five stages without consent to stop a knife. He had said the price was half a stage and then taken ten times it. He had never told her what the spending took from underneath the stages, from the foundation itself, from the order and not the substance.

And she let him. She let the omission stand, on purpose, in the yard, with snow in her shoes, because she needed him to hold the door for nine more days and because she was afraid of what the whole answer would do to the work.

Both of them knew she let him. That was worse than the theft.

Du Hong broke the quiet. “You went somewhere. You do that now. You go somewhere and count.”

“I count breaths,” she said, which was true and incomplete, and she hated herself for learning his trick so fast. “Again.”

They went again. This time she lasted longer, because anger steadied her legs better than hope. When the exchange ended she stood panting with her blade low and felt the deep shift come unasked, a small unpleasant crack loud enough to hear through her own ribs, heat racing down her arms.

Body-Forging stage seven.

Built from losing badly for an hour and from carrying water and from walking lame for days. No quest. No price stated. Just work, the way stage six came from carrying Pei Lan.

Du Hong whooped despite himself. “Seven. You went to seven in the yard. Shen Xiu will see it at drill.”

She nodded, dizzy, hand pressed to her sternum where the rim ached. She did not know the rim was cracked. She knew only that something at the centre of her chest caught when qi moved fast, like a cup with a chipped lip.

She wanted a sponsor to be allowed to train past stage nine at all. That was the next wall, beyond the drill, beyond seven. Servants were not given the map. She filed that want alongside the furnace books, two tally slips in the same sleeve.

Yuan spoke once more, softer.

“I am here. I do not go.”

She walked off the yard with Du Hong’s praise ringing in her ears and the first deliberate omission between them sitting in her chest like a stone. It was worse than the theft. Theft could be forgiven. Letting a lie stand, on purpose, because you needed the liar, could not be undone.

She did not go back.
