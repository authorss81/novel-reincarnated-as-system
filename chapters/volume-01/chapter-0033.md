# Du Hong

The outer yard in evening held blue snow and kitchen smoke.

Ning Yan met Du Hong by the well-house where they had talked before, because he sent word through the crew boy and she owed him a meeting. Her goal was plain. She meant to tell him not to get involved and mean it.

Resistance was what he had become. He wore an inner disciple's coat now, by her recommendation, filed and sealed after the Hall's winter session. Eighteen, broad, bad at lying, finding out what the coat cost. He looked older by months in weeks.

“You look fed,” she said. Past tense, trying for lightness. “The inner kitchen agreed with you.”

“It fed me and then it told me the price.” He leaned on the well-house, not meeting her eyes at first. “They sent me to report you in three days. Said a servant-rank with unreconciled paper would be worth noticing. Said my noticing would be remembered.”

Her hands went cold inside her mittens. Another son offered another clerkship. The Hall paid in futures. Snow settled on his shoulders and did not melt. Behind him the kitchen chimney smoked, and the smell of evening grain came across the yard, ordinary and unbearable.

“You refused,” she said. It was not a question, but it needed answering.

“I have not done it.” He looked at her then, angry, frightened. “They told me already what I would be given for it. A weighing place. A sponsor's nod. You should understand you ruined a perfectly good career for me by getting me made inner. I was nobody and it was comfortable.”

She felt terror of a kind the Hunter would not give her for another two volumes. Not fear of being reported. Fear of being followed. He had decided to follow her, badly, generously, without skill, and she had to be a person to someone, which meant being answerable.

“I never asked you to follow,” she said. Past tense, low. “You filed for me. You carried for me. You owed nothing after.”

“I know.” He kicked snow. “I am still here. Do not tell me to go back to being nobody. I will not do it, and you asking would take it from me.”

She did not ask him not to. Because she knew asking would take it from him, and because she was selfish enough to want one person in the sect who knew her name without wanting her stopped.

Yuan spoke, present, gentle.

“I see him. I honour. I fear for him.”

She wanted to warn Du Hong about Volume 2, about dying because of her, but she had no words for a future, only for winter. She told him the practical things instead. Where she slept. When the crew went below. What Shen Xiu promised and what the Hall paid.

“Walked careful,” she said. “You reported nothing. You kept your coat. If they pressed again, you said you saw nothing unreconciled.”

He nodded, jaw tight. “And you?”

“I go below in three days with a lamp.” She met his eyes. “If I do not come up, my pallet hides paper. You take it to Lowwater, to Feng Lushi, and you do not read it on the road.”

He stared. “Swore you would not make me carry.”

“I swore many things.” She touched his sleeve once, quickly. “You are the only one I trusted with this.”

He swallowed, nodded, eighteen and terrified and honoured, which was the most dangerous combination in the world. She left him at the well-house with kitchen smoke in his coat and understood this conversation was where his dying started, two volumes off, caused by her, unforgiven.

She did not ask him to stop following. That was the consequence, and she carried it up to the crew door with snow in her shoes.

Behind her, Shen Xiu's promise still stood. Three days. Somebody would do the reporting, early or late. She walked inside to be ready.
