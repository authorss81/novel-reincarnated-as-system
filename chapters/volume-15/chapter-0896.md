# Line for Line

“Two hundred and fourteen days since the ninth stage. Six hundred and forty-five days in that room. The cage out four hundred and twelve. This arm open six hundred and fifty-two days. One thousand three hundred and eighty-seven gone at this morning.”

She said the figures standing at the near end of the table in the making-place, on the twenty-third of the month beginning two thousand nine hundred and twenty-four, with the lamp an eyelash in front of her and the register under her hands.

The bar stood six hundred and two days against its wall, dated nowhere.

## The Rest of It

“Today I read the rest,” she said out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice. “One clause in my voice and the rest of it waiting for nine days. The waiting is done. Line for line, standing, out loud, before it is a line anywhere. That is the fourth kept.”

She stood at the near end of the table with both hands on its edge and breathed four counts standing still, the way she breathed for lines. Her right arm hung open at the elbow for six hundred and fifty-two days. The lamp stood an eyelash on the register's edge, two paces of light on cuts she had felt but never read.

She set the lamp at the edge of the register, the way she set things she was keeping, and two paces of light lay along the cuts, and past the light the lines went on further than she could see.

“The register I have never seen,” she said, “and I cannot see to the end of it. So the reading stands. I read what my light reaches and then I move the lamp. That is the method and I have no other.”

## Him, on Standing

“I want you standing and breathing,” he said.

She stood at the near end of the table with both hands on its edge and heard him the way she heard everything now, undefended, and there was no room in her head that was his alone.

“I am standing,” she said. “Breathing is four counts and I have done it for nineteen years. What is the want behind the want.”

“The lines run past your light,” he said. “You stand here until your arm shakes and your rib pulls and the eyelash goes out. I want you standing through all of it and breathing through all of it and stopping when the body says stop.”

“I stop when the reading stops,” she said. “That is the arrangement. Bodies wait on readings on this stair.”

“Bodies do not wait,” he said. “I am asking for the body first for once. Stand. Breathe. Read one line. Then tell me you are still standing.”

“I stand and I breathe and I read,” she said. “That is the order and it is the same order as everything: empty first, then take. The body empties into standing and the line goes into the empty. I have done it that way since the first stage I ever took.”

“Then do it that way now,” he said. “One line. Tell me you stand.”

## The Second Clause

She put her left hand with three fingers and a thumb on the second line of the register and felt the cuts under her fingers, and she bent into the two paces of light, and she read it standing, in the ordinary voice.

Her breath went four counts standing still. The lamp flame bent with her bending and stood back up. His half went on inside her where she could hear it, counting the standing the way he counted everything she would not.

“Whoever is heard is answered in the voice that hears him.”

She stood at the table with her hand on the line and said it in her own voice, before it was a line anywhere else.

“That is the second clause,” she said. “Heard and answered, and the answering in the hearing voice. Four hundred years and the keeping answers. That is a mercy and it is the second one.”

She stood with her hand on the line and thought about nine years of hearing, a voice at ten that answered what she asked and held what she would not, and the answering had always come in his voice, because his was the only voice she had.

“Heard in his,” she said out loud, standing. “Answered in his. That is how it was with me for nine years before I knew there was a keeping. The clause says it plain and I am saying so after it.”

“I am still standing,” she said, because she had promised him the telling.

“You are still standing,” he said. “I am counting the standing the way you count landings. Read one more.”

She stood at the table with the lamp two paces on the cuts and her hands on the register, and the standing went on the way sittings go on, breath by breath, four counts and four again. The arm shook once and she breathed nine standing still until it stopped. The flame bent with her breath and stood back up.

## The Third and Fourth

She moved the lamp a hand's breadth along the register, and the light went with it, and she put her hand on the third line.

“No name taken into the keeping is sent anywhere.”

She read it standing, in her own voice, and the place behind her fourth rib pulled.

“Third,” she said. “No sending. Lu An carries oil up nine hundred and four steps tomorrow the way he carries it today, and no line moves him. That is a mercy and it is the third one.”

She stood with her hand on the third line and felt the keeping in it the way she felt stone under sitting, which was as a thing that held without asking. No sending had been the whole of the binding she said, no one sent and no one entered, and here it stood written before she said it, a mercy waiting for a mouth.

She moved the lamp again, and her arm shook at intervals she could not predict, and she lay her right arm along the table edge until it stopped, the way she lay still for the shaking.

“What is bound is kept where it stands.”

“Fourth,” she said, standing. “Kept where it stands. The first of the Sisterhood stays eight flights below with black hands and no idea of any record. That is a mercy and it is the fourth one.”

Her arm shook at the fourth line, hard, the way it shook when she stood too long, and she put her right hand flat on the table edge and breathed nine standing still until it stopped, and the lamp flame bent with her breath and stood back up an eyelash.

“Standing,” she said, with her hand flat. “Still standing. The body waits on the reading and the reading is nearly done.”

“I am still standing,” she said.

“You are still standing,” he said. “One more line past your light. Move the lamp.”

## Moving the Lamp

She moved the lamp the last hand's breadth with both hands, the way she had moved it for every line, and the flame went with the moving, bending flat and standing back up, lower every time, wick as much as oil.

“Eyelash,” she said out loud, standing. “Two paces and going. This is the last moving it has in it and I am spending it on the last line.”

She bent into the light with her left hand on the cuts, three fingers and a thumb finding the starts, and the lines ran under her fingers the way the stair ran under her feet, one after another, made by hands that never wore anything.

She moved the lamp the last hand's breadth, and the flame sank with the moving, the way it sank with everything now, and stood back up an eyelash above the wick, and the light reached along the last line she could find.

“The offer stands open, and no answering is asked for it.”

She read it standing, in the ordinary voice, in the making-place, first in her own voice before it was a line anywhere.

“Fifth,” she said. “Open and unasked. Nothing in it asks for an answer. That is a mercy and it is the fifth one, and it is the last one my light reaches.”

She stood with both hands on the register and the five clauses in her mouth the way figures sat in her mouth, keeping and answering and no sending and kept standing and open unasked, and not one of them a price, and not one of them a condition she could find, and the finding of conditions belonged to the second reading and not to this one.

She lifted her hands off the register and put them flat on the table edge, and her breath went nine counts standing still, and then four, and then four again.

“The whole of it read once,” she said out loud, standing, with the lamp an eyelash in front of her and the lines gone past her light in both directions. “Five clauses. Every one of them a keeping or an answering or a standing open. Not one of them a price. Not one of them a sending. I have read it line for line in my own voice and there is nothing in my mouth now that is not in the keeping.”

She sat down at the near end of the table, because standing was for saying and the saying was done for today, and the stone took her sitting the way stone takes everything.

Her breath went four counts sitting still. Her hands lay flat on the floor with three fingers and a thumb on each, empty the way the vessel had been empty, except that the vessel was shut now and her hands were only resting.

“Tomorrow I read it again,” she said, sitting. “A reading read once is a finding. Read twice it is something else.”

Five clauses in her voice, and the second reading waiting on tomorrow.
