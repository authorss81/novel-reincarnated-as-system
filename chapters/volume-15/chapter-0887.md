# The Landing Count

The stair above the fourteenth landing went up into dark that had no bottom to it and no top she had seen, and she stood at the foot of it with her lamp in her hand and did not go up.

That was the whole of the morning for a count of a hundred. Standing and not going up, with the light going four paces out and stopping, and the sentences going on above her one at a time.

## What She Does Instead

She had counted everything for nineteen years. Boards under a middle. Paces off a near end. Days gone at a morning. Breaths on a rise. She had counted a stair she had walked four times and she had counted eight hundred and eighty steps without counting them once, and both of those were true, because the second one had been merit and not arithmetic.

On this stair she counted the landings and not the steps, and she had said why on the first day she went up, standing, in the ordinary voice, at the first hour, and she said it again now, because a reason said once on a stair was a thing said to stone, and stone kept nothing.

“A landing is a thing I can be wrong about and a stair is not,” she said out loud, with her back against the wall of the fourteenth. “I am going up it for the last time and I do not want to arrive holding a number.”

The flame bent and righted itself.

“That is the reason and it has not changed in a month. Landings.”

## Him, on Counting

“You count when you are afraid,” he said.

She stood on the fourteenth landing with the lamp in her left hand and looked down past the light into the dark below, where the thirteenth lay, and the twelfth under it, and the eleventh under that, all the way down nine flights to a room she had left on the twenty-second of the month beginning two thousand eight hundred and sixty-four.

“I am afraid,” she said. “That is true and it is not news. Fear is the cheapest thing on this stair and I have been spending it for a month.”

“I am not asking you to stop,” he said. “I am asking for the shape of it. I want a number you do not have to carry.”

She turned that over the way she turned every sentence he said, fully, without deciding in advance.

“There is no number I do not carry,” she said. “Every number I have is in my mouth or under my hand. The ones in my mouth I say. The ones under my hand I do not.”

“Then say the ones in your mouth,” he said, “and leave the stair out of them.”

## What Counting Was For

She sat on the fourteenth with the lamp beside her and looked at her own hands in the edge of the light, three fingers and a thumb on the left where it held the handle, three fingers and a thumb on the right lying flat on the stone doing nothing.

“I learned it at twelve,” she said out loud, sitting. “A price for a standing that goes on has no day. I knew that at twelve and never used it. Counting was never about knowing. It was about holding. Boards held. Days held. Breath held. Everything I could not touch I held by counting, and the holding was warmer than the asking.”

The sentences went on above her one at a time.

“Nine years of being warm at him,” she said, “and the warmth was mine. I said that on the seventh of the month beginning two thousand eight hundred and ninety-four and it has not gone stale. Counting him was the warmest holding I had. Tonight I hold a stair by not counting it, and it is the coldest holding I have, and both of them are true.”

“You hold me still,” he said.

“I hold you still,” she said. “You are held and you are not a number. That is the whole of what nineteen years taught me and it took a stair with no top to teach it.”

## The Figures, in Their Place

She said the day's figures on the fourteenth, on the twenty-sixth of the month beginning two thousand eight hundred and ninety-four, because the fourth landing was a day's climb below her and she was not going down to it for this.

“One hundred and eighty-seven days since the ninth stage. Six hundred and eighteen days in that room. The cage out three hundred and eighty-five. This arm open six hundred and twenty-five days. One thousand three hundred and sixty gone at this morning.”

The bar stood five hundred and seventy-five days against a wall she had not touched in a month, and dated nowhere, and she said that last, the way she always said it last.

“Five hundred and seventy-five and dated nowhere.”

Her breath went four counts sitting still, because she had sat down without noticing that she had, with her back against the wall and the lamp on the stone beside her.

“Nineteen years of counting,” she said out loud, sitting, into the dark, “and the whole of it was a way of holding things without touching them. I held the boards. I held the days. I held him. Watch me now not hold a stair.”

## The Ascent Without a Number

She went up past the fourteenth with the lamp held low, so the light ran along the treads instead of into her eyes, and she did not count one of them.

That was harder than the climb the night before, when the light went down by the step and she watched it go. Not counting was a thing she had never done walking, except once, on the twentieth, when she worked out loud while walking, and it cost her.

She remembered the fall the way she remembered everything on this stair, which was with her body first and her mouth second. The place behind her fourth rib pulled as she climbed.

“Not counting,” she said between breaths, because saying it kept her from doing it. “Fourteenth behind. Next ahead. That is all the arithmetic there is.”

Halfway up the flight her feet wanted a number the way her mouth had wanted food for two years without being hungry, which is to say out of habit and not out of need, and she stopped and put her forehead against the wall until the wanting went past.

“You are doing it,” he said.

“I am doing it badly,” she said, with her face to the stone.

“Badly is still doing it,” he said. “I want the badly. I want the whole of the badly and not the clean version.”

She laughed, once, with her forehead against the wall, because in nine years he had never once asked for the badly.

Three treads higher the wanting came back, stronger, with a shape on it this time: ten treads, twelve treads, a number for the flight the way she had numbers for everything. She stopped again and set the lamp on the tread and stood with both hands against the wall, and the place behind her fourth rib pulled, and she breathed nine standing still until it came back to seven.

“Ten,” she said out loud, to hear how it sounded. “Twelve. There. Said and spent, and not even in order. Not counted. Said is not counted and that is the difference between a mouth and a column.”

“Saying is not counting,” he said. “I am keeping the said ones. They are not numbers. They are breath with shape.”

## The Fifteenth, Named

The landing above the fourteenth was narrower than the fourteenth, and the wall on the stair side fell away sooner, and the dark past it was deeper, and she said it where it was used.

“Fifteenth.”

She set the lamp down and sat beside it and looked at the flame, which stood the height of a grain of rice above the wick, and at the light going four paces out and stopping, and at her own hand with three fingers and a thumb lying flat on stone no lamp had touched before tonight.

“You bring light where there is none,” he said. “Four paces of it. That is not nothing on a stair that never had any.”

“Four paces going down by the night,” she said. “Six on the third and four here. The going is the truth of it. I watch it go the way I watch days go. Not counted. Watched.”

“One more landing and not one step counted,” she said out loud, sitting. “Nineteen years and I have finally done a thing badly on purpose and it has got me somewhere.”

The sentences went on above her one at a time, plain, reporting, through her to him, and his half went on inside her where she could hear it, and the two goings sat next to each other the way they had sat for a month.

She put her hand on the place behind her fourth rib without deciding to, the way she had been doing since the twentieth, and it did not do anything at all, and she left her hand there the way she left the lamp where it stood, which was as a keeping and not as a mending.

“Here is the decision,” she said out loud, with her hand where it was. “I go up counting landings and nothing else until there are no more landings. When the stair stops giving me landings I stop counting and keep going. That is the arrangement and I am saying it standing.”

She stood up to say it, because decisions were lines, and then she sat back down, because the stone was cold and her arm was shaking and sitting was where she lived now.

“Decisions are standing,” she said, sitting. “Keeping them is sitting. I have got both.”

She sat on the fifteenth landing with the lamp a grain of rice in front of her and her hands flat on stone no lamp had touched before tonight, and the decision sat next to her the way the nothing had sat next to the fourteen makings. Landings and nothing else until no more landings. Then going on without counting. That was the arrangement and she had said it standing, and the stone took her sitting the way stone takes everything.
