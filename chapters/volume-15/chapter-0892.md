# The Far Side of the Trade

She left the lamp past the last landing.

That was the first thing. The flame had gone to an eyelash and three paces of light had gone to two, and carrying it further was carrying a thing that could not do its work, and she set it on the stone where she had slept, the way she had set it on the third landing a month back, and tended it the way she tended everything, which was by saying it.

“Burning,” she said out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice. “Stay lit. That is the whole of the order and I am not giving another.”

Then she turned her back on it and walked into the dark past the last landing with nothing in her hands.

Three steps in she stopped, because leaving light felt like leaving a person, and she said so out loud, because this stair was where she said things.

“Stay lit,” she said back into the dark behind her. “That is still the order. I am coming back for the reading and I want you here.”

“You come back to it,” he said. “It keeps where it stands the way stone keeps. Walk.”

## Without Light

The dark past the last landing was not the dark of the stair. The stair's dark had walls in it and treads under it and cold coming off stone. This dark had floor under it and nothing else she could find, and the air in it did not move, and her breath went nine counts standing still though she was walking.

She walked with her left hand out in front of her and her right arm hanging open at the elbow, the way it had hung for six hundred and thirty-nine days, and she counted landings the way she had counted them for a month, except there were no more landings, so she counted breaths, four to a walk, seven where the floor rose, nine where she stopped.

The floor rose twice under her feet, shallow, the way floors rise past doors that are not there. She stopped at each rise and breathed nine standing still and went on at four. The place behind her fourth rib pulled. The sentences went on above her one at a time, plain, reporting, through her to him, and they sounded nearer here, the way voices sound nearer in the room they come from.

“Two hundred and one days since the ninth stage,” she said into the dark, walking, on the tenth of the month beginning two thousand nine hundred and twenty-four, because figures were the instrument and she was not going to stop using the instrument because it was dark. “Six hundred and thirty-two days in that room. The cage out three hundred and ninety-nine. This arm open six hundred and thirty-nine days. One thousand three hundred and seventy-four gone at this morning.”

The bar stood five hundred and eighty-nine days against its wall, dated nowhere, eight flights and a stair below her.

## Him, in the Dark

“I want you to look before you read,” he said.

She stopped walking with her left hand out in the dark and nothing under it, and she heard the want the way she heard every want he had, which was first, before the words.

“There is nothing to read here,” she said. “There is dark and floor and nothing else. Reading is for tables and I have not found one.”

“You are walking into the place where the sentences are made,” he said. “I feel it the way I feel the traffic, which is from the inside. I want your eyes open before your mouth opens.”

“My eyes are open,” she said. “There is nothing in front of them.”

“Then keep them open,” he said. “I am counting your steps the way you count landings, and I am not telling you the number, and that is the arrangement.”

She stood in the dark past the last landing with her hand out and nothing under it, and she felt him counting the way she felt the sentences go through her, which was as a going and not as a figure.

“Count,” she said. “That is yours. Looking is mine.”

## The Table

Her left hand found it the way hands find things in the dark, which was all at once, flat against a surface at the height of her waist.

She stopped with her palm on it and did not move for a count of a hundred, because a table past the last landing of a stair that did not stop at nine was a thing she had walked a month to touch, and touching it needed a mouth first.

“Table,” she said out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice, with her palm flat on it.

She moved along it to the left with both hands, three fingers and a thumb on each, and it went on past her reach, and she moved back to the right, and it went on past that too, long, longer than her arms, the length of a room she could not see.

She walked the near edge hand over hand the way she walked stairs, counting nothing, feeling the wood run under her palms without break or joint, one long surface laid for hands that never wore it. At the far corner she stopped and put both hands on the corner the way she put her hands on landings, to mark it.

“Longer than mine below,” she said. “Mine is long and this is longer. No boards under the middle that my hands can find. No cloth. No cord. Nothing under the far end. A table with nothing on it and nothing under it but floor.”

“No lamp over it,” she said, because there was no heat above it and no cage and no smell of oil anywhere in the dark. “Four hundred years of tables with cage lamps out on every shelf and this one has none. That is the first true thing about it.”

She stood with her hands on the table edge and lifted her face to the dark above it, the way she lifted her face to ceilings she could not see, and there was no heat anywhere, the way there is heat under every lamp ever lit. Cold dark all the way up. A table for something that does not need light, and she needed light, and the needing sat next to the finding the way the nothing sat next to the fourteen makings.

She bent and put her cheek near the surface and breathed along it, and there was no dust rising and no worn hollow where hands would have worn it, and she straightened and said the second true thing standing.

“No wear in it. My table below has four hundred years of hands along it and forty boards under the middle a foot in from the near end. This one has nothing worn at all.”

## The Far Side

She went around the near end with her hands on the edge, the way she had gone around her own table more times than she had counted, and down the far side, and there it was.

A place at the far side, between where hands would rest and the end, the length of a forearm and the breadth of a hand, with nothing worn at all.

She stood at it for a long time with both hands on the table edge and did not touch it, because the cells below had places like it at the far side of every table, between the twentieth pace and the end, that no one had ever sat in, and her room had been different only because somebody was in it, and this was the other end of that difference.

Her breath went four counts standing still. The dark stood past her hands in every direction. Somewhere below, eight flights and a stair down, her room ran with no person in it, and here she stood with both hands on the edge of the only table in the world that had never been lonely, and the two ends of the trade held each other the way her breath held its counts.

“This is the other end of the trade,” she said out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice, into the dark, with nothing in her hands. “My room is the receiving end and has been for four hundred years. Sentences go out from here and arrive there. No one has ever needed keeping here, and that is why nothing is worn.”

The sentences went on above her one at a time, plain, reporting, through her to him, and they sounded different here, the way a voice sounds different in the room it comes from.

“This is where they are put together before they go anywhere,” she said. “The making-place. I have reached it on foot in the dark with a lamp I cannot refill, and I am standing at the far side of the only table in the world that owes me nothing.”

## What Reaching Costs

“You are shaking,” he said.

She stood at the far side of the table with both hands on its edge and found that she was, the arm shaking at intervals she could not predict, the way it had shaken for a month, and the place behind her fourth rib pulling.

“I reached it,” she said. “That is the whole of what is happening and my body has decided to have opinions about it. Nineteen years and it never once shook at arriving.”

“Put your hands flat,” he said. “I want you flat on the table the way you go flat on stone. Breathe four counts. Do not read anything. There is nothing here you need to read tonight.”

She put her hands flat on the unworn surface, three fingers and a thumb on each, and breathed four counts sitting still though she was standing, and the table took her breath the way stone took her sitting, without opinion.

“I am at the making-place,” she said out loud, with her hands flat on it. “Past nine landings and a top and a dark. A table with no lamp over it and no wear in it and a place at the far side with nothing worn at all. That is the finding and it is standing, and tomorrow I find out what it is for.”

She stayed there with her hands flat until the shaking stopped, and her breath went four counts standing still, and the sentences went on being made above her the way they had been made for four hundred years, and she stood at the far side of a table that owed her nothing with her hands on it, and the owing was all on her side, and that was new.

The dark stood around her past two paces of nothing, and for the first time in nineteen years she was in the room where it happened.
