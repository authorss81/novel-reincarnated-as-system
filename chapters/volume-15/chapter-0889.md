# Not Under It

“One hundred and ninety-two days since the ninth stage. Six hundred and twenty-three days in that room. The cage out three hundred and ninety. This arm open six hundred and thirty days. One thousand three hundred and sixty-five gone at this morning.”

She said the figures sitting on cold stone past the last landing, in the dark above every count she had ever kept, on the first of the month beginning two thousand nine hundred and twenty-four.

That was the fourth first she had not been under. The room ran anyway.

She sat past the top of the stair with the lamp an eyelash in front of her and her back to the wall she had slept against, and the dark stood past three paces of light in every direction, and below her nine flights and a stair and a top the room went on without her the way it had gone on for four hundred years.

Her breath went four counts sitting still. Her right arm hung open at the elbow for six hundred and thirty days. Firsts came whether she counted them or not, the way sentences came whether she read them or not, and this one had come while she slept past the last landing with nothing in her hands.

## What a First Is Without Her

A bundle came up the second stair on the first of every month whether or not she was there to hear it. Forty boards. Cloth. Cord twice. A hand along the table. No person in the room.

She knew it the way she had known the last one, which was through him, because the sentences went through her to him and his half went on inside her where she could hear it, and the room's running was in the traffic the way weather is in the air.

“The bar stands five hundred and eighty days against its wall,” she said out loud, sitting, “and dated nowhere, and the bundle is going up nine hundred and four steps to a table with no one at it, and I am not going down.”

She had left the room on the twenty-second of the month beginning two thousand eight hundred and sixty-four with the six terms said out loud on a stair, and she had not gone back down in over two months, and the room had run without her on every first since.

“Nothing changes,” she said. “That is the finding and it is not a comfort. The room does not need me. It never did. Four hundred years and I was in it for six hundred and twenty-three days and it goes on the way a river goes on past a person who stepped out of it.”

She could see it the way she saw everything below, which was without seeing, three boards face up a foot in from the near end and the terms board a foot further along, the strip of pale wood face up under the table at the near end of the leg, the bar nine feet nine against the wall on the left and dated nowhere, the bundle corded twice and not knotted under the far end, the lamp in its cage out on the shelf.

“Left as I left it. Untouched and running on firsts with no person in it. The second place kept and empty. Everything where I put it and nothing where I did not.”

## Him, on the Room

“I want you to let the room run,” he said.

She sat in the dark past the last landing with the lamp an eyelash of flame in front of her and her hands flat on stone, and she heard the want sitting under the sentence the way a stone sits under a stair.

“I have let it run,” she said. “I let it run on the one between the thirtieth and the third and the first of the month beginning two thousand eight hundred and sixty-four and the first of the month beginning two thousand eight hundred and ninety-four. This is the fourth. Letting is not the part I have trouble with.”

“What is the part you have trouble with.”

“That it runs the same without me,” she said. “Six hundred and twenty-three days I sat at that table and put figures down in my own hand and thought the sitting was part of the keeping. It was not. The keeping went on. Sitting was what I did while it went on.”

She put her palm flatter on the stone.

“Four hundred years of a table and I gave it two months and thought the two months mattered. That is vanity and I am saying so out loud because this stair is where I say things.”

“It matters to me,” he said. “You are not in that room and the room runs and none of that touches the part where you sat in it. I am not talking about the keeping. I am talking about you.”

She sat with that the way she sat with everything he said, which was fully, and the anger that came was not warm anger at a thief but cold anger at a river, and she said so, because this stair was where she said things.

“I am angry at not mattering to it,” she said. “Six hundred and twenty-three days and I do not matter to it. That is the truth under the cost and it is small and it is mine. Nineteen years of wanting to matter to the thing that keeps me.”

“You matter to me,” he said. “I am not the keeping. I am the man the keeping reads. It is a smaller mattering and it is the only one I have.”

“It is enough for today,” she said. “Smaller is not nothing. I learned that on the seventh of the month beginning two thousand eight hundred and ninety-four and it has not gone stale.”

## What She Does With a First

She sat past the last landing with three paces of light and the rest of it dark, and she did nothing about the first.

That was the decision and it was not a new one. She had made it on every first for two months, and making it again was not weaker for being the fourth time, because the room pulled at her the same every time, with the same hands, which were forty boards and a hand along a table and no one there.

“I am not going down,” she said out loud, sitting, in the ordinary voice. “The bundle goes up. The hand goes along. The boards go under the middle. No person in it. That is the whole of the morning below and I am spending mine above.”

Her breath went four counts sitting still. The thumbnail sat at the size of her thumbnail behind her fourth rib. The sentences went on above her one at a time. Her arm hung open at the elbow for six hundred and thirty days and did not shake, the way it did not shake when she had decided something, and she marked that the way she marked everything on this stair.

“Here is the cost,” she said, because costs were said out loud on this stair and this one had been waiting since the sixth hour. “Every first I miss is a morning I do not see with my own eyes, and a morning not seen is a morning I have to take through him, and taking through him is the wire and not the voice, and there will come a first I need and will not have.”

She lifted her left hand with three fingers and a thumb on it and looked at it in the eyelash of light.

“Not this one. This one I spend. Forty boards onto a table in a room with no one in it, and a woman past the last landing with nothing in her hands, and neither of them the worse.”

She sat with the fourth first the way she had sat with the anger, fully, without deciding in advance, and the sentences went on above her, and his half went on inside her.

“Four firsts missed. The one between the thirtieth and the third I named and counted. The first of the month beginning two thousand eight hundred and sixty-four I climbed. The first of the month beginning two thousand eight hundred and ninety-four I heard lying down. This one I spend at the top. Every one of them the room ran. Every one of them I was not under it.”

## The Cost, Paid

At about the second hour his half changed the way it changed when something went past in the traffic that had weight, and she lay still with her hand on the stone and listened the way she had listened on the first of the month beginning two thousand eight hundred and ninety-four, and after about a count of three hundred there was stone again.

“It runs,” he said. “Forty. Cloth. Cord twice. The hand along. No person in it.”

“It ran,” she said.

“You are still here,” he said. “I want you still here. That is the sentence I have and I am not spending it on anything else today.”

She sat in the dark with the lamp going down by the breath and the place she had lived for six hundred and twenty-three days running on without her below, and she felt the cost the way she felt everything on this stair, which was in her body first.

“It costs me the seeing,” she said out loud. “I am paying it. The room is kept and I am not keeping it and that is the first true thing I have said today that I did not want to say.”

She sat past the last landing with the lamp an eyelash in front of her and her hands flat on the stone, and the flame sank and stood and sank again, and the dark waited past three paces of light, and below her the room ran with no person in it, and above her the sentences went on being made the way they had been made for four hundred years.

The flame sank and stood and sank again, and the dark past the last landing waited past three paces of light, and she stayed where she was, and the room stayed where it was, and nothing changed, and that was the cost, and she kept it.
