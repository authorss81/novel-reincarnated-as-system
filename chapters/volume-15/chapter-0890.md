# The Name He Gives

She went down to the lamp at about the sixth hour, because the flame past the last landing had gone to an eyelash and tending was tending wherever the lamp stood.

That was the first time in three days she had gone down past the ninth, and the stair took her the way it always took her, which was cold first and then colder, past the fifteenth she had named without counting, past the fourteenth, past the thirteenth where she had fallen and not fallen, with her left hand on the stone and the lamp caged in the crook of her left arm.

Her breath went seven counts on the way down, the way it went on rises, because going down past the ninth was a rise of its own, back toward the light she had left. The place behind her fourth rib pulled. Her right arm swung open at the elbow for six hundred and thirty-three days and took nothing, the way it took nothing.

“Ninth,” she said where it was used, going past. “Tenth. Eleventh.” Saying landings was what she did with counts she could not put down anywhere else, and tonight the count she could not put down was the asking.

## On the Twelfth

He was on the twelfth landing.

She knew him by his breathing before her light reached him, because she had lived eight flights above him for two months and his breath had a shape the way everyone's breath had a shape, and his was quick and young and careful on the rises.

He had two lamps with him, both dark, and a cloth black at the edges, and his hands were black to the wrist, and he stood up when her light came down the stair, the way a person stands when they have been waiting to be seen.

She had had him in a room for two hundred days. She had never once asked his name, and she had said out loud more than once that she was not going to.

Two hundred days of oil carried up eight hundred and eighty steps on his own account, three lamps in rotation, a store never counted in any figure. He kept the oil count the way she kept days. She had watched his hands go black over two months and had never once asked what the hands belonged to.

“Nineteen years of record,” she said out loud, stopping three treads above the landing, “and I have asked one person his name in all of it. The bearer told me on the twenty-sixth and I did the arithmetic in my mouth instead of on a board.”

The boy stood with his dark lamps and his black hands and waited, because waiting was what he did best.

## Him, on Asking

“I want to hear it when you hear it,” he said.

She stood on the stair three treads above the twelfth landing with the lamp in her arm and looked down at the boy in the edge of her light, and at the dark past him going down nine flights to a room that was running without her, and at her own left hand with three fingers and a thumb on the lamp handle.

“I have never asked anybody anything,” she said. “That is true and it is nineteen years true. Asking is a taking and I have done taking enough on this stair.”

“Asking is not taking,” he said. “You are the only person I know who thinks it is. I want the name in your mouth and not only in the record.”

“The record has nothing in it about him,” she said. “He has been in it without knowing there is one. That is the whole of what the record holds and it is not nothing.”

“Then ask him,” he said. “I am not asking for the record. I am asking for you.”

## The Second Name

She went down the three treads and stood on the twelfth landing in front of the boy, the way she stood in front of things she said lines to, which was facing them, with nothing in either hand.

The lamp hung in the crook of her arm. The sentences went on above her one at a time, plain, reporting, through her to him. His half went on inside her where she could hear it.

Her mouth would not open.

That was the whole of it for a count of twenty. Nineteen years of record and one name asked in all of it, and the second one sat behind her teeth the way the count had sat for four days, a weight and not a number. The boy stood with his dark lamps and waited, because waiting was what he did best, and he did not look away.

“Asking is not taking,” he said in her head. “I am here. Say it.”

“What is your name,” she said.

It was the second name she had asked in nineteen years of record, and it came out in the ordinary voice, standing, the way findings came out, because there was no other way she knew how to say things that mattered.

The boy looked at her lamp, and at her arm hanging open at the elbow the way it had hung for six hundred and thirty-three days, and at her face past the light, and he said it.

“Lu An.”

The name went past into the traffic one word, plain, reporting, through her to him, and nothing answered, the way nothing answers names at once. She stood on the twelfth landing with the name in her mouth where she had put it, and the boy stood with his dark lamps, and the stair stood around them both the way it had stood for four hundred years, and none of the three moved.

She did not repeat it. She put it in her mouth the way she had put the bearer's name in her mouth on the twenty-sixth, and did the arithmetic there instead of on a board, and the arithmetic was this: two names asked in nineteen years, the first one carried up nine hundred and four steps for four hundred years, the second one standing on a twelfth landing with black hands and no idea of any record.

“Two,” she said out loud, because figures were the only thing she had ever been able to trust, and this one had a derivation, which was her own life. “Two asked. Nineteen years. That is the count and there is nothing else behind it.”

## The Third Voice

Feet came up the stair from below while she stood there, slow, heavy, stopping every few treads the way laden feet stop, and a man came into the edge of her light with oil on his hands to the wrist, black in the lamplight, and a cloth over his shoulder black at the edges the same way the boy's was.

He stopped two treads below the landing and looked at her lamp, and at the boy, and at her arm, and he nodded the way working people nod at each other, once, without surprise.

“Going up,” he said. “Mind the tread past the tenth. It takes the foot.”

“I know it. The polished tread past the twelfth. Mind your own feet.”

“I mind them,” he said, and went past them up the stair with his oil hands and his black cloth, and the dark took him past three paces of light, and his feet went on up toward the thirteenth, steady, laden, stopping every few treads the way laden feet stop.

She stood on the twelfth with the boy and watched the light move on the wall where the stranger had gone, until it steadied, and the feet went on until they were past hearing.

She stood on the twelfth with the boy and watched the light move on the wall where the stranger had gone, until it steadied.

“Oil on his hands,” she said out loud. “There is oil in this building and it is on hands and not in any store I have counted, and I have never counted it and I am not going to start.”

## What Stays in Her Mouth

She said the day's figures on the twelfth, on the fourth of the month beginning two thousand nine hundred and twenty-four, because she had not said them at the sixth hour and the day needed them.

“One hundred and ninety-five days since the ninth stage. Six hundred and twenty-six days in that room. The cage out three hundred and ninety-three. This arm open six hundred and thirty-three days. One thousand three hundred and sixty-eight gone at this morning.”

The bar stood five hundred and eighty-three days against its wall, dated nowhere.

Then she looked at the boy, who stood with his dark lamps the way he had stood for two hundred days, and she felt the name sitting in her mouth where she had put it, unspoken by her, unrecorded, her own and not the record's.

“I asked,” she said to him. “That is the whole of what happened on this landing and it is not small. I asked and you said and I am carrying it in my mouth and not on any board.”

The boy nodded, once, the way the stranger had nodded, and picked up his dark lamps, and went down the stair past her toward the eleventh, with his black hands and his quick young breath, and she stood on the twelfth and listened to him go until his feet were past hearing.

“You have it,” he said in her head.

“I have it,” she said out loud, to the empty landing, to the lamp, to the man inside her head who had wanted it in her mouth. “Two names in nineteen years and both of them carried, and the second one is in no record and no column and no line anywhere, and that is where it stays until I say otherwise.”

She picked up her lamp and went back up toward the dark past the last landing, with the name in her mouth and nothing in her hands, and the stair took her the way it always took her, cold first and then colder, and it had never once felt like going home until tonight.

“Ninth,” she said, going past, going up. “Tenth. Eleventh. Twelfth behind.” The landings went past where they were used, and the name sat in her mouth where she had put it, unspoken by her, unrecorded, and the going up was lighter than the going down had been, though nothing on the stair had changed but her.
