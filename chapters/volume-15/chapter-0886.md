# Wick and Oil

The dark above the ninth had a different cold in it.

She had been up past the ninth landing for a month, and she knew the cold of the tenth and the eleventh and the twelfth the way she knew the back of her own hand, but the stair past the thirteenth was colder than any of them, and it came at her sideways, off stone that had never once held a lamp.

She stood on the thirteenth landing with her back to the wall and her lamp in her left hand and waited for her breath to come back to four counts.

## The Light She Has

The flame stood about the height of her smallest finger above the oil, and it leaned toward the stairwell every time she moved, and righted itself every time she stopped.

She had tended that lamp on the third landing since the twenty-third of the month beginning two thousand eight hundred and four, and she knew what it looked like when it was burning on oil and what it looked like when it was burning on the wick, and this was the second one.

She lifted it to the level of her eyes and looked at it the way she had looked at forty boards a foot in from the near end of a table for four hundred and seventy-nine days, which is to say without affection and without looking away.

Four hundred and seventy-nine days of tending lived in her hands the way breath lived in them. She knew the weight of it full and the weight of it going, and the way the handle warmed through the cloth, and the exact sound the cage made when she set it down on stone. None of that was a figure. All of it was hers.

“You are shorter than you were,” she said to it, out loud, in the ordinary voice, because the stair past the ninth was a place where she said things out loud and that had been true for a month.

The flame leaned and righted itself and said nothing, because it was a flame.

## What the Arm Does

Her right arm hung open at the elbow the way it had hung since day two thousand two hundred and ninety-four, and the cold got into the open place first, the way water gets into a cracked pot first.

She had stopped expecting it to take her weight on the twentieth, when she put it out over the edge of a landing she had never stood on and it did not take anything at the elbow, and she had not asked it to take anything since.

She worked the fingers of her left hand around the lamp handle and shifted the whole weight to the left side, and the right arm swung a little with the movement, and the open place behind her fourth rib pulled the size of her thumbnail and held.

Five days it had sat at that size. It was not smaller yesterday and it was not smaller today.

“I am not asking you to carry,” she said to the arm, out loud, because she had begun saying things to parts of herself on this stair and it had stopped feeling strange a fortnight back. “You carry the rib and I carry the lamp and that is the whole of the arrangement.”

## Him, Before the Climb

He had been quiet since the sixth hour, which was not gone quiet, and she had learned the difference the way you learn the difference between a room with one person in it and a room with none.

“I want you back under light before the dark takes the wick,” he said.

She stood on the thirteenth landing with the lamp in her hand and the cold coming sideways off the stone and considered that the way she considered every sentence he said, which was fully, and without deciding in advance.

“The lamp lives on the third landing,” she said. “I left it there on the twenty-third of the month beginning two thousand eight hundred and four and I have tended it since and I have never carried it higher. If I take it back down now I do not come up again tonight.”

“I know where it lives,” he said. “I am not asking for the lamp. I am asking for you.”

“That is a different sentence,” she said.

“It is the sentence I have,” he said. “You are counting what is left in it and I am counting what is left in you and those are not the same count.”

She put her back flatter against the wall and felt the stone take the shape of her shoulder blades through the cloth.

“You have been counting me for nine years,” she said. “Count me going up.”

## What the Sentences Do

She stood on the thirteenth with the lamp in her hand and listened upward, past where her light reached, to where the sentences started above the eleventh, going one at a time through her to him.

That was the wire and not the voice. The working in the room below came up through nine landings of stone once, at about the second hour of a first, and after about a count of three hundred there was stone again. The sentences had no hour on them. They went all day, plain, reporting, at their own intervals, and his half went on inside her where she could hear it, and the two goings sat next to each other and neither moved the other.

“Nine flights of traffic over my head,” she said out loud, “and all of it going through one woman standing on a landing with a lamp going down by the step. That is the arrangement and I am not refusing it. I am climbing through it.”

“You climb through everything,” he said. “Four hundred and seventy-nine days in one room and a month on this stair and you have never once gone around a thing. I want you going around this one flight. Rest the arm. Rest the rib. The top keeps.”

“The top keeps,” she said. “I am not kept. Going up.”

## The Figures, Said Where They Are Used

She said the day's figures on the thirteenth, on the twenty-third of the month beginning two thousand eight hundred and ninety-four, because she was going to need them above it and it was cheaper to say them once. “One hundred and eighty-four days since the ninth stage. Six hundred and fifteen days in that room. The cage out three hundred and eighty-two. This arm open six hundred and twenty-two by the count. One thousand three hundred and fifty-seven gone at this morning.”

She said the bar last, because the bar was dated nowhere and had stood five hundred and seventy-two days against a wall she could not see from here, and saying it last was her way of keeping it where it belonged, which was below.

“Five hundred and seventy-two and dated nowhere.”

The sentences went on above her one at a time, plain, reporting, through her to him, at their own intervals, and his half went on inside her where she could hear it.

“Landings, not steps,” she said out loud, standing, because a landing was a thing she could be wrong about and a stair was not, and she was going up it for the last time and did not want to arrive holding a number.

## The Climb

She went up past the thirteenth with the lamp held out to the side at the full length of her left arm, so the light went ahead of her feet instead of into her eyes.

The tread above the thirteenth was narrower than the tread below it, and the stone was drier, and there was no polish on it anywhere, which meant no feet had worn it smooth, which meant no one went up there, which meant she was the first, and she said none of that out loud because the first of a thing was not a figure and she had stopped spending those.

Her breath went seven counts on the rise. Her left hand sweated on the lamp handle. The flame bent flat against the wick with each step and stood back up between them, shorter every time, and she watched it the way she had watched everything on this stair, which was the way a person watches a thing that is telling the truth.

Halfway up she stopped and set the lamp on the tread above her and rested her forehead against the stone of the wall, and the cold went into her forehead and steadied her the way cold stone had steadied her for two days of sitting.

“You are past it,” he said. “I am with you and you are past the worst of the flight.”

“I know where I am,” she said, without opening her eyes. “I am three treads from the next landing and the landing is the thing I count and I have not got to it yet.”

“Then get to it,” he said. “I am not carrying the lamp. I am keeping the count of you.”

She got to it the way she got to everything on this stair, which was one tread and then another, with her breath going seven on the rise and the place behind her fourth rib pulling, and the flame bending flat with each step and standing back up between them, lower every time.

Three treads from the landing her left knee went, the way knees go on stairs in the dark, sideways and without warning, and she caught herself on the wall with her right shoulder, and the open arm swung with the catch and did nothing, the way it had done nothing since day two thousand two hundred and ninety-four, and she stood there with her face to the stone until the shaking stopped.

“Not the arm,” she said out loud, with her shoulder against the wall. “Not asked. Not used. The legs do the stair and the left hand does the lamp and the right does the rib. That is the division and it holds.”

She got to the landing.

“Wick,” she said to herself, and then out loud, because out loud was the instrument. “Oil would stand higher. This is wick and it is going down by the step.”

She picked the lamp up and went on.

## What Gives First

What gave first was not the light and not the arm.

It was her breath, on the third tread from the top of that flight, going nine counts standing still when she was standing on a stair and not still at all, and she stood there with her face to the wall and waited for it to come back to seven and then to four, and it did, because breath was the one instrument she had that had never once lied to her.

She stood with her face to the wall until her breath came back to four, and the flame stood back up, lower, and she picked up the lamp.

“You keep my count,” she said. “Keep it close. Tonight it is the only number on this stair.”

“I keep it close,” he said.

## The Fourteenth, Named

The landing above the thirteenth was a landing the way the others were landings, stone and wall and dark and nothing else, and she said it where it was used.

“Fourteenth.”

She set the lamp on the stone and sat down beside it with her back against the wall and her hands flat on the floor on either side of her, and the flame stood about the height of a grain of rice above the wick now, and the light reached four paces out from where it stood and stopped there.

Six paces it had reached on the third landing. Four paces here.

She looked at the difference between those two reaches for a long time, the way she had looked at the difference between nine years and four hundred, and she did not put it into a figure because there was no figure for the reach of a light, and she had spent nineteen years refusing to put figures in that had nothing behind them.

“I went up on wick as much as on oil,” she said out loud, sitting, in the ordinary voice. “That is the cost of tonight and I am paying it with light.”

He was quiet for a count of twenty, which was his way of letting a cost sit where it was instead of standing in front of it.

“Come down to the twelfth to sleep,” he said. “I want you sleeping where the stone knows you.”

She looked at the flame going down by the breath, and at the dark past the fourteenth landing going up, and at her own left hand with three fingers and a thumb on it lying flat on stone that had never held a lamp before tonight.

“The stone here knows me now,” she said.
