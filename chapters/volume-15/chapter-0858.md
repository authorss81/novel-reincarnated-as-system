# The Forearm Price

Ninety-nine days since the ninth stage. Five hundred and thirty days in that room. The cage out two hundred and ninety-seven days. Right arm open five hundred and thirty-seven days and it is not going to close. One thousand two hundred and seventy-two of the days gone at this morning and not one coming back.

There was a lamp burning on the stone of the third landing above a room that was not a room, and there was a woman sitting beside it who had a name in her mouth that she had asked for two days before, and there was a question under the name that she had not asked, and she was going to ask it.

## What She Wants to Know

She said it out loud, sitting, in the ordinary voice, at about the sixth hour of the twenty-eighth of the month beginning two thousand eight hundred and four, before she did anything else about it.

She said, “Figures first and the reason after. Ninety-nine days since the ninth. Five hundred and thirty in that room. Two hundred and ninety-seven since a lamp in a cage went out. Five hundred and thirty-seven with this arm open and it is not going to close. One thousand two hundred and seventy-two of the days gone and not one coming back.”

She said, “And here is the reason. Two days ago a man told me his name through a wall and I did the arithmetic in my mouth and came back up, and I have had two days to sit beside a lamp with it, and what I have got is that I know how many steps he walks and I do not know what he walks them for.”

She said, “And it is shame, and I have not had it out loud in nineteen years, and I am having it out loud now, before I ask him what he is paid in, because that is the order I do things in.”

She said, “I have been read to since I was seven years old by a layer I never asked the maker of, and I have stood at a table for nineteen years putting figures into hands I never asked the owner of, and the shame is not that I did not know. The shame is that it never once felt like not knowing.”

She said, “And what I want is to find out what a man does for four hundred years on nine hundred and four steps. That is the want and it stands on its own, with nothing underneath it holding it up.”

She picked up the lamp and went down the stair in its light to the first landing, and she counted the landings and not the steps, and she said so out loud where it was used.

## The Asking

She set the lamp on the stone of the first landing and stood at the gap in the near wall of that room, and she did not go through it.

She said, out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice, “Han Liang.”

And after about a count of forty the boots came up the second stair, slow, without boards in them, and a man came out of the place in the wall behind the shelf and stood in the dark of that room, and did not come to the gap.

He said, “Say it.”

She said, “I asked your name two days ago and you gave it. I am asking a second thing today and you do not have to answer, the same as before.”

He said, “Ask it.”

She said, “What have you been doing for four hundred years, up and down nine hundred and four steps with forty boards under one arm.”

He said, “Carrying.”

She said, “I know the carrying. I have the arithmetic in my mouth and it is one thousand eight hundred and eight steps every carrying. What is the carrying for.”

There was a silence in that room that went on for about a count of a hundred and forty, and the cage lamp stood out on the shelf, and the bar stood against the wall on the left where it had stood four hundred and eighty-seven days, and the bundle lay under the far end corded twice and not knotted.

He said, “I carry boards up. Hands go along them. I carry what is left back down.”

She said, “And what are you paid.”

He said, “Out of a forearm.”

She stood at the gap with her hands on the stone on either side of it, and the lamp burned behind her on the landing, and a little light went through onto the floor of that room and stopped.

She said, “Say that again, and say it slow, because I have a place in front of me that is of the length of a forearm and the breadth of a hand, and I have refused it twice, and I want to know what you mean and I do not want to be wrong about it.”

## Out of a Forearm

He said, “There is a place at the far side of every table in this trade. Of the length of a forearm and the breadth of a hand. It is kept. No person has ever sat in any of them.”

She said, “I know that place. I have had my hands on four of them. Go on.”

He said, “I have carried boards up and down nine hundred and four steps for four hundred years, and I have never once been told what the trade is for, and I have never once asked.”

She said, “And the pay.”

He said, “Out of a forearm. That is what I was told when I was given the road. A forearm out of every keeping, for the carrying. I did not ask what a forearm was and no person told me.”

She said, “And did you know.”

He said, “After a time I knew. A man learns the length of what he is paid in. I carry forty boards a month past places of the length of a forearm, and I am paid out of one of them, and I knew within the first year and I have known every year since.”

She said it out loud before she could stop herself, standing at the gap, in a voice that was not quite the ordinary voice.

She said, “And you went on.”

He said, “Once I said no to it.”

She stood still.

She said, “Say that again.”

He said, “Once, about three hundred years ago, I said no to it, on that stair, out loud, with boards under my arm. I put them down on the turn and I said I would not carry any more of them. And there was no person on that stair to hear it, and the boards lay there, and after about a count of a hundred and forty I picked them up again and went on up, because there was nowhere else on that road for a man to go.”

She said, “And after that.”

He said, “After that I never said it again.”

## What That Does to Four Hundred Years

She stood at the gap for a long time with that in her, and the lamp burned on the landing behind her, and the man stood in the dark of that room beside a table twenty-two paces long and waited, the way a person waits who has waited four hundred years and is good at it.

She said, out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice, “Here is what I have got, and I am saying it before I go, because a thing said afterwards is a thing you have made up.”

She said, “You have been inside the bargain’s keeping for four hundred years without ever having been told what it is, and you worked out your own pay out of the length of it, and you said no to it once, out loud, on a stair, with boards under your arm, and no person heard it, and you picked them up again.”

She said, “And that is more than I have done. I said no twice where everything could hear it and sent it down in my own hand, and you said it once where nothing could hear it and picked the boards up again, and yours cost you three hundred years and mine cost me forty days.”

She said, “And I am angry. And that is not the shame from this morning, and I have worked out that it is not the same, and it is worse, because the shame was at myself and the anger is at a trade that pays a man out of a kept place and never tells him what keeping is.”

She said, “And what I want now is not what I wanted this morning. This morning I wanted to know what you do. Now I want to know what it would take to stop paying you out of it. And I do not know, and I am saying that I do not know, and that is the whole of the want.”

He said, out of the dark, “There is no stopping it from where you are standing.”

She said, “I know.”

She picked up the lamp and went back up the stair to the third landing, and she did not count the steps, and the light went four steps in front of her and stopped, and the boards lay on the table in the dark behind her where he had put them without looking.

She set the lamp on the stone where it had stood and sat beside it, and she said the finding out loud, sitting, in the ordinary voice, as one sentence, because there was not one word to put in front of it and not one word to put after it.

She said, “He has been paid out of a forearm for four hundred years, and he knew, and he said no to it once, and nobody heard it, and he goes on.”

And that was the day, and it was not what she had gone down that stair for, and it changed everything under it, because there was now a man in the trade who had priced it from the inside without ever having been told, and she had heard him, and she could not unhear him.
