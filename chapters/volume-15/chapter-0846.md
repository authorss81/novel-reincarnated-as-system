# The Lamp She Left

Sixty-three days since the ninth stage. Four hundred and ninety-four days in that room. The cage out two hundred and sixty-one days. Right arm open five hundred and one days and it is not going to close. One thousand two hundred and thirty-six days lighter at this morning and not one going back.

There was no person in that room and no person on any stair in that tower, and there was a lamp burning on a stone floor in a building that had never had a lamp on it, set down on the second landing above a table twenty-two paces long.

## Up Again, And Not Counted

She went up at about the second hour of the twenty-second of the month beginning two thousand seven hundred and seventy-four and she did not count it, and she did not say out loud that she was not going to, because she had said that at the foot of it on the thirteenth and a rule that has to be announced twice is not a rule.

The stair wound and there was no rail, and the stone was the same stone, and her breath went four counts on the level places and seven on the rise.

She picked the lamp up off the stone of the first landing and carried it on up.

She said, out loud, on the last step before the second landing, in the ordinary voice, “One lamp.”

She said, “And that is the whole of what I have brought up a stair to look at a building, and I am saying that out loud standing here so that it is said.”

## The Same Room And Worse

There was a doorway on the far side of that landing and a room behind it with a table in it, and she stood in the doorway and did not go in for about the length of one of her own mornings, and then she went in.

She said, out loud, after a count of a hundred and forty, in the ordinary voice, “It is worse.”

She said, “And I want to be exact about what worse is, because if I am not exact then I am going to be frightened of the wrong thing for the rest of this.”

She said, “The table is the same. Twenty-two paces, four feet off the floor, a leg at every two paces, and the flag that moves at about the nineteenth. The cage is out on the shelf at the far end. It is not a different room. It is the same room again, and that is already more than I had, and it is not what makes it worse.”

She put her left hand on the leg of that table at the near end of it and stood there for about a count of forty.

She said, out loud, “My hand knows that leg. I have never been in that room before today and my hand has been on a leg the same as that one two hundred days running, and it went along it without me deciding anything.”

She said, “And that is how I knew it was twenty-two paces before I had paced it. I knew it because I have already walked one.”

She said, “What makes it worse is under the middle of that table.”

## Forty Boards Face Up

She carried the lamp to the middle of that room and put it down on the top of that table, and the light went along the wood a long way, further than it had gone on her own because her own is twenty-two paces and the light on it goes a hand’s width, and this one is the same length and the light went about eleven paces.

Under the middle of that table there were forty boards lying face up in a stack.

She said, out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice, “Forty.”

She said, “That is the number that goes on my table on the first of a month, four to a minute, in a cloth with a cord going round it twice. Forty. And I have counted forty on the first of a month for nineteen years and I have counted them out loud in that building every single time, and there has not been one single one of them that has ever had a second number on it.”

She stood there.

She said, “There is a second number here.”

She said, “And I am not going to say it out loud. I have been in this room about a count of a hundred and forty and I do not know who put forty boards face up in a room with no lamp in it and no person in it and no bundle coming up any stair to it, and a figure I cannot place is a thing that goes in somebody’s arithmetic, and I have said out loud in that room that I am not going to do that.”

She ran her thumb along the edge of the top board the way she finds writing, and it was not straight, and there was something cut on it, and she put her hand down flat on the top of the stack and left it there.

She said, out loud, “That is a finding and I am carrying it and I am not saying it in here.”

She stood there for about a count of ninety and then she said the next part out loud as well, because a thing she is carrying by herself is a thing she is going to be wrong about.

She said, “The cost of not saying it out loud is that it has no date. I do not know when forty boards were put face up in this room. I do not know whether it was the first of a month or whether they have been lying there since the room was made, and I do not know what the second number on the top one is, and if I find it out in ten days I will not be able to tell anybody when I found it.”

She said, “That is a rule I have had since I was nine years old and I have never once wanted to break it as much as I want to break it in this room, and I am not going to break it in a room with a bundle under the far end of it and nobody in it.”

## Under The Far End

She carried the lamp to the far end of that table and there was a bundle under it with two turns of cord in it and not a knot.

She said, out loud, standing, “That is a bundle.”

She said, “That is my bundle. Corded twice and not knotted, under the far end of that table, with about the same height of air under it as mine has had for as long as I have been in this room, and I have put a hand into that one five times and never pulled.”

She said, “And I am not going to put a hand into this one. I said that out loud in that room about the two hundredth day and I meant it, and a rule about bundles is the only rule I have got that has been in my hand and in my mouth and not on a board.”

She put her hand flat on the top of that table instead.

She said, “There are nine days to the first of this month, and forty boards are going on a table four miles down a mountain from me on the morning of the first, and I am not going to be standing under them, and that is a thing I chose on the thirteenth and I have not gone back on it.”

## The Lamp Is A Mark

And then she looked at the lamp standing on that table and did not pick it up for about a count of ninety.

She said, out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice, at about the fourth hour of the twenty-second of the month beginning two thousand seven hundred and seventy-four:

“Here is what I have got wrong, and I have had it wrong since the seventh hour of the thirteenth, and I am going to say it before I do anything else about tonight.”

She said, “I have been carrying one lamp up a stair as though a lamp were light. It is not. A lamp in a hand is light on about four feet of the world. A lamp set down on a stone floor is light on a whole landing.”

She said, “And I cannot have the first and I cannot have the second, because there is one of it.”

She said, “And I worked out about a count of a hundred and forty ago that I have not been carrying a light up here. I have been carrying a hand that reaches about four feet. That is a different thing, and I have been calling it the same thing since the thirteenth of this month because it is convenient and because it does not make me say anything.”

She said, “I am not carrying a lamp up a stair. I am carrying the end of my own reach. And everything behind the end of my own reach is not there, and I have been behaving as though the stone behind me were still being looked at, and it has not been since the sixteenth.”

She said, “So wherever I put it, everything behind me is unmarked. That is the arithmetic and it is the whole of the cost and I have had it since the thirteenth and I have spent nine days being afraid of the wrong thing.”

## What She Did About It

She carried the lamp off that table and down the length of that room and out through the doorway onto the stone of the second landing and put it down on the stone and left it burning.

She said, out loud, standing, “There is a lamp burning on this landing.”

She said, “And I am saying that out loud before I set it going, and I am not going to take it back. A lamp standing on a stone floor burns whether I am standing beside it or not. I have not left one thing of mine running in a room in nineteen years. I have not had a fire and I have not had a lamp going and I have not had one thing of mine that kept going when I went out of the room.”

She said, “And it is going to burn on this landing tonight with me further up a stair, and there is not one thing I can do about it, and it is the first thing I have ever set going that I cannot stop.”

She picked up nothing, because she had left the only lamp she had, and she stood on that landing in the dark with her left hand on the stone and her right arm open in front of her.

She said, out loud, in the ordinary voice, at about the fourth hour and twenty minutes:

“I am going up in the dark. I am going up past a light I can see behind me and I am not going to carry anything, and that is the first morning in nineteen years I have gone into a part of a building with nothing in my hands.”

And she went up, and the lamp went six paces out from where it stood and stopped, and the first landing behind it had no light on it at all.
