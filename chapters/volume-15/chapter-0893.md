# The First Clause

“Two hundred and four days since the ninth stage. Six hundred and thirty-five days in that room. The cage out four hundred and two. This arm open six hundred and forty-two days. One thousand three hundred and seventy-seven gone at this morning.”

She said the figures standing at the near end of the table in the making-place, on the thirteenth of the month beginning two thousand nine hundred and twenty-four, with her hands on wood no lamp had ever stood over and no hands had ever worn.

The bar stood five hundred and ninety-two days against its wall, dated nowhere, eight flights and a stair and a top below her.

## What It Is For

Four hundred years of a building with a receiving end and no one at the other one. That was the whole of what she had found, and she said it out loud standing, because findings were lines.

“Receiving end below,” she said, with her hands flat on the unworn surface. “Forty boards under the middle. Cloth. Cord twice. A hand along. No person in it. This end makes and that end takes. Four hundred years and no one at this end until tonight.”

She walked the near edge with both hands the way she had walked it in the dark, and the wood ran under her palms without break, and she thought about her own table below the way she thought about everything below, which was as a keeping and not as a hunger.

That table had hands along it four hundred years. This one had none. That table took. This one made. Between the two of them the sentences went, one at a time, plain, reporting, through one woman standing at the top of a stair.

“Today I find out what it is for. Not the building. The table. A table with no lamp over it is a table for something that does not need light, and I need light, so whatever it is for, it was not made for me.”

She went along the near edge with both hands until she found what she had felt the night before without knowing, which was a thickness at the middle of the table, flat, the size of a board, lying where a board would lie.

## The Register

It was a register the way boards were boards, wood with lines in it, and the lines were cut deep enough to find with her fingers in the dark.

She stood at the near end of the table with both hands on it and did not read it, because reading was for light and her lamp stood past the last landing at an eyelash, and she had left it there the night before the way she had left it on the third landing a month back.

She felt along the edges of it with three fingers and a thumb, finding the corners, the thickness, the weight of it lying where a board would lie. It did not move under her hands. Boards under the middle of her own table moved when the hand went along. This one lay the way stone lies.

“Flat,” she said out loud. “Heavy. Cut deep. Lying where a board lies and not moving. That is the whole of what my hands know and it is enough to go back for light with.”

“I am going back for it,” she said out loud. “That is the errand and there is no other errand. One lamp. One register. One woman going between them in the dark.”

She went back past the top and down to where she had slept, with her left hand on the wall and her right arm hanging open at the elbow for six hundred and forty-two days, saying the way where it was used though there were no landings here, only dark and floor.

“Top behind,” she said into the dark. “Lamp ahead. That is the whole of the errand.”

And the lamp stood on the stone where she had said it would stay lit, and the flame stood an eyelash above the wick, and the light went two paces out and stopped there.

“Good,” she said to it. “Two paces is enough for one line. I am not asking you for the whole of it.”

She carried it back in both hands, the way she had carried it up the last flight, with her right arm hanging open at the elbow for six hundred and forty-two days and doing nothing, and the place behind her fourth rib pulling.

The dark past the top gave way under her feet the way it had given way going out, floor and nothing else, and she walked it with her lamp two paces in front of her, wick as much as oil, going down by the step. Twice the flame bent flat with her walking and stood back up between steps, lower every time, and twice she stopped and shielded it with her hand until it steadied.

## Him, on Reading

“I want it in your voice first,” he said.

She stood at the near end of the table with the lamp set on its surface, two paces of light on unworn wood, and the register under her hands, and she heard the want under the sentence the way a stone sits under a stair.

“It will be in my voice,” she said. “Everything on this table goes through me. That is the wire and I am it. Nothing here speaks the way a person speaks and nothing here is going to.”

“That is what I want,” he said. “Not its voice. Yours. I want the first of it in yours before it is in anything else.”

She stood at the table with the lamp two paces of light on unworn wood and the register under her hands, and she heard the want sitting under the sentence the way a stone sits under a stair, and it was the same want he had wanted for everything, the count and the binding and the question, hers first.

“You have had everything first in mine,” she said. “The Anchor. The count. The binding tomorrow or the next day. This is no different except that it is written and not derived.”

“It is different,” he said. “Derived is yours. Written is theirs. I want theirs in yours before it is anywhere else. That is the whole of the difference and it is not small.”

She put her left hand with three fingers and a thumb on the first line of the register and felt the cuts under her fingers, and she bent into the two paces of light, and she read it the way she read everything, which was out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice.

Her breath went four counts standing still. His half went on inside her where she could hear it, quiet the way he went quiet when she read, holding the hearing open.

## Read Standing

“The keeping keeps whoever stands under it,” she read. “Told or untold.”

She stood at the near end of the table in the making-place with her hand on the line and did not move.

“That is the first clause,” she said out loud, in her own voice, before it was a line anywhere else. “Four hundred years and the first of it is a keeping. Not a price. Not a sending. A keeping, and it keeps whoever stands under it, and telling has nothing to do with it.”

She stood at the table with her hand on the line for a count of a hundred, the way she stood with findings, letting it sit in her mouth the way figures sat, first in her own voice before it was anything else.

“Told or untold,” she said again, quieter, to hear whether it was true the second time. “Hundreds of rooms. Hundreds of tenders. Four hundred years. Kept whether told or not. That is a mercy and it is the first one.”

The sentences went on above her one at a time. His half went on inside her where she could hear it.

She moved her hand to the next line, and the cuts went on past her light, line under line, more than she could hold in two paces, and the flame sank as she watched, the way it had sunk for three nights, wick as much as oil.

She felt along the next cuts with her fingers the way she had felt the first, finding the starts of lines going on further than her light reached, four more she could feel and more past those she could not.

“Rest of it past my light,” she said. “One clause read standing in my own voice. The rest of it line for line when there is light to read by. The lamp is an eyelash and tonight it gives one line. That is the measure of tonight.”

She lifted her hand off the register and set the lamp back from the edge, the way she set things she was keeping, and she said the decision standing, because decisions were lines.

“Nine days the first clause waited,” she said. “One clause in my voice and the rest of it line for line. The lamp gives one line a night and tonight gave the first. That is the measure and I am keeping it.”

“I am keeping it with you,” he said. “One clause a night. Line for line. Standing. That is the arrangement and it holds.”

“I read it all standing, line for line, out loud, in my own voice, before it is a line anywhere. That is the fourth kept.”

She lifted the lamp off the table and turned toward the dark where she slept.

The rest of the reading would be standing, and it would be tomorrow.
