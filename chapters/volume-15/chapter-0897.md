# Every Part a Mercy

The lamp went to an eyelash in the night.

She woke past the last landing in dark with no usable light in it, and she knew before she was awake enough to answer it, the way she knew everything on this stair, which was with her body first. No light anywhere she could read by. The crook of her left arm nearly empty where she had caged it.

## What Out Means

She sat in the dark with her back against the table and her hands flat on the stone, and she did not move for a count of a hundred, because moving in dark with no light needed a mouth first.

“Dark for reading,” she said out loud, sitting, in the ordinary voice. “Three nights on wick as much as oil and tonight it gives nothing I can read by. The light is gone for my purposes and I am saying so.”

She sat in the dark with her back against the table and her hands flat on the stone, and the lamp an eyelash beside her giving nothing, and she thought about three paces going to two and two going to this, the way she had thought about days going for nineteen years, which was as a going and not as a grief.

“It gave me the top,” she said. “Three paces to see a top by. Two paces to read one clause by. An eyelash to finish by. That is the whole of what it owed me and it has paid it.”

Her breath went four counts sitting still. The sentences went on above her one at a time, plain, reporting, through her to him, and his half went on inside her where she could hear it.

“I tended it on the third landing since the twenty-third of the month beginning two thousand eight hundred and four,” she said. “I carried it past nine landings and a top. It gave me three paces and then two. It is done and I am not.”

She said the day's figures sitting in the dark with no lamp, on the twenty-eighth of the month beginning two thousand nine hundred and twenty-four, because the day needed them and figures were never light.

“Two hundred and nineteen days since the ninth stage. Six hundred and fifty days in that room. The cage out four hundred and seventeen. This arm open six hundred and fifty-seven days. One thousand three hundred and ninety-two gone at this morning.”

The bar stood six hundred and seven days against its wall, dated nowhere.

## Him, Without Light

“I want you to take what is true,” he said.

She sat in the dark at the near end of the table with her hands flat on the stone and heard him the way she heard everything now, undefended, and there was no room in her head that was his alone.

“The register needs light,” she said. “My light is an eyelash and there is no oil in this building and there has not been for nine years. Reading twice was for today and today has no light to read by.”

“Your hands find the lines,” he said. “The cuts are deep enough to find with your fingers in the dark. I want the second reading in your voice whether there is light or not.”

She put her hands on the table edge and found the near end the way hands find things in the dark, which was all at once.

“My hands read lines,” she said. “A second reading is for conditions and conditions hide in the small cuts. Fingers find lines. Eyes find conditions.”

She held up her left hand in the dark where no one could see it, three fingers and a thumb, the hand that could not write a false line, and she felt the cuts of the first line in memory the way she felt landings under her feet.

“Then read it with your fingers,” he said, “and let the conditions come to your mouth the way figures come. I want what is true out of you and not what is lit.”

“Fingers, then,” she said. “Mouth after. That is the order and it is the same order as everything.”

## Read a Second Time

She stood up at the near end of the table, because readings were standing, with nothing in either hand and no light anywhere, three fingers and a thumb on each hand finding the first line by the cuts.

She found it the way hands find things in the dark, which was all at once, the first cuts under her left hand where the lamp light would have lain. Her breath went four counts standing still. His half went on inside her where she could hear it, holding the hearing open for a reading with no light in it.

“The keeping keeps whoever stands under it,” she read, standing, in the ordinary voice, out of the dark, first in her own voice the way she had read it first by lamplight. “Told or untold.”

She moved her hands a breadth along, the way she had moved the lamp, and found the second line.

“Whoever is heard is answered in the voice that hears him.”

She read it standing, and the place behind her fourth rib pulled.

“Second reading and the same words,” she said out loud, standing. “First reading found five clauses. Second reading finds the same five. So it stands.”

She stood with her hand on the second line and pressed the small cuts the way eyes would press them, feeling for the turn, the hook, the narrowed cut where a keeping asks its price. The cuts ran even under her fingers, deep, unhurried, made by hands that never wore anything, and there was no turn in them.

She moved her hands again, and found the third, and the fourth, and the fifth, line for line in the dark, standing, out loud, in her own voice before they were lines anywhere else.

“No name taken into the keeping is sent anywhere. What is bound is kept where it stands. The offer stands open, and no answering is asked for it.”

## What the Second Reading Finds

She stood at the table in the dark with both hands on the register and waited for the conditions to come, the way she had waited for figures to come for nineteen years, because the first reading of a bargain always finds conditions and the second never does, and that had been true in the only other reading she had ever made, on the day she sent the refusal down.

Nothing came.

She read the five again with her fingers, slower, pressing the small cuts the way eyes would press them, looking for the turn where a mercy becomes a price, the way the ninth had become a refusal and the refusal a count.

“The keeping keeps,” she said, testing the first the way she tested figures, for what was behind it. “Who stands under it. Told or untold. No price behind the keeping. No asking behind the told.”

“The hearing answers,” she said, testing the second. “Whoever is heard. In the hearing voice. No price behind the answering.”

“No sending,” she said, testing the third and the fourth together, because they stood together. “No name moved. What is bound kept standing. No price behind the staying.”

“The offer stands open,” she said, testing the fifth. “No answering asked. No price behind the open.”

“The keeping keeps,” she said. “The hearing answers. No sending. Kept where it stands. Open and unasked.”

She lifted her hands off the register and stood in the dark with nothing in them.

“There are no conditions in it at all,” she said out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice. “That is the finding of the second reading and it is the reverse of every reading I have ever made. Every part of it is a mercy and not one of them is a lie. The Scribe writes terms and answers figures and never once lies, and this bargain is five mercies with nothing behind any of them.”

The sentences went on above her one at a time.

## The Reversal

“You are quiet,” she said.

“I am taking it,” he said. “Five mercies. No conditions. No price. No sending. I have spent nine years waiting for the turn where the keeping asks for something, and there is no turn, and I am taking that the way you take figures, which is whole.”

She sat in the dark with his taking next to her taking, two takings side by side the way the sentences sat next to his half. Nine years he had waited for the turn. A month she had climbed to find the price. Both of them arriving at no price on the same night, in the dark, with no light to see it by.

She sat down at the near end of the table, because standing was for saying and the saying was done, and the dark stood around her past no paces of light at all.

“Made in good faith,” she said, sitting, to the dark, to the table, to the man inside her head. “Every part of it real. Nothing in it a lie. Four hundred years of a keeping and it puts five mercies on a table and asks nothing. That is the reversal of this stair: I came up to find the price and there is no price.”

She sat in the dark with her hands flat on the stone and the five mercies in her mouth the way figures sat in her mouth, keeping and answering and no sending and kept standing and open unasked, and her breath went four counts sitting still, and the sentences went on above her one at a time.

She put her hand on the place behind her fourth rib and it did not do anything at all.

“No price on the table,” she said. “One price on the record, and that one is mine. Those are two different things and I am keeping them apart the way I keep landings apart from steps.”

The dark past the last landing waited past no light at all, and the register lay under her hands with five mercies in it, and she had nothing left to read by, and the reading was done, and the price she had climbed a month to find was nowhere on the table.
