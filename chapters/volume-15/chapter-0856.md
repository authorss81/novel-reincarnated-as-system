# The Uncarried Light

Ninety-four days since the ninth stage. Five hundred and twenty-five days in that room. The cage out two hundred and ninety-two days. Right arm open five hundred and thirty-two days and it is not going to close. One thousand two hundred and sixty-seven of the days gone at this morning and not one coming back.

There was a lamp in her left hand on the stone of the first landing above a room she had walked out of the day before, and there was a stair going up out of it that did not stop, and there was a woman going up it who had said out loud that she was never going to stand four feet off the near end of that table again.

## The Cold Out of the Stone

She went up at about the seventh hour of the twenty-third of the month beginning two thousand eight hundred and four, and she carried the lamp, and the light went four steps in front of her and stopped.

She said, out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice, on the second landing, “Figures first and the reason after.”

She said, “Ninety-four days since the ninth. Five hundred and twenty-five in that room. Two hundred and ninety-two since a lamp in a cage went out on a shelf I am never going to see again. Five hundred and thirty-two with this arm open and it is not going to close. One thousand two hundred and sixty-seven of the days gone and not one coming back.”

She said, “And here is the reason. I carried this lamp down nine landings and down eight hundred and eighty steps and up again yesterday, and I have carried it as far as the first landing above that room, and I am not going to carry it any further.”

The cold came up out of the stone through the soles of her shoes and into the open place at her elbow, and her breath went seven counts on the rise, and the lamp shook a little in her hand, and she stood still until the shaking stopped.

She said, “It is anger, and I am naming it before I argue with it, because I do that.”

She said, “I am angry at the lamp. Which is a thing with no fault in it, and I know that, and I am angry at it anyway, because it has shown me four feet of the world at a time for five days and I have called that seeing.”

She said, “And what I want is to see the next landing before I am standing on it. That is the whole of the want and there is nothing else under it.”

## Four Feet of World

She set the lamp down on the stone of the second landing and stepped four paces up past it and looked back.

The light went out from it along the floor and up the wall on the left and stopped, and past the edge of it there was nothing, and the stair above the nothing was the same stair she had been climbing.

She said, out loud, standing above it, in the ordinary voice, “That is four feet.”

She came back down and picked it up again and went on up to the third landing, and she counted the landings and not the steps, and she said so out loud, because a rule said twice is not a rule and a rule said once still has to be said where it is used.

She said, “Third. And that is a landing and not a stage, and there is nothing cut in my ribs about it.”

She put the lamp down on the stone of the third landing and stood beside it for about a count of a hundred and forty, with her left hand flat on the wall and her right arm open in front of her.

She said, “Here is the arithmetic and it is short. One lamp. It is one of three and the other two are four miles down a mountain. There has not been anything worth the name in this building for nine years and there is none to be had on this mountain, and that boy carries what there is on his own back and I have not asked him for any of it since the sixteenth of the month beginning two thousand seven hundred and seventy-four.”

She said, “And a lamp in a hand is light on about four feet of the world. I said that out loud on the twenty-second and it was true and it is still true.”

She said, “And here is the rest of it, which I have not said. Every landing I carry it past in my hand is a landing I have looked at. Every landing I leave it standing on is a landing I can find my way back to. I cannot have both with one of it, and I have had five days of pretending that I could.”

The lamp burned, and the light went six paces out from where it stood and stopped, and the stair above it went on up out of the light the way it had gone on before there was ever a lamp on it.

She said, “That is the whole of what a lamp is. It is not light. It is the end of my own reach. And I have been carrying the end of my own reach up a stair and calling it a plan.”

## What She Leaves Burning

She stood there for about a count of ninety with the lamp at her feet, and then she said the decision out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice, before she did anything else about it.

She said, “I am leaving it here. Burning. On the stone of the third landing. And I am going up past it in the dark with nothing in either hand, and I am not bringing it back down.”

She said, “And I want the price said before it is paid, because a price said afterwards is a thing you have made up. The price is that everything behind me from this landing down is unmarked ground the moment I go past the reach of this light. If the lamp goes out there is no light in this building above the fourth landing of that tower, and there is no person in this building but me, and no person in the four miles under it who knows where I am.”

She said, “And I am paying it on purpose and I know what it is.”

She said, “And I am frightened. And that is not the same as the anger, and I have worked out that it is not the same, and it is worse, because the anger is at a thing and the fright is at nothing, and nothing is bigger than any thing I have ever stood in front of.”

She sat down on the stone beside the lamp and put her back against the wall and stayed there until about the eighth hour, with her right arm open across her knees and her breath going nine counts standing still.

She said, “And here is the last part, and then I am going. I have had a lamp in my hand for five days and I have gone exactly as far with it as I could see, which is four feet at a time, and four feet at a time is a way of staying where you are while you are moving. I am going to find out what is up there, and a thing I carry in my hand is not going to find it out for me.”

She stood up.

She said, “Lamp on the third landing. Burning. Mine. Left on purpose. That is the record and there is nothing else in it.”

## Up in the Dark

She went up past the reach of the light with her left hand on the stone and her right arm open in front of her, and the dark closed over her head the way water closes over a stone.

The stair wound and there was no rail on it, and the stone was cold under her hand, and after about a count of forty she could not see the light behind her any more, and after about a count of a hundred and forty she put her hand out for a wall on the right and there was nothing there, and she took her hand back and went on.

She said, out loud, in the dark, in the ordinary voice, “There is no wall on the right.”

She said, “And I had my hand out for it without deciding anything, and I have been doing that since the thirteenth, and I am saying it now so that it is said.”

She went on up, and the cold got into her arm until the arm shook, and she stopped and stood still with her hand flat on the left-hand wall and waited until the shaking stopped, and then she went on again.

She said, “Fourth.”

And the word went out into the dark and did not come back, and there was no lamp under it and no light on it, and it was a landing because she said it was, and that had to be enough, because there was nothing else up there that was going to say it for her.

There was a lamp burning on the stone of the third landing of a building with nine landings above a room that was not a room, and a woman going up past the fourth one in the dark with nothing in her hands, and a room four miles down a mountain with three boards face up on it that she had left on purpose.

And the lamp burned behind her with no person beside it, and the light went six paces out from where it stood and stopped, and everything past the stopping place was unmarked, and she had marked it so herself, and that was the cost, and it was paid before anything was found.
