# A Name on the Stair

Ninety-seven days since the ninth stage. Five hundred and twenty-eight days in that room. The cage out two hundred and ninety-five days. Right arm open five hundred and thirty-five days and it is not going to close. One thousand two hundred and seventy of the days gone at this morning and not one coming back.

There was a lamp burning on the stone of the third landing above a room that was not a room, and there was a woman sitting beside it with her back against the wall, and there were boots on stone a long way below her, going up, where no boots had any business to be on the twenty-sixth of any month.

## Boots Below

She heard them at about the sixth hour of the twenty-sixth of the month beginning two thousand eight hundred and four, sitting on the stone of the third landing with the lamp beside her, and she stayed still for about a count of forty and counted the steps between one and the next.

She said, out loud, sitting, in the ordinary voice, “That is a person.”

She said, “And I want that said first, because there has been no person in this building but me for three days, and there has been no person on any stair in that tower but one boy for nineteen years, and those boots are not his.”

She stood up and picked up the lamp and went down the stair toward the sound, with her left hand on the stone and the light going four steps in front of her and stopping, and the boots went on coming up, steady, four to a minute, the way a person climbs who has climbed the same stair a great many times.

She said, out loud, on the second landing, “Who is on that stair.”

And the boots stopped.

And a voice came up out of the dark below her, an old voice, rough with the cold, that she had never heard in her life, and it said, “Boards.”

She stood on the second landing with the lamp in her hand and did not move for about a count of ninety.

She said, “It is grief, and it came up under me before anything was said, and I am putting it down in front of the figures where I can see it.”

She said, “I have been in this building five hundred and twenty-eight days and there has been a man on a stair under me the whole of it, and I have never once spoken to a person in this building who was not that boy, and I did not know until this morning that there was anybody to speak to.”

She said, “And what I want is to hear him say one true thing before I decide what he is. That is the want and there is nothing under it but that.”

## Through the Gap

She went down past the first landing to the gap in the near wall of that room, and she did not go through it, and she set the lamp on the stone of the landing and left it there.

She said, out loud, standing at the gap, in the ordinary voice, “I am not coming into that room. I said I was never going to stand in it again and I meant it. But there is a man under it on a stair and I am going to speak to him through this wall, and that is not the same as going back.”

The boots came on up the second stair, and a man came out of the place in the wall behind the shelf where the cage lamp stood, into the dark of that room, with about forty boards under one arm in the dark, and he put them down on the middle of that table without looking at anything, the way a person puts down a thing he has put down a great many times.

She said, through the gap, “Hold there.”

And he held.

She said, “I have watched a man do that eight times in nineteen years and I have never said one word to him, and I am saying one now. I am above this room on a stair and I am not coming into it, and I want to ask you something, and you do not have to answer.”

He said, out of the dark, “Ask it.”

She stood at the gap with her hands on the stone on either side of it and her right arm open in front of her, and she said it out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice, and it was the first time in nineteen years that she had said anything of the kind to anybody.

She said, “What is your name.”

There was a silence in that room that went on for about a count of forty, and the boards lay on the table in the dark, and the cage lamp stood out on the shelf, and the bar stood against the wall on the left where it had stood four hundred and eighty-five days.

He said, “Han Liang.”

She said it back, to fix it, the way she fixed every figure she had ever had.

She said, “Han Liang.”

She said, “And I have never asked any person for a name in nineteen years of record, and I have asked you for yours through a wall in the dark, and I want that said with it, because a thing said afterwards is a thing you have made up.”

## What He Looks Like

She stood at the gap a while before the asking, with the lamp behind her putting a little light through onto the floor, and she looked at him, because there had been a man under her for nineteen years and she had never once looked at him.

He was old. Grey through, bent a little under nothing, with hands that had carried forty boards a month for four hundred years and showed it, and a coat cut short for the mountain, and no lamp anywhere about him, and boots that made no more sound standing than they made climbing.

She said, out loud, standing at the gap, in the ordinary voice, “I am looking at you, and I want that said, because I have watched a man put boards down eight times without looking at anything, and I never looked back, and looking one way is not the same as looking at.”

He stood in the dark beside the table and let himself be looked at, the way a person stands who has been looked past for four hundred years and is good at that too.

She said, “That is the whole of the looking and I am not making anything of it. You are a man and you are old and your hands show the carrying. And I know your name and the number of your steps, and that is everything I know, and I am saying how little it is out loud so that it stays little.”

## The Arithmetic in Her Mouth

She stood at the gap for about a count of a hundred and forty and did not go into that room, and the man stood in the dark beside a table twenty-two paces long and did not come to the gap, and the lamp burned on the landing behind her and put a little light through onto the floor.

She said, “And here is the arithmetic, and I am saying it in my mouth instead of on a board, because there is no board here and I am not going back for one.”

She said, “Nine hundred and four steps going up that stair, and the step at the turn does not change. That is the stair you walk and I have never walked it and I never will.”

She said, “And nine hundred and four coming back down. Twice nine hundred and four is one thousand eight hundred and eight. That is every carrying, up and down, and you have walked it with forty boards under one arm.”

She said, “And I have said figures out loud into that table every morning since I was nine years old and every one of them went somewhere, and you are the somewhere they went, and I have never once said a figure to you.”

He said, out of the dark, “You say them to the table.”

She said, “I do.”

He said, “Then they go where they go. That is the whole of it.”

She said, “How long.”

He said, “Four hundred years.”

She stood at the gap with that in her, and the lamp burned behind her, and the boards lay on the table in the dark where he had put them without looking.

She said, “Four hundred years of nine hundred and four steps, and I have been standing four feet off the near end of that table for nineteen of them, and I never asked.”

She said, “That is not a figure. That is a person. And I did not know there was one until this morning, and I am not going to be all right about that for some time, and I am not going to try.”

## What She Carries Up

She picked up the lamp off the stone and went back up the stair to the third landing, and she did not count the steps, and she counted the landings, and she said so out loud, because a rule used has to be said where it is used.

She said, “Third. And that is a landing and not a stage.”

She set the lamp down on the stone where it had stood and sat beside it with her back against the wall, and she put her hand flat on the floor of that landing for about a count of forty.

She said, out loud, sitting, in the ordinary voice, “His name is Han Liang, and he carries forty boards up nine hundred and four steps, and he has done it four hundred years, and he puts them down without looking at anything.”

She said, “And here is the thing I am carrying up out of that, and it is not a figure and it is not a plan. I have spent nineteen years saying figures into a table as though there were no hands at the far end of it, and this morning there were hands at the far end of it, and they belonged to a man, and he told me his name through a wall.”

She said, “And I am glad. And that is the first glad in this building since the thirteenth, and I am saying it out loud because it would be dishonest not to, and it sits next to the grief and neither of them moves the other.”

There was a lamp burning on the stone of the third landing, and a woman sitting beside it with a name in her mouth that she had asked for and been given, and a man going back down nine hundred and four steps under her with nothing under his arm, four to a minute, ten minutes, the way he had gone down ten thousand times before.

And she had asked, and he had answered, and the asking could not be unasked, and that was the evening, and it was not what she had gone up that stair for, and it was the only thing that had happened on it that day.
