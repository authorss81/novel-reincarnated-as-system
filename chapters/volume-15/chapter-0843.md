# The Second Opening

Fifty-four days since the ninth stage. Four hundred and eighty-five days in that room. The cage out two hundred and fifty-two days. Right arm open four hundred and ninety-two days and it is not going to close. One thousand two hundred and twenty-seven days lighter at this morning and not one going back.

There was no person in that room at the first hour, and no person in it at any hour, and the lamp on the wood a foot in from the near end of that table was not lit, because a boy of sixteen carries it up at the seventh hour and he had not been up yet.

## Two Openings In One Wall

She said out loud at the first hour, standing, in the ordinary voice, in the dark, four feet off the near end of that table:

“I am going to look at the wall.”

She said, “Not the table. Not the boards. The wall at the near side of this room, which is the wall I have faced every morning of my life since I was seven years old and which I have not looked at once in nineteen years.”

She went to it on her feet. She went the length of the near side of that table and put her left hand flat on the stone, which is how she has walked beside a table for nineteen years, and the stone was cold and the same as it has been every morning.

She said, out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice:

“There are two openings in this wall.”

She said, “The first one is the doorway the tower stair comes in at. I have looked at that doorway four times in nineteen years and three of those were when I was coming in or going out, and the fourth was on the ninth of the month beginning two thousand seven hundred and forty-four when I stood in the middle of this room on purpose and looked at it, and there was nothing in it and I wrote that down.”

She said, “And I am standing three feet from the second one and I have never once put a hand into it.”

## What Is In The Gap

The second opening was not a doorway. It was a gap between stone and stone with no lintel on it and no frame, and it was about the breadth of her and it went up.

She put her left hand into it and the stone on either side of it was colder than the stone she had been standing against, which is the first thing she said out loud about it.

She said, “The stone is colder. It is not the cold that comes up out of a floor in the morning. It is a cold that has been in a place with no lamp in it and no person in it for a long time, and I can feel the difference between those two colds in my hand the way I can feel the difference between a board and a stone.”

She said, “And there is air coming out of it. That is what told me it went anywhere. You cannot have air standing still in a gap that does not go anywhere. That is not a mystery, it is a draught, and I have felt a draught through a shut door twice in my life and both times it meant there was another room on the other side of it.”

She got her whole forearm into it up to the elbow and she could not get it past her shoulder.

She said, out loud, “It is narrower than it looks. It looks like a doorway from about six feet back and it is not one, and I stood six feet back from it looking at it for about a count of a hundred and forty this morning and I did not know that until I put my arm in.”

She said, “And I can feel the other edge of it with my fingertips when I have my arm in as far as it will go. Stone, and then nothing, and then the stair going up on the other side of the nothing.”

She stood with her hand in the gap and counted nothing.

She said, “And I am going to say the thing that is true out loud before I put a foot in it, because if I go in there and find four feet of stone and a wall, I want it on this page that I said it first.”

She said, “There is no reason in nineteen years of record for this to exist.”

## The Reason There Was None

She said, “And the reason there is no reason is not that I have missed it. I have counted everything in this building. I counted a stair in daylight and got eight hundred and eighty in two runs of four hundred and forty and I put the figure in the record. I have counted every morning of my own life for nineteen years. I have a number for the days since my arm opened and a number for the days since I have been lighter and a number for the days I have been standing in this room.”

She said, “And there is no number in this record for anything above this floor. There is not one. And that is not a gap in my record. That is the record being complete.”

She said, “I did not write it down because there was nothing to write down. I have stood at the top of eight flights and eight hundred and eighty steps for four hundred and eighty-five days and there has never once been a reason for me to put a number on anything over my own head, and I have said that out loud more than once in this room as though it were a strength.”

She said, “It was not a strength. It was a woman who did not ask.”

She said, “And here is what I thought the top of a tower was for, and I have had a great deal of time to think about it in a room at the top of one.”

She said, “I thought it was for the wind. I have been four miles up a mountain for nineteen years and there is a thing about the air on this mountain that I have never been able to account for, and I decided a long time ago that it was because there was nothing above me to stop it.”

She said, “I thought it was for a cistern, or a room where the rain goes, or a place where somebody has to go up on a bad afternoon and clear a hole out of it. Every one of those would have been a place with a use in it, and a use has a shape, and I would have found the shape in about a count of a hundred and forty.”

She said, “And none of those would have been the thing. And the thing is that I did not need the stair, so I never once wondered what was at the top of it, and that is not a thing that happens to people who are paying attention.”

## Not Counting This One

She said, “And here is the decision and I am saying it standing, at the first hour, in the ordinary voice, at the gap, before I go through it.”

She said, “I am not going to count this stair.”

She stood with her hand in the cold air coming out of it and did not move.

She said, “I have counted every stair I have ever walked. I counted a stair I have walked four times in nineteen years and I got eight hundred and eighty, and I am not going to go back and take that figure as wrong today. Eight hundred and eighty is in the record and it is right.”

She said, “And I am not going to count this one, and I am going to say why, because I have never in my life not counted anything without a reason and I am not starting at a gap in a wall.”

She said, “A landing is a thing I can be wrong about. I have been wrong about plenty. I was wrong about a question yesterday and I have said so out loud. A landing is a floor with an end to it and you can be in a room and not know it is a room, and I have been wrong about a room.”

She said, “A stair is not like that. If I put a number on this stair then the number is the thing I have brought up it. And a number is a thing you can hold, and I have been holding numbers instead of things for nineteen years.”

She said, “And I am going up it for the last time.”

She stood there and did not move.

She said, “That is not a figure and it is not arithmetic and I am not going to argue with it in a wall. I do not know how high this goes. I am not going to know when I come back, because I am not coming back down, and I am not going to arrive at the top of it holding four hundred and something instead of the place I am going to.”

## And Then She Went In

She said, “There are eighteen days to the first of this month and I want that said, because that is the morning this room does the one thing it does and I am going up a stair today instead of standing under it.”

She put her left hand on the left side of the gap and went in sideways.

She said, out loud, once she was on the other side of the stone, in the ordinary voice:

“There is a stair.”

She said, “It goes up. And there is no rail on it, and there is no light in it, and the step at the bottom of it is worn.”

She stood on it in the dark for about a count of forty.

She said, “The step is worn. Not broken, worn, in the middle, the way a step gets worn when a great many people have gone up and down it and not one of them has ever gone down it in a hurry and not one of them has ever gone up it with a lamp.”

She said, “And I am not going to put a hand on that stone and read how worn it is. That is the whole of my discipline on this stair and it starts now and it is going to be the only one I have got for a long time.”

She did not turn round.

And the last thing she said on the floor of that room in four hundred and eighty-five days was said to a dead lamp in a cage at the far end of a table twenty-two paces long, and it was this.

She said, “I am going up.”

There were three pieces of wood lying face up about a foot in from the near end of that table and a fourth about a foot further along, and a bar against the wall on the left that had been there four hundred and forty-two days, and a bundle under the far end with two turns of cord in it and not a knot, and a strip of pale wood face up under the table at the near end of the leg, and a lamp out in a cage on the shelf that nobody had lit and nobody was going to.

And the room was dark, and she was in it, and she had been in it every morning since she was nine years old, and this was the first morning she had walked out of it on purpose and gone up instead.
