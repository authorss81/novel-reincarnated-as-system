# What He Wants

He was talking when she woke.

Not gone quiet. She had learned the difference in two months and she could hear it the way she heard a room with one person in it against a room with none. His half went on inside her where she could hear it, low, unhurried, turning something over the way she turned figures, and she lay still past the last landing with her hands flat on the stone and listened the way she had listened for nine years, which was without deciding in advance.

She knew the sound of him turning things. Nine years of it. Figures turned. Want turned. Her anger turned and turned until it came out cold. This turning was slower than any of those, the way the traffic is slow past a heavy line, and it did not come out anywhere.

She lay still with her hands flat on the stone and kept her eyes closed, because opening them would show her dark she already knew, lamp nearly out and table unworn and register cut deep, and the listening was better shut.

## The Man at the Edge

“I am here,” he said. “The bargain is on the table and you are standing at it every day and I am standing it with you. That is the arrangement and it holds.”

She sat up at the near end of the table in the making-place, on the seventh of the month beginning two thousand nine hundred and fifty-four, with the lamp on the stone beside her and the oil nearly out of it, and the register under her hands, five mercies read twice.

“Two hundred and twenty-eight days since the ninth stage,” she said out loud, sitting, because mornings were lines even when they had no light. “Six hundred and fifty-nine days in that room. The cage out four hundred and twenty-six. This arm open six hundred and sixty-six days. One thousand four hundred and one gone at this morning.”

The bar stood six hundred and sixteen days against its wall, dated nowhere.

She sat with the figures the way she sat with mornings, fully, without deciding in advance. Two hundred and twenty-eight days since the ninth and the ninth shut. Six hundred and fifty-nine days in a room she had not seen in over a month. Four hundred and twenty-six days of a cage lamp out on a shelf. Six hundred and sixty-six days of an open arm. One thousand four hundred and one gone and not one coming back. Every one of them derived and every one of them hers.

“You are turning something,” she said.

“I am turning it,” he said. “Five mercies. No conditions. The ceiling pays and the many pay and none told. Best in nine hundred years. I turn it the way you turn figures and it comes out the same every time.”

She put her hand on the place behind her fourth rib without deciding to, and it did not do anything at all.

“What does it come out to.”

“That it is right,” he said. “That is what it comes out to. Right and worse both. I have nothing past that and I am not inventing any.”

She sat with his turning the way she sat with everything he said, which was fully, and the turning went on inside her where she could hear it, audible, unwithdrawn, at the edge of something she could not see past, the way the register ran past her light.

“You turn it the way I turn figures,” she said. “Over and over and the same every time. What are you turning toward.”

“Toward the answering,” he said. “Five mercies on the table. One bound on the record. One question you have never asked. I turn all three and they come out the same every time.”

She sat with his turning the way she sat with the traffic, fully, without deciding in advance. Five mercies read twice. One bound kept where he stands, unknown to him. One question sitting behind her teeth the way the count had sat for four days, a weight and not a number. Three holdings and the morning holding all of them without moving any of them.

## Nineteen Years Unasked

She stood up at the near end of the table, because what she had to say needed standing, with nothing in either hand, three fingers and a thumb on each, the open arm hanging for six hundred and sixty-six days.

“Nineteen years of record,” she said out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice. “A voice in my head since I was ten. Counted with me. Asked at the sixth year. Staying at the ninth. Given a pen. Said out loud three times. Heard now, undefended, unwithdrawn from. And in nineteen years I have never once asked it what it wants.”

She stood with the history the way she stood with figures, all of it derived, none of it invented. Ten years old and a voice. Twelve and a price with no day. Sixteen and a refusal at the ninth hour. Nineteen and a stair that did not stop. Every year of it his and hers both, and not one question in any of them about what he wanted.

The sentences went on above her one at a time, plain, reporting, through her to him.

“That is the method and it never required anything from me,” she said. “It asked and I answered or refused. It wanted and I guessed. Nine years of guessing at a man inside my own head. Today the guessing stops.”

She stood in the dark past the last landing with the lamp nearly out beside her and the table with no lamp over it in front of her, no wear in it, and a place at the far side with nothing worn at all.

Eight flights and a stair below her a strip of pale wood lay face up under a table in the same place, and a boy with a figure in a column carried oil with no idea of it yet, and in her head a voice that was a person went on, and was heard now.

## What She Has Never Said

“What do you want,” she said.

She said it standing, in the ordinary voice, first in her own voice before it was a line anywhere, the way she had said the count and the binding and the reading and the question before it.

Then she sat down, because standing was for saying and the saying was done, and she waited.

He did not answer.

She sat at the near end of the table with her hands flat on the stone and listened the way she had listened for nine years, and his half was there the way it was there everywhere now, audible, unwithdrawn, at the edge of something, and what she heard was not absence.

The turning had stopped. That was the first thing she heard. Nine years of turning things over and now stillness, the way the traffic goes still past a line going in. He stood at the edge of the decision the way she had stood at the edge of landings, with his mouth closed and his half open, and the stillness was not empty.

It was a man declining.

## His and Hers to Keep

She sat with the silence the way she sat with stone, fully, without deciding in advance, and she did not fill it for him.

That was the hardest thing she had done on this stair, harder than climbing on wick, harder than binding, harder than hearing the argument and not answering it. Nineteen years of saying things out loud and the one silence she kept was his.

“I am not withdrawing,” she said out loud, sitting, into the silence, because some things needed saying even inside a silence. “I am not letting go. Small does not make right and this is not that turn. I am sitting here with you not answering and I am not going anywhere.”

She sat with the not withdrawing the way she sat with stone. Nine years of staying through taking and untelling, through refusal and hearing, through small shares and hard true things, and the staying had never once been the same as the letting go, and it was not now.

“I am not filling it for you,” she said. “Nineteen years of saying things out loud and the silence stays yours. I am not putting words in it the way I put figures in columns. It sits next to me unfilled and I am keeping it unfilled.”

He did not answer that either, and the not answering was the same shape, a man at the edge of a decision and not a man who has gone, turning five mercies and a ceiling and a thousand years the way she turned figures, and coming out the same every time.

“The offer stands,” she said. “Five mercies on the table. One bound on the record. One question asked. No answer. That is the whole of where we are and I am keeping it.”

She put her hands flat on the stone and kept sitting, and the silence sat next to her the way the nothing had sat next to the fourteen makings, and she had gone up this stair to find out who was speaking, and she had found a table, and a bargain, and a man, and the man had everything she had ever wanted him to have except an answer.

Her breath went four counts sitting still. The lamp stood nearly out on the stone beside her. The register lay on the table with five mercies in it. Eight flights and a stair below, a boy carried oil with black hands and no idea of any record. In her head a voice that was a person went still at the edge of a decision and did not go anywhere.

She did not fill it.

He did not leave it.

The offer stood.
