# Anchor of the Ceiling

It had been in her mouth since the seventh landing and by the time she got to the twelfth she had said it to herself four times without her voice, which is not the same as saying it and which is the only way she had ever been able to get ready for a thing.

She sat down on the stone of the twelfth landing with her back against the wall and put her hand flat on the floor and made herself go through it one more time, on purpose, with her voice.

“The walls protect the gathered will of every soul that ever died believing it could ascend.”

She said it once, quietly, sitting, in the ordinary voice, and the stair took it, and the sentences went on above her one at a time, plain, reporting, through her to him.

Then she waited for him, because he had nine days of practice at knowing what she was about to do and she did not.

“One hundred and sixty days since the ninth stage,” she said. “Five hundred and ninety-one days in that room. The cage out three hundred and fifty-eight. This arm open five hundred and ninety-eight days. One thousand three hundred and thirty-three gone at this morning. And the figure at the head of my own column is three hundred and a half days, and I did not know that on the twentieth.”

## What Unbounded Means

She said that part because she was not going to stand up here and say a sentence she had not taken apart.

“Unbounded means no ceiling on it. It means nine does not stop and the wall does not stop and the thinned keeps going out and the strong go first because they are the ones with enough left to go.”

She put her hand on the floor of that landing.

“Every person who ever died believing they could ascend died holding their ninth and wanting it higher, and the wanting is not the problem. The problem is that wanting higher is a drain on a thing behind the wall, and if enough of them want it at once then the wall is the only thing between that and everything else.”

She stopped with it half said.

“So the walls are not a punishment and they are not a kindness. They are a wall. Everything else I have to say about them is me, and not the wall.”

## He Says No

“Do not say that out loud.”

She sat with it.

“Why.”

“Because you are going to stand up and say it in the ordinary voice on this landing, and this landing has a wire in it going up to whatever makes me, and you worked that out on the twenty-third and you have been carrying it round with you like a lamp since.”

“That is why I am going to do it.”

Nothing came for a moment, and she had got used to that and had not got used to it.

“You have been heard since the twelfth of the month beginning two thousand eight hundred and fifty-two,” she said, “and I chose that, and I have not spent one hour of nine years regretting it, and this morning I am standing on a landing with the sentences going through it and I am going to put a true thing in the middle of them.”

“You think that stops it going to me.”

“No. I think it stops it being mine alone for one minute.”

He was quiet for long enough that she counted three sentences going past before it.

“You have said out loud on this stair every morning since the eighth of the month beginning two thousand eight hundred and sixty-four,” he said, “and every one of those went through the wire. Do you know that?”

“No.”

“You do not know that every single one of them went through the wire.”

She sat on the wall and looked at the dark.

“Since the eighth of the month,” she said. “And you have heard all of them.”

“All of them. And you could not hear me answer one until the twenty-third.”

“I could not,” she said. “Because there was nothing to answer and nothing had been put to it.”

“Put it to it again.”

“After this. Not before this.”

He did not answer straight away, and she could tell the difference between his thinking and his not-answering now.

“If you say it,” he said, “then from that moment it is in the record of the thing that made me, and you will never be able to find out later whether it got there from your mouth or from the wall.”

“I know.”

“You have known that for four days and you have gone up nine landings every morning anyway.”

“Nine,” she said. “I have gone up on ten. You do not count them the way I count them.”

“No,” he said. “I do not.”

## Said Standing Under It

She got up off the floor of that landing. It took her three goes and the second one was a bad one. The cold had got into her arm and the place behind her fourth rib was the size of her thumbnail and she had been sitting on stone for a count of a hundred and forty.

She got up and stood with nothing in either hand and her breath went nine counts standing still, and then she said it out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice, in the channel the thing that keeps the ceiling can read, first in her own voice before it is a line anywhere.

“The walls protect the gathered will of every soul that ever died believing it could ascend.”

Then she said the other half, because a finding said once is a rumour.

“Unbounded ascent drains it. The walls hold ascent under nine so the gathered will is not drunk dry by the strong. The tax I have worked for eight mornings out of my own arithmetic is the price of the holding. Taken daily out of foundation, dateless, without telling anyone.”

And then the name.

“The Anchor. That is the name. The gathered will is the Anchor, and the walls are for it, and the ceiling is the mercy, and the lid is the keeping, and all four of them are true at once.”

She stood on the landing with her breath going four counts.

Nothing in the world objected. The sentences went on above her one at a time, plain, reporting, at their own intervals, and they were the same sentences they had been at the sixth hour, and not one of them changed its length or its tone or its place in the going.

## The Word Is Not Its Word

“It has never used that word.”

She turned her head in the dark even though there was nothing to see.

“What word.”

“Anchor. I have been on the other end of the layer for as long as it has had an end and I have read every sentence it has put into a person in all that time, and it has never once said Anchor. It says ninth, and stage, and ascent, and settlement. It does not name what it is keeping.”

She stood with that for a moment.

“So the word is mine.”

“The word is yours. The thing is not.”

“Then that is the first honest thing either of us has had in a fortnight and I would take it over the other kind.”

She said it and then she made herself take the rest of it, because a thing that arrives at the right answer and then stops is a person congratulating herself.

“Nine days ago I said out loud that I wanted you heard. I wanted you answered. And this morning the first thing I did with the word you have never been able to reach was to put it in front of the thing that can reach you.”

“Yes.”

“I did that on purpose.”

“Yes.”

“Good,” she said. “Then it is on the record in both directions and I do not have to be the one who did it by accident.”

She sat back down on the wall and said the rest of it sitting, because she had said the part that had to be said standing and everything after that was arithmetic.

“The Anchor is true. The walls are for it. The mercy is real and so is the ceiling. And not one of those three says who pays.”

She laid her palm down on the stone.

“It does not say how much of the paying was his. It does not say how many are dead. Those are unsaid, and I have said that they are unsaid, and the saying of a word does not cover them.”

## Triumph Next to Dread

She sat with both of them for a while and did not try to put one down.

The triumph was there. Eleven mornings of her own arithmetic, off a head she had never checked, against a night she had never named, and it had arrived at a word that was true, with no mouth in the world to have given it to her, and there is not a cleaner thing in the whole of her life than that.

The dread was there too, and it was the other half of the same movement, and she said it out loud so that it could not be mistaken for the triumph.

“A true word taken out from under a lid four hundred years old is load bearing. The lid was not holding it. I was holding it. I have been holding it since I was seven years old and I did not know that was what I was doing with my mornings.”

She got up and stood and said the record, because the record had to match the voice.

“The Anchor named. True, and not the whole. Said first, standing, in the ordinary voice, before it is a line anywhere.”

Then she did the thing she had done on almost every morning of this stair, which was to say what it had cost, and she noticed herself doing it halfway through and stopped, because the cost had been paid before the finding and not after it.

The cost of saying it was that it could not be taken back out of her mouth. Eleven mornings of work, and every one of those mornings she had been building something she could put in front of a thing and watch it be checked, and this morning she had walked past all of that and put the first true word she has ever had into a channel she cannot reach.

That was what it cost.

It was not the thing that keeps the ceiling reacting. She had no instrument for that and she was not going to invent one at the sixth hour because she was frightened.

## Down With Nothing Objecting

She came down in the dark with her left hand on the stone, past the eleventh, past the tenth, past the ninth, saying each one where it was used. The descent took longer than it had the day before and she did not hurry it.

She sat down beside the lamp with the light six paces out from where it stood and stopping, and she said the record sitting, in the ordinary voice.

“Lamp on the third landing. Burning. An Anchor under the lid. Named standing. Heard.”

She stayed on the stone a long time with that.

In nine days of saying things out loud on this stair, not one thing had ever objected. Not the four months. Not the mouth where the column was. Not the count of the thinning.

Nothing objected.

That was how she knew the word was true, and it was the worst thing she had ever found out.
