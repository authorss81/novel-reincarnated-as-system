# Another Table

Sixty days since the ninth stage. Four hundred and ninety-one days in that room. The cage out two hundred and fifty-eight days. Right arm open four hundred and ninety-eight days and it is not going to close. One thousand two hundred and thirty-three days lighter at this morning and not one going back.

There was no person in that room and no person in the room above it and no person on any stair in that tower, and a lamp was standing on the stone of a landing that had never had a lamp on it before.

## Four Feet Of Stone Going Up

She went up.

She said out loud, on the first step, standing in the ordinary voice in the dark, “I am not counting this,” and she said it into a gap in a wall at about the seventh hour and forty minutes of the nineteenth of the month beginning two thousand seven hundred and seventy-four, and then she did not say it again for a long time.

The stair went up steeply and it wound, so that she could not see up it, and there was no rail, and the stone was dry and cold under her left hand all the way.

She said, out loud, after a while, “It winds. I did not think it would wind. I have climbed four staircases in my life that I could count and every one of them went straight, and a stair that winds is a stair that does not want you to see what is at the top of it.”

She said, “And I am not reading anything into that. I am noting it and I am not reading anything into it, because I have a rule and the rule is that I do not give a building a face.”

Her breath went four counts on the level places and seven on the rise, the way it has gone since she was nine years old, and it went worse than that a long way up, and she did not stop and did not say anything about it.

She carried the lamp in her left hand with three fingers and a thumb on the bowl of it and her right arm open in front of her in the dark the whole way, and about halfway up she found out what a person does when she cannot hold the wall and the light at the same time.

She said, out loud, “She holds the wall. And she carries the light out in front of her where she cannot see it, and she goes up a stair on the side of it.”

## The First Landing

The landing was about the size of the fourth landing of that tower and the ceiling on it was low enough that she put her head down coming off the last step.

She put the lamp on the stone of it, and the light went out from it flat along the floor and up the far wall and stopped, and it stopped a great deal further than it stopped in a long room, because stone gives the light back and a wall two hands off the floor gives it back better than twenty-two paces does.

She said, out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice, “It goes about six paces here. It went a hand’s width in there.”

She said, “And I had it in my mind that a lamp in a room is a different thing from a lamp on a landing, and I have just found out that it is not the same at all.”

She said, “A lamp standing at the near end of a long room goes out along the wood and stops at about a hand’s width, and a lamp standing in the middle of the same table goes a great deal further, because it has twenty feet of it to work with. Out here it has stone on three sides at about the height of a person, and the light comes back on itself.”

She said, “That is not a small difference. That is the difference between knowing where one step is and knowing where six of them are.”

She said, “And there is a doorway on the far side of this landing and the light reaches the edge of it and not inside it.”

She stood there for about a count of a hundred and forty.

She said, “Four hundred and ninety-one days in a room where light went a hand’s width along a table, and one landing of a stair, and a lamp on a stone floor, and the light goes six paces and comes back off the walls.”

## And Then A Table

She carried the lamp to the doorway and stood in it.

She said, out loud, after about a count of forty, in the ordinary voice:

“There is a table in there.”

And then she stood in that doorway for a long time and did not go in, and when she said the next thing she said it in the ordinary voice and she did not give a reason for it.

She said, “It is twenty-two paces long.”

She said, “I paced it. I have paced that table about two hundred days running and I know what twenty-two paces of that table sounds like from a doorway, and it is the same sound. Four feet off the floor. A leg at every two paces. And the flag under it that moves at about the nineteenth.”

She said, “And there is a lamp in a cage on a shelf at the far end of it.”

She stood there.

She said, “That lamp is out. And I have never seen it before this morning and I am looking at it from about six paces and I can see that it is out, and I have been in a room four hundred and ninety-one days with a lamp in a cage on a shelf at the far end of a table that has been out two hundred and fifty-eight days, and I know exactly what that looks like from here.”

## Standing In The Doorway

She put her left hand flat on the doorframe and stood in it and did not go in.

She said, out loud, “I am not going to go into it.”

She stood there for about a count of ninety.

She said, “And I am not going to say why, and I have thought about whether to say why and I have decided not to, and that is a decision and not an evasion.”

She said, “There is a table twenty-two paces long in that room and it is the same table as mine and I have known what that table is since I was nine years old, and I am going to come back to it with two lamps and stand at the far end of it, and I am not going in tonight.”

She looked at the near end of that table from the doorway for about a count of ninety, in the six paces of light off the floor of the landing.

She said, “There is nothing on the near end of it. A foot in from this end of mine there are three pieces of wood face up and a fourth a foot further along, and I put them there myself, one of them on the first of the month beginning two thousand six hundred and eighty-four and one of them on the first of this month, and there is nothing on that table.”

She said, “There is nothing on that table and there has not been one thing on that table and I did not put one thing there and it is not mine and it never was.”

She said, out loud, standing in that doorway, “And I want to say what I thought I was going to find, because I had six days to get ready for it and I did not get ready for this.”

She said, “I thought I was going to find a roof. I thought I was going to find the under side of a cistern, or a stone with weather on it, or a room with a hole in the roof of it where the rain comes in and where somebody has to go up and clear it.”

She said, “I thought I was going to find a use, and I thought I was going to be disappointed about it, and I had about a count of a hundred and forty ready for being disappointed.”

She said, “And what is in that room is a table twenty-two paces long.”

She said, “And I am not disappointed. I want that said before anything else about it, because I would have expected to be, and I am not, and I do not know yet what a person is supposed to do with that.”

## What Is Above A Room

She said, “And here is the whole of what I have found out in about a count of a hundred and forty, and I am going to say it standing, in the ordinary voice, because I have had six days to be frightened of finding out less than this and I have found out more.”

She said, “There is more building above this room.”

She said, “That is the sentence. That is all of it. I have stood at the top of eight hundred and eighty steps for four hundred and ninety-one days and I have said out loud in that room, more than once, that there was nothing above this line. And I was not being poetic and I was not being careful. I was wrong, and I was wrong in the plainest possible way, which is that I never went and looked.”

She said, “And the thing above my room is a table twenty-two paces long with a lamp in a cage on a shelf at the far end of it, out, and nothing on the near end of it, and I am standing in the doorway of it and I am not going in.”

She carried the lamp back to the middle of the landing and put it down on the stone where the light went six paces and stopped.

She said, out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice, at about the eighth hour and thirty minutes, “I am going to come back up this stair with both of my hands and I am going to find out what is at the far end of that table, and I am not going to count one step of the way.”

There was a landing with a lamp standing on it in a tower that had never had a lamp on it, and a doorway in the far wall of that landing, and a table in the room behind the doorway, and not a person in either of them, and no lamp lit anywhere above the woman who had lived at the bottom of it for four hundred and ninety-one days.
