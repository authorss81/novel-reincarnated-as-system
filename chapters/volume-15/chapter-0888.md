# The Top of the Stair

The cold brought her awake before the sixth hour.

She had slept on the fifteenth landing sitting up with her back against the wall and the lamp caged in the crook of her left arm, the way she had slept every night for a month, and the cold came up through the floor into her before she was awake enough to be angry at it, and by the time she was awake she was already sitting, which was the arrangement.

## What Morning Is Up Here

Morning past the ninth was not light. There was no opening anywhere above her room that let the outside in, and the dark sat the same at every hour, and the only morning she had was the one she said.

She said it sitting, with her hand on the stone, on the twenty-ninth of the month beginning two thousand eight hundred and ninety-four, because mornings were lines and she said them even when there was no one to hear but stone.

“One hundred and ninety days since the ninth stage. Six hundred and twenty-one days in that room. The cage out three hundred and eighty-eight. This arm open six hundred and twenty-eight days. One thousand three hundred and sixty-three gone at this morning.”

The bar stood five hundred and seventy-eight days against its wall, dated nowhere, and she said that too, last, the way she always said it last.

Then she picked up the lamp and looked at the flame, and the flame was lower than a grain of rice, and the light went three paces out now and stopped there.

“Three,” she said out loud. “It was six on the third and four on the fourteenth and three here. That is not a figure. That is a going.”

She put her hand on the place behind her fourth rib the way she had been doing since the twentieth, and she took her hand off the way she took her hand off things that were decided.

“Same,” she said. “Not worse. Same is bearable on this stair and I am marking it.”

## Him, on the Last Flight

“I want to be there when you see it,” he said.

She stood on the fifteenth landing with the lamp in her hand and the dark above her going up past where three paces of light could reach, and she heard the want in it before she heard the words, the way she had learned to hear him in a month.

“You are there for all of it,” she said. “You have been in my head since I was ten and you hear everything now. There is no seeing it that you miss.”

“That is hearing,” he said. “I am not talking about hearing. I want the moment your feet stop and your mouth opens and you do not have a landing for it. I want that one with you.”

She put her back against the wall and felt the place behind her fourth rib pull.

“You will have it,” she said. “Everything I see on this stair goes through me to you. That is the wire and not the voice and you know it better than I do.”

“I know it,” he said. “I am still asking.”

“Then yes,” she said. “Be there. You are there. That is the whole of the answer and it is not nothing.”

## The Climb Past Counting

She went up past the fifteenth with the lamp held in both hands in front of her, because the flame was too low to lean anymore and both hands around it kept the air off, and her right arm hung open at the elbow and did nothing, the way it had done nothing for six hundred and twenty-eight days.

Her breath went seven counts on the rise. The place behind her fourth rib pulled with every step. The sentences went on above her one at a time, plain, reporting, through her to him, and his half went on inside her where she could hear it, and the two goings sat next to each other the way they had sat for a month, and neither moved the other.

The flight was longer than any flight below it. She knew that the way she knew everything on this stair, which was with her feet, and she did not count it, because landings were the count and the landing was ahead.

Twice she stopped and set the lamp on a tread and rested with her forehead to the wall. Twice the flame sank until she thought it had gone, and twice it stood back up when she lifted the lamp, lower every time.

The third time she stopped she did not set the lamp down. She stood with it in both hands and her forehead to the stone and listened to him counting under his breath the way she counted landings, which was without telling anyone, and she had never once heard him count anything in nine years.

“You are counting,” she said, with her face to the wall.

“I am counting your steps,” he said. “You do not count them and someone on this stair should. I am not telling you the number. That is the arrangement I made without asking.”

She stood with her forehead against the stone and felt the counting go on the way she felt the sentences go through her, as a going and not as a figure.

“Count,” she said. “Someone should. It is not me on this flight.”

“Wick and oil both nearly gone,” she said to herself on the second stop, and then out loud, because out loud was the instrument. “Wick mostly. Oil mostly gone. Going up anyway.”

“You are nearly there,” he said. “I feel the stair changing under your feet.”

“I feel it too,” she said, with her face to the stone. “It is getting narrower and the air is getting thinner and there is less stone over my head. Do not tell me what that means. Let me get there.”

“I am not telling you,” he said. “I am climbing it with you.”

## What She Finds

The landing above the fifteenth was not a landing.

She got to the top of the flight with the lamp in both hands and the flame standing the height of an eyelash above the wick, and there was no wall ahead past three paces of light, and no stair going up, and no rail, and nothing to count.

She stood on the last tread for a count of forty with both hands around the lamp and did not step forward, because stepping forward off a count she had kept for a month needed a mouth first.

“There is a top,” she said out loud, standing, in the ordinary voice, into dark that went past her light in every direction. “Nine landings above my room and the stair keeps going past every one of them and here it stops. There is a top and I am standing under it.”

She stepped up onto the stone, and the stone took her weight, and the place behind her fourth rib held, and the sentences went on above her one at a time.

“There,” he said. “Your feet stopped and your mouth opened and you have no landing for it. I was here for it the way I asked to be here.”

“You were,” she said. “Every step of the flight and the stopping at the end of it. That asking is answered.”

## The Room She Cannot See

She held the lamp out at the full length of her left arm and turned herself in a slow circle on the stone, the way she had turned in the doorway of the cells on the twenty-sixth of the month beginning two thousand seven hundred and seventy-four, and the light went three paces out and stopped there in every direction, and showed her floor, and wall behind her, and nothing else.

It was a place. That was the whole of what three paces of light could tell her, and it was enough for tonight, because she had climbed on wick as much as on oil for three nights and the flame was an eyelash and the room past her light was not going anywhere.

She went to the edge of the light in four directions with her hand out, the way she had mapped the cells, and found floor under her feet every way, and wall only behind her where the stair came up, and nothing else anywhere. No door stood in the dark. There was nothing to open and nothing to knock on, and she said so out loud, because a doorless place was a finding.

“No door,” she said. “Four ways and floor every way and wall only where I came from. Tomorrow I walk it. Tonight I sit at the edge of my own light.”

She sat down with her back to the stair she had come up, so nothing could come at her from below without her knowing, and she set the lamp on the stone in front of her, and she said the landing where it was used, because that was the habit and the habit was hers.

“Last.”

The flame sank and stood and sank again, and the light went three paces out and stopped there, and past the light the dark went on in every direction without wall or tread or rail.

“Here is what I have,” she said out loud, sitting, with her hands flat on stone no lamp had touched before tonight. “A top. A place past the last landing. Three paces of it seen and the rest of it dark. A lamp at the end of its going. A woman at the end of her count with nothing counted in her hands.”

She was quiet for a long moment, and the sentences went on above her, and his half went on inside her, and the counting under his breath had stopped, the way counting stops when what it counts arrives.

She was quiet for a long moment, and the sentences went on above her, and his half went on inside her.

“Tomorrow I go in,” she said. “Tonight I sit at the door of it the way I sat at the door of everything for nineteen years, except this time there is no door.”

She leaned her head back against the stair wall and felt the cold come through into her skull, and it steadied her, the way it always steadied her.

“Stay with me,” she said. “That is not arithmetic. That is the want and I am saying it out loud.”

“I am staying,” he said. “That is not arithmetic either. That is the answer and I am giving it standing.”
