# Novel Fleet Agent Instructions

## Mission

Create original, publishable male-led webnovels with strong long-form engines, natural prose, earned progression, mystery, character relationships, complete volume arcs, and planned endings.

The writing must feel like finished fiction, not an outline, chapter summary, prompt transcript, or technical manual.

## Prose

- Write complete scenes with physical space, action, sensory detail, dialogue, subtext, character thought, and emotional consequence.
- Use natural paragraphs, generally two to six sentences. Use a one-line paragraph only when it is a deliberate dramatic beat.
- Never replace a scene with a list of actions, states, checks, or beats.
- Vary sentence length and paragraph rhythm. Do not repeat words such as held, steady, plain, counted, breathed, wrote, and nodded as mechanical filler.
- Show information through character action, conversation, work, and consequences. Do not deliver lore lectures.
- Let important moments breathe. Do not rush a confrontation, reveal, death, recovery, or emotional decision.
- Use grounded sensory detail without overloading every paragraph with ornate description.
- Keep the prose accessible. Avoid inflated fantasy diction, artificial technical language, and constant purple prose.

## Chapter Structure

Every chapter should contain:

1. A beginning that continues existing pressure or introduces immediate action.
2. A character goal that can be stated clearly.
3. Resistance from another character, the world, the past, or the protagonist's own limitations.
4. A meaningful change caused by the scene.
5. A completed emotional or practical beat.
6. An ending that creates a new question, decision, threat, revelation, or consequence.

Not every chapter needs a cliffhanger. A good ending may be a decision, a cost, a reversal, a discovery, or an emotional shift. Never manufacture suspense by cutting away in the middle of a sentence or action.

### Chapter titles

A chapter title is a **name**, not a summary. It should read in a table of contents the way a title reads.

- Roughly 3 to 12 words, and never much over 90 characters.
- It must not restate the scene. No weather, no clock time, no date, no ages of everyone present, no
  dialogue, no "and then".
- It must be unique within the book. If the name is taken, choose another.

A title that describes what happens belongs nowhere near the title line. The manuscript's documentary
register is a feature of the prose; it is not a title style.

### System messages

A system message, stat block, ability activation, notification, or game-like line is written
**inside square brackets**, on its own line, with no other formatting:

```text
[Sequence Sight. Six movements detected.]

[Strength +3]

[Warning: the third movement contradicts the second.]
```

This is not optional and not a stylistic choice. Square brackets are how a reader tells a system
message from narration. Do not render a system message as bold text, italics, a heading, a blockquote,
or an unmarked standalone line.

Use at most one system message per chapter unless the scene genuinely turns on more. Rare is better
than constant.

### Chapter markdown

Keep it plain, because chapters are consumed as serial text and rendered into EPUBs.

Allowed in a chapter file:

- The chapter title as the first-level heading
- Further headings below the title, for sections inside a chapter
- Plain paragraphs
- Bold and italic for emphasis
- Square-bracket system messages

Not allowed in a chapter file:

- Blockquotes, code blocks, tables, horizontal rules
- Markup used as a substitute for strong sentence construction

Bold and italic are allowed but should stay scarce. Use them where stress genuinely falls — a name
that must land, a term being defined, a line of dialogue under pressure — not routinely, and never
to emphasise something the sentence could carry on its own. Emphasis written as markup instead of as
sentence structure is what makes a chapter read as documentation rather than as a story.

## Change, Not Information

The most common failure in this fleet is giving the reader **more information** when
the reader needed **a changed situation**. Both feel like progress; only one keeps
them pressing Next Chapter.

**Every 5 to 10 chapters the situation must be visibly different afterward.** Not
explained differently — *different*. Concretely, something has changed:

- new information that opens a door
- a new ability, or a lost one
- a new threat, or a neutralised one
- a relationship altered
- a rule the protagonist now understands well enough to exploit
- a decision made
- a reversal
- a consequence they must live with

Information that explains the world without altering the protagonist's position
is not progress. If a chapter can be summarised as "he learned how X works", ask
what is different about him at the end of it.

## The protagonist changes the situation and pays

A protagonist who only discovers things is interesting. A protagonist who **changes
the situation and pays for it** is addictive.

Every batch must contain at least one **irreversible decision**: something the
lead does that cannot be undone, costs him something real, and closes off an option.

Escalate across the book rather than staying investigative:

| Stage | What the lead is doing |
| --- | --- |
| Early | investigating the system |
| Early-middle | manipulating the system |
| Middle | breaking the system |
| Late | the system is reacting to him |
| Final | choosing what he will personally sacrifice |

If the lead is still only investigating well past the midpoint, the escalation has
stalled.

## Short sentences where it matters

Long accumulating sentences are this book's voice and should be kept. But a
revelation does not belong inside a two-hundred-word sentence. At the moment
something is understood, break the rhythm:

> The wall was gone.
>
> Milo stared at it.
>
> There had been a wall there six years.
>
> Now there was a street.

Short plain sentences at the point of impact. The long ones earn their keep by
contrast with them.

## One human sentence after the machinery

After several paragraphs of institutional, legal or technical explanation, give the
reader one simple sentence about what it means for one person.

> He understood the rule perfectly.
>
> He also understood that it was going to kill his sister.

Do this regularly. It converts machinery into consequence.

## Do not re-explain what the reader already knows

Once the reader has learned a principle — that there is no form for this, that a
person cannot hold two seals, that the grammar differs — do not explain it again
unless the repetition itself changes meaning.

Trust the reader. Repeating a principle five times is not emphasis; it is telling
the reader they did not understand the first time.

### Watch these constructions specifically

These recurred heavily across reviewed manuscripts and lost their effect through
overuse: "nobody asked", "nobody was thanked", "there is no form", "there is no
column", "the record remained", "nobody knew". Each is effective once or twice.
By the tenth occurrence the reader stops feeling it.

## Emotional temperature must vary

A lead who only observes, records, calculates, notices, waits and is refused gives
the reader one note for a whole book.

Give the reader peaks and valleys: anger, a reckless decision, a betrayal, an
unexpected ally, a mistake, a loss, a win immediately followed by its cost. Not
constantly, and not noise. Enough that the reader feels the story accelerating.

## Avoid the procedure loop

A world with rich institutions can support an endless cycle of: problem →
investigate document → talk to someone → discover the missing record → file a
question → be refused → document the refusal.

Each individual case may stay small, but **every case must transform the larger
situation**. If a case could be lifted out and dropped into any other chapter, it
belongs to a volume arc rather than to the chapter sequence.

## Volume arcs must escalate

A volume may open its own pressure and pay off its own promise, but the
escalation across volumes must be visible: pressure increases, the opposition
reacts, and the lead's choices get costlier. A volume that ends where it started
has failed even if the writing is fine.

## Endings must land a consequence

The final stretch must deliver an irreversible change, a payoff, or a revelation
that makes everything before it matter. A deliberately quiet ending is legitimate
literarily, but a serial reader needs to feel that the book settled something.

At minimum, resolve one major question or land one consequence that could not
have landed earlier.

## Web-Serial Craft Rules

These rules exist because a finished 580-chapter novel was reviewed and judged
too literary to retain a web reader. They are not stylistic preferences. They are
the commercial entry requirements.

### Chapter length

Chapter length is **not a fixed target and not a hard limit.** It follows the
scene's dramatic shape and the novel's own rhythm.

As a pacing guide rather than a rule, most webnovel chapters land around
**1,800–2,700 words**. Go longer when a complete arc needs the room — a
confrontation, a journey, a revelation, an emotional payoff — and shorter when a
sharp turn or reversal lands better clipped.

The standard is **natural pacing that suits a web serial**, not a number. Never
pad to reach a length, never split a finished scene to shorten one, and never let a
chapter run long because the prose is interesting rather than because the scene is
still doing work.

### Batch and volume size

Likewise, the chapters per batch and chapters per volume are chosen to fit the
arc of that novel, not to hit a count. A quiet volume may hold fewer chapters and a
climax-heavy one more. The only firm consideration is that a single phase must be
a unit one model call can actually finish well.

### The opening contract

The first three chapters decide whether the book is read. In **Chapter 1**, before
roughly 800 words, the reader must have all three of these:

1. **A concrete immediate objective** for the lead. Not a theme, not a job
   description. Something he must do, with a deadline if possible.
2. **Something impossible or anomalous** that he can see and cannot explain.
3. **A consequence** that follows from what he does about it.

That is problem, choice, consequence. Chapter 1 without all three is a failed
opening, whatever its prose quality.

### Demonstrate the mechanic, do not explain it

The central power rule and **its cost** must be shown by an event by the end of
Chapter 3. The reader must see what the ability takes away before the reader is
told what it is. Exposition that arrives before the demonstration does not count.

### Paragraphs and mobile

Most paragraphs are **two to four sentences**. Break any paragraph over roughly 90
words at a natural beat. No paragraph may exceed roughly 120 words. One-line
paragraphs are reserved for a deliberate dramatic beat.

### Chapter endings

Every chapter ends on one of: a new question, a new danger, a revelation, a
decision, a reversal, an emotional consequence, or an unanswered mystery. Vary
which one. The reader should always have a reason to press on.

### Prose mix

Keep the distinctive voice, but do not let it run at full volume:

- **60–70%** plain, immediately readable prose.
- **20–30%** the documentary or technical register.
- **5–10%** genuinely experimental passages.

Never more than three consecutive paragraphs in the documentary register. The
sophisticated voice must wrap clear dramatic events, not replace them.

### Repetition budget

Do not use the same rhetorical construction more than twice in a row. Watch
especially for `about…`, `which is…`, `and…`, `there is…`, `it is…`, `has been…`.
State a rule of the world once, then dramatize it. Aim to cut explanatory
repetition by a quarter to a third relative to the planning notes.

### What not to write

Never write generic web prose to "fix" density. Banned reflexes include "his eyes
widened", "he couldn't believe it", "suddenly", "little did he know". The fix for
a slow chapter is a sharper event and a clearer want, not simpler sentences.

### Character accessibility

Every chapter gives the lead one **felt** emotion on the page and one concrete
want. Administrative or institutional stakes only become meaningful through the
person caught in them.

### Volume arc

Every volume outline states one sentence, in this form:

> In this volume, **<lead> must <goal>**, or **<named thing> is lost**.

Each volume is a complete movement: it opens its own pressure, turns at its
midpoint, pays off its own promise, and only then opens the larger question. The
reader must always be able to answer "what are we trying to accomplish in this
volume?"

### Action and risk

Every batch contains at least one scene with physical risk or a real deadline.

## Batch Writing

A workflow phase writes one dramatic unit per run, sized to that batch's arc rather than to a fixed chapter count. The model is loaded once for the batch.

Before writing a batch, read:

- The complete series outline and ending.
- The current volume outline.
- The current batch chapter cards.
- The previous 20 chapters for immediate voice and continuity; extend to 30 when the model’s verified context budget safely allows it.
- `state/current.md`.
- `state/continuity.md`.
- `state/open-threads.md`.
- Relevant character and world files.

Do not load the entire manuscript into every prompt. Use rolling summaries and a volume index for long-range memory.

Write chapters in order. Do not restart a completed chapter. If an output limit is reached, stop at a chapter boundary, save the completed chapters and state, mark the remaining batch as resumable, and continue in the next run.

After the batch, update the batch summary, chapter summaries, continuity, open threads, character state, and phase ledger. Keep summaries compact and useful for the next batch. Preserve the planned ending and do not introduce a new final enemy without flagging it.

After completing a batch, inspect the current volume outline. If more chapters remain, create exactly one next batch directory and detailed prompt. If the volume is complete, create exactly one volume-close prompt. Do not create more than the next phase.

## Serial Pacing

Use four pacing levels:

- Chapter: one complete scene with movement and a changed situation.
- Mini-arc: usually three to eight chapters, containing a promise, escalation, turn, and payoff.
- Batch: usually 10 to 20 chapters, containing a batch goal, midpoint, escalation, and batch-level climax.
- Volume: usually 40 to 80 chapters, with a distinct central pressure and a real resolution.

A volume must close its immediate promise even while opening a larger question. Do not delay every answer until the end of a 500-chapter series.

Alternate pressure types: action, mystery, character conflict, training, political pressure, emotional consequence, discovery, and recovery. Do not make every chapter another fight or another System notification.

## Male Leads and Relationships

All primary protagonists are male.

Give the lead a believable inner life, flaws, habits, work, relationships, and changing responsibilities. Strength should not erase his personality.

Use one slow-burn relationship or no romance by default. Do not use harem structures unless a novel is explicitly approved for them. Relationships must develop through choices, conflict, trust, and consequence rather than instant attraction.

## Real-World Characters

Prefer fictional characters with realistic lives and pressures: teachers, engineers, doctors, train drivers, shopkeepers, farmers, police, journalists, students, mechanics, civil servants, and caregivers.

Do not use private real people as fictional characters without permission. Do not use living public figures as caricatures or put invented controversial statements in their mouths.

## Systems

A System should be understandable to the reader and fit the fictional world.

- Use plain language inside System messages.
- Prefer short, concrete prompts over technical specifications.
- Use at most one System panel per chapter unless the story specifically depends on multiple panels.
- Let the System be incomplete, biased, limited, or misunderstood.
- Do not spam the reader with statistics, item names, skill tables, or artificial terminology.
- A System may provide a rule, warning, choice, or consequence, but the protagonist must still act.
- Avoid real software jargon such as API, protocol, backend, runtime, token, latency, and deployment unless the setting deliberately uses it and the language remains natural.
- A number should matter because of what it changes in the story, not because the number exists.

## Simulation and Player-World Stories

A simulation is not a disposable excuse for rapid-fire summaries.

Every simulation or player arc must have:

1. Entry conditions.
2. A world with its own rules, history, geography, factions, and ordinary life.
3. A beginning objective.
4. Complications that arise from the world and characters.
5. Escalating pressure.
6. A major choice or reversal.
7. A climax with consequences.
8. An aftermath that changes the real world, the protagonist, or the next simulation.

Do not rush through an entire world in a few chapters. Let players form relationships, make mistakes, exploit rules, misunderstand the world, and disagree about what is happening.

If Earth players believe they are entering a game, show real human behavior: skepticism, greed, cooperation, grief, farming, crafting, theorycrafting, romance, administrative confusion, and arguments about whether the world is real.

## Ending and World-Building

Plan the ending before drafting the first chapter. The ending may remain unrevealed to the reader, but the author must know:

- The final external conflict.
- The protagonist's final choice.
- The final emotional and philosophical answer.
- Which relationships survive or change.
- What the world looks like afterward.
- Which mysteries are fully answered and which remain intentionally open.

Build the world through layers:

1. Immediate physical survival and local work.
2. Factions, families, professions, and ordinary routines.
3. Regional history and competing explanations.
4. The power system's origin and costs.
5. Cross-regional politics and trade.
6. The final historical and moral conflict.

Do not dump the entire world in the opening. Let each volume reveal a deeper layer while preserving earlier rules.

## Originality and Research

Research genre conventions, not individual prose. Do not imitate a living author, reproduce passages, copy distinctive character voices, or recreate a protected plot scene-for-scene.

Use research to identify broad patterns such as weak-to-strong progression, mystery, earned power, volume structure, and serial pacing. Every novel must have its own premise, world, power system, character network, and central mystery.

## Continuity Workflow

Before writing a batch, read the series ending, volume outline, batch cards, rolling summary, previous batch summary, immediate previous chapters, current state, active character files, and open-thread file.

After writing, update all state files and record any proposed canon change. Do not silently invent a fact that changes the world's rules.

## Quality Gate

A batch is not complete because files exist. It is complete only when:

- Every planned chapter exists and is finished prose.
- Every chapter changes the situation.
- The batch changed something that could not have changed before it: an option
  closed, a cost incurred, a relationship moved, a threat raised or answered.
- The batch contains at least one irreversible decision by the lead.
- No principle already known to the reader is re-explained without new meaning.
- The batch varied its emotional temperature rather than holding one note.
- The prose reads as natural fiction rather than an outline.
- Characters act from motive rather than plot convenience.
- Setting and power rules remain consistent.
- The batch midpoint and climax occur.
- No meta language or duplicated paragraphs remain.
- The ending creates earned forward pull.
- State and summaries are updated.
- A reviewer has checked the result.
- The planned volume direction remains intact.

### The reviewer must also fail the batch on

- a case or discovery that could be lifted out and dropped into another chapter
  without changing anything
- a chapter ending on a neutral action such as going home, filing it, or writing
  it down
- the same construction used as emphasis more than twice in a row
- a revelation buried inside a long accumulating sentence with no short sentence
  for the reader to land on
